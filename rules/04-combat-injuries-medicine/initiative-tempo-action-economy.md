# 4.3 — Initiative, Tempo & Action Economy

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines how combat timing works once active sequencing matters. It governs initiative, tempo, action commitments, reaction windows, interruptions, simultaneous actions, and how extreme speed differences affect combat without creating unstable turn multiplication.

## 4.3.1 Combat Uses Dynamic Tempo Rather Than Fixed Rounds

Combat should not assume that every participant receives one equal turn inside a fixed-length round.

Instead, combat is resolved as a sequence of meaningful action windows.

An action window exists whenever one or more characters have a meaningful opportunity to act, respond, interrupt, reposition, or continue an existing commitment.

The engine may still use hidden timing values for consistency, but the player-facing experience should feel continuous rather than round-based.

## 4.3.2 Initiative Determines the Opening Advantage, Not Permanent Turn Order

Initiative matters most when:
- combat begins;
- a new threat appears;
- multiple actors attempt incompatible actions at nearly the same time;
- someone tries to seize an opening;
- an interrupted exchange must be reordered.

Winning initiative does not grant permanent first place for the entire battle.

After the opening, tempo can shift because of:
- movement;
- speed;
- surprise;
- successful pressure;
- failed attacks;
- recovery time;
- jutsu preparation;
- injury;
- terrain;
- hesitation;
- interruptions;
- tactical choices.

## 4.3.3 Tempo Represents Control of the Exchange

Tempo is not a single permanent stat.

It describes who currently has the initiative in the practical sense: who is forcing others to respond.

A character with tempo may:
- pressure an opponent;
- chain actions;
- dictate distance;
- force defensive responses;
- constrain available choices;
- create or exploit openings.

A character without tempo may still act, but often through:
- defense;
- retreat;
- counterattack;
- interruption;
- prediction;
- accepting risk to seize initiative back.

Tempo can change repeatedly during a fight.

## 4.3.4 Speed Affects Opportunity Frequency and Window Size

Higher speed should matter through:
- acting earlier;
- completing actions sooner;
- repositioning before slower responses complete;
- reacting inside narrower windows;
- chaining compatible actions;
- recovering from commitments sooner;
- interrupting slower actions;
- denying opponents time to perform complex actions;
- increasing the difficulty of tracking and responding to movement.

Speed should not normally translate directly into a fixed number of extra full turns.

## 4.3.5 Action Commitment Matters

Actions have commitment.

A light jab, long sprint, complex jutsu, heavy weapon swing, medical procedure, and sustained defensive technique do not consume equivalent time or attention.

Actions may differ in:
- initiation time;
- execution time;
- recovery time;
- required concentration;
- mobility during execution;
- interruptibility;
- ability to transition into another action.

This allows fast simple actions to naturally occur more often than slow complex ones without inventing arbitrary action points.

## 4.3.6 The Engine Tracks Action Phases When Necessary

Important actions may be treated as having up to three phases:

### Initiation
The action begins: drawing a weapon, forming hand seals, shifting stance, beginning a sprint, preparing a throw.

### Execution
The meaningful effect occurs: the strike lands, projectile is released, jutsu manifests, movement crosses danger space.

### Recovery
The actor returns to a state where they can freely transition into another action.

Not every routine action needs all phases explicitly tracked. The model is used when timing, interruption, or counters matter.

## 4.3.7 Actions May Overlap

Combatants do not politely wait for one another to finish.

Examples:
- one shinobi forms seals while another closes distance;
- two fighters strike simultaneously;
- someone throws a kunai while retreating;
- a medic continues stabilization while allies defend them;
- a defender begins a dodge before an attack fully resolves.

Overlapping actions are resolved through Ruleset 2 when uncertainty or opposition exists.

## 4.3.8 Reactions Are Opportunities, Not Free Extra Turns

A reaction is a limited opportunity to respond to a developing event.

Examples:
- dodge;
- block;
- parry;
- intercept;
- counterattack;
- substitution;
- protect an ally;
- abandon an ongoing action to evade;
- trigger a prepared defense.

Reaction capability depends on:
- awareness;
- perception;
- speed;
- readiness;
- current commitment;
- distance;
- technique requirements;
- injury and fatigue;
- available defensive options.

A reaction does not automatically grant a complete additional turn.

## 4.3.9 Reaction Windows Vary

Different threats create different reaction windows.

A distant visible fireball may provide substantial warning.

A concealed senbon launched from close range may provide almost none.

A slower character can still react to a faster character when:
- they anticipated the action;
- they began responding earlier;
- the faster action was telegraphed;
- distance creates additional time;
- the defender uses a simpler response;
- terrain limits the attacker;
- the defender does not need to physically match every movement.

This is critical for avoiding the false rule that higher raw speed automatically defeats all defense.

## 4.3.10 Prediction Can Compensate for Speed

Perception, experience, intelligence, sensory information, pattern recognition, and familiarity can allow a character to begin acting before an opponent's movement is fully committed.

This does not make the slower character physically faster.

It allows them to spend less time deciding and position a response in advance.

Examples include:
- reading body language;
- recognizing hand seals;
- predicting a preferred attack route;
- understanding an opponent's habits;
- using dōjutsu or sensory abilities;
- setting traps or controlling terrain.

## 4.3.11 Defensive Responses Have Opportunity Costs

A defender forced to dodge repeatedly may lose:
- position;
- offensive pressure;
- time;
- access to prepared techniques;
- line of sight;
- footing;
- protection of an ally.

Even successful defense can therefore create cumulative tactical pressure.

This allows a superior attacker to dominate without every attack needing to land.

## 4.3.12 Chaining Actions Requires Compatibility

Characters may transition quickly between compatible actions.

Examples:
- feint into strike;
- throw a kunai while advancing;
- land from a jump into a sprint;
- complete hand seals into immediate jutsu release.

Incompatible transitions require more time or recovery.

Examples:
- changing direction after fully committing to a heavy strike;
- abandoning a complex jutsu halfway through;
- switching from carrying a wounded ally to a two-handed weapon;
- recovering after a failed high-force attack.

Mastery and training may reduce transition costs.

## 4.3.13 Multi-Action Sequences Are Resolved as Pressure, Not Guaranteed Extra Turns

A much faster character may perform several short linked actions before a slower opponent completes one long action.

However, this should emerge from:
- action duration;
- speed;
- recovery;
- distance;
- reaction windows;
- commitment.

It should not come from a rule such as “twice the speed equals two turns.”

## 4.3.14 Interruptions Occur When Timing and Position Permit

An action can be interrupted if another combatant can meaningfully affect it before execution completes.

Possible interruptions include:
- striking someone during hand seals;
- forcing a dodge before a jutsu releases;
- knocking away a weapon during a wind-up;
- breaking concentration;
- blocking a movement path;
- attacking a medic during treatment.

Interruptions are not automatic. Ruleset 2 resolves whether the attempt succeeds when uncertain.

## 4.3.15 Interrupted Actions May Have Partial Consequences

An interrupted action may:
- fail completely;
- be delayed;
- lose resources;
- misfire;
- execute at reduced effectiveness;
- force the actor to transition into defense;
- create an opening.

The specific result depends on the action and how far it progressed before interruption.

## 4.3.16 Simultaneous Actions Are Allowed

If two actions reach meaningful execution at effectively the same time, both may resolve.

Possible outcomes include:
- both attacks land;
- both miss;
- one attack disrupts the other;
- attacks collide;
- one combatant accepts being hit to complete their own attack.

The system should not force an arbitrary ordering when simultaneity is the more plausible result.

## 4.3.17 Holding an Action Means Waiting for a Trigger

A character may intentionally delay commitment while watching for a condition.

Examples:
- attack when the enemy crosses a doorway;
- intercept if someone approaches the medic;
- release a trap when the target enters range;
- counter when a specific jutsu begins.

Holding an action trades immediate initiative for a potentially stronger response window.

The trigger must be something the character can reasonably perceive.

## 4.3.18 Delaying Is Different From Preparing

A character can simply choose not to act immediately and preserve flexibility.

Preparation is more specific and may involve:
- charging chakra;
- forming partial seals;
- readying a weapon;
- taking aim;
- establishing a stance;
- setting a trigger condition.

Preparation may improve execution but can reveal intent or create vulnerability.

## 4.3.19 Movement Is Integrated With Action Economy

Movement should not exist as a separate universal “move action.”

Characters may:
- attack while advancing;
- retreat while throwing;
- reposition while forming simple seals;
- sprint at the cost of other options;
- perform complex actions that restrict movement.

The important question is not whether movement consumes a discrete action slot, but how much movement is compatible with the current action and speed.

## 4.3.20 Extreme Speed Differences Create Dominance Through Compression

When one fighter is dramatically faster, the slower fighter may experience:
- shorter reaction windows;
- fewer safe openings;
- more forced defense;
- difficulty tracking movement;
- greater positional disadvantage;
- interrupted actions;
- inability to complete slow techniques;
- repeated loss of tempo.

At sufficient disparity, the slower character may be effectively overwhelmed.

This should emerge from interaction of speed, perception, anticipation, distance, and action complexity rather than an arbitrary “speed threshold = no actions.”

## 4.3.21 Perception Limits Usable Speed

Moving faster than an opponent matters only if the actor can still perceive, decide, control their body, and execute accurately at that pace.

Very high movement speed without sufficient perception, control, or mastery may create:
- reduced accuracy;
- poor turning ability;
- overshooting;
- vulnerability to traps;
- slower decision-making relative to motion;
- greater risk from terrain.

This prevents raw speed from becoming universally dominant.

## 4.3.22 Injury and Fatigue Affect Tempo

Injuries and fatigue can affect:
- action initiation;
- execution speed;
- reaction windows;
- recovery time;
- movement;
- concentration;
- chaining ability.

These effects should arise from the actual injury or condition rather than a generic combat penalty whenever possible.

## 4.3.23 Complex Jutsu Create Timing Risk

Hand seals, chakra shaping, aiming, charging, maintaining concentration, or environmental setup may create longer commitments.

A powerful technique may therefore be tactically difficult to use under pressure even when the user has enough chakra.

Higher mastery may reduce:
- preparation time;
- seal count;
- transition time;
- vulnerability during execution.

This creates a natural combat value for technique mastery beyond raw power.

## 4.3.24 Clones, Summons, Puppets, and Multiple Bodies

Additional bodies do not automatically grant unlimited action economy.

The system should consider:
- whether the user directly controls them;
- cognitive/control burden;
- independent intelligence;
- communication delays;
- shared chakra or resource costs;
- coordination skill;
- battlefield congestion.

Independent summons may act independently.

Directly controlled puppets or bodies may be limited by the controller's attention and skill.

Clones may expand battlefield presence but still create resource and coordination costs.

Detailed rules belong to relevant special-ability systems, but this section establishes the action-economy principle.

## 4.3.25 Team Coordination Can Create Shared Tempo

Well-coordinated teams can chain pressure more effectively than individuals.

Examples:
- one fighter forces a dodge into another fighter's attack;
- a defender intercepts while an ally prepares a jutsu;
- teammates rotate pressure to deny recovery;
- one character deliberately creates an opening for another.

Teamwork should create tactical advantage through timing and coordination, not a flat numerical bonus alone.

## 4.3.26 No Universal Action Count

There should be no global rule such as:
- one movement + one action + one bonus action;
- exactly two actions per turn;
- exactly one reaction per round.

Instead, what a character can accomplish before the tactical state changes depends on:
- speed;
- action complexity;
- mastery;
- current commitment;
- distance;
- awareness;
- fatigue;
- injuries;
- opponent pressure;
- environment.

The engine should still enforce consistency internally so that action freedom does not become narrative favoritism.

## 4.3.27 Hidden Timing Values May Be Used Internally

The engine may use concealed timing units or action-duration estimates to maintain consistency.

These should support simulation rather than become mandatory player-facing numbers.

The player normally needs to understand relative timing:
- immediate;
- quick;
- brief;
- committed;
- prolonged;
- interrupted;
- too slow under current pressure.

Exact hidden timing can be exposed only when a mechanic genuinely requires precision.

## 4.3.28 Action-Economy Compression

When the outcome of repeated low-risk exchanges is obvious, the engine may compress several actions into one summarized sequence.

Example:
- an elite taijutsu user rapidly overwhelms an ordinary opponent through a short combination.

Compression must preserve:
- resource use;
- elapsed time;
- injuries;
- positional changes;
- opportunities for meaningful interruption.

## 4.3.29 Minimum Timing State

During detailed combat, the engine should be able to track:
- current action or commitment;
- approximate phase if timing matters;
- who currently holds tempo;
- available reaction windows;
- movement/position;
- held or prepared actions;
- important recovery or delay;
- ongoing simultaneous actions;
- relevant timing effects from injury, fatigue, jutsu, or terrain.

## Core Design Rule

Speed should determine **how often meaningful opportunities appear, how narrow response windows become, and how easily a character can control tempo**.

It should not simply grant a fixed number of extra turns.
