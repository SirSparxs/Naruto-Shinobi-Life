# 4.8 — Damage Model

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines how physical contact becomes bodily trauma. It does not determine whether an attack lands; Ruleset 2 and Sections 4.5–4.7 already establish resolution, defense, contact quality, location, angle, and mitigation context.

The damage model should answer:

**What physical stresses reached the target, how much of those stresses were mitigated, and what injuries are plausible from the remaining exposure?**

## 4.8.1 No Universal Hit-Point Damage

The system should not treat all attacks as interchangeable quantities of one resource.

A punch, sword thrust, electrical discharge, burn, poison needle, crushing collapse, and explosion may all be dangerous for different reasons.

The simulation may use hidden numeric magnitudes internally, but they should represent specific physical effects rather than a universal pool of "damage."

## 4.8.2 Damage Is an Exposure Profile

A consequential hit should be describable through one or more exposure components.

Common components include:
- blunt/kinetic force;
- cutting/shearing;
- piercing/penetration;
- crushing/compression;
- thermal heat;
- cold;
- electrical exposure;
- explosive overpressure;
- fragmentation;
- chemical/toxic exposure;
- chakra disruption;
- special technique-specific effects.

An attack may combine several components.

Example:
An explosive tag may produce blast pressure, heat, fragmentation, and forced movement.

## 4.8.3 Damage Type and Injury Type Are Different

Damage type describes the physical mechanism.

Injury type describes what happened to the body.

Example:
- blunt force may cause bruising, fracture, internal bleeding, concussion, or organ rupture;
- cutting force may cause superficial laceration, tendon severing, vascular injury, or amputation;
- electrical exposure may cause pain, muscle disruption, burns, arrhythmia, or nerve injury.

The damage model produces trauma potential.
The injury system determines the actual injury.

## 4.8.4 Magnitude, Concentration, and Duration All Matter

The danger of an attack depends not only on total force or energy, but also on:
- how concentrated it is;
- how long exposure lasts;
- how large an area receives it;
- how deeply it penetrates;
- how quickly it is delivered.

A needle and a hammer may carry very different consequences even when their total energy is similar.

## 4.8.5 Contact Quality Modifies Effective Exposure

Section 4.7 contact quality affects how efficiently the attack transfers its intended stress.

A graze may:
- reduce penetration;
- shorten exposure;
- reduce force transfer;
- change location.

A strong clean hit may:
- improve alignment;
- increase depth;
- transfer more momentum;
- preserve the attack's intended geometry.

Contact quality should modify the physical interaction, not apply a universal multiplier.

## 4.8.6 Blunt / Kinetic Trauma

Blunt attacks transfer force without primarily cutting or penetrating.

Relevant factors include:
- mass;
- velocity;
- striking surface;
- acceleration;
- contact area;
- body reinforcement;
- target support;
- impact angle;
- whether the target can move with the blow.

Possible consequences include:
- bruising;
- muscle damage;
- fractures;
- joint injury;
- concussion;
- organ trauma;
- knockback;
- loss of balance.

## 4.8.7 Force Transfer and Target Movement

A target able to move with an impact may absorb force differently from a target pinned against a rigid surface.

Examples:
- a punch that launches someone backward may transfer less local crushing force than the same punch driving them into a wall;
- a body trapped between two forces may experience severe compression.

Movement and collision are therefore part of the final trauma calculation.

## 4.8.8 Cutting / Shearing Trauma

Cutting attacks concentrate force along an edge.

Relevant factors include:
- edge sharpness;
- attack speed;
- force;
- blade angle;
- draw length;
- tissue resistance;
- armor;
- contact quality.

Possible consequences include:
- superficial cuts;
- deep lacerations;
- muscle/tendon injury;
- vascular injury;
- nerve injury;
- severing.

A sharp weapon may cause severe local damage with relatively little blunt trauma.

## 4.8.9 Piercing / Penetrating Trauma

Piercing attacks concentrate force on a small point and attempt to enter the target.

Relevant factors include:
- point geometry;
- velocity;
- force;
- penetration resistance;
- armor;
- angle;
- depth;
- body region;
- wound path.

Possible consequences include:
- puncture;
- organ damage;
- vascular injury;
- retained projectile;
- exit wound;
- internal bleeding.

## 4.8.10 Crushing / Compression

Crushing occurs when tissue is compressed between forces or against a hard surface.

Examples:
- collapsed structure;
- giant summon;
- earth technique;
- heavy restraint;
- limb caught between surfaces.

Possible consequences include:
- fractures;
- tissue destruction;
- vascular compromise;
- compartment-type injury;
- organ compression;
- impaired breathing.

## 4.8.11 Thermal Heat

Heat damage depends on:
- temperature;
- duration;
- exposed area;
- material/clothing;
- moisture;
- whether flame adheres;
- protective techniques.

Consequences may include:
- superficial burns;
- deep burns;
- airway injury;
- dehydration;
- pain;
- tissue destruction;
- secondary ignition.

A brief flash and prolonged burning should not be treated identically.

## 4.8.12 Cold

Extreme cold may cause:
- numbness;
- slowed movement;
- tissue injury;
- brittle surfaces;
- hypothermic effects;
- reduced dexterity.

Cold effects may be localized or systemic depending on duration and exposure.

## 4.8.13 Electrical Exposure

Electrical attacks may have several distinct effects:
- pain;
- involuntary muscle contraction;
- temporary paralysis;
- nerve disruption;
- burns;
- cardiac disturbance;
- interference with fine motor control.

Relevant factors include:
- voltage/potential;
- current path;
- duration;
- conductivity;
- moisture;
- grounding;
- body region;
- chakra-specific behavior of the technique.

Electrical techniques should not be reduced to "lightning damage."

## 4.8.14 Explosive Effects

Explosions may create:
- blast overpressure;
- heat;
- fragmentation;
- debris;
- forced movement;
- structural collapse;
- hearing injury.

The consequence depends strongly on:
- distance from source;
- cover;
- enclosure;
- explosion size;
- body orientation;
- shielding.

An enclosed explosion may be more dangerous than the same explosion in open space.

## 4.8.15 Fragmentation and Debris

Explosions and structural damage can create secondary projectiles.

Fragments should behave as actual penetrating or cutting threats with their own:
- trajectories;
- velocities;
- hit locations;
- cover interactions.

They should not simply be folded into generic explosion damage when their injury consequences matter.

## 4.8.16 Falling and Deceleration

Fall injury depends on:
- height;
- landing surface;
- body orientation;
- ability to reduce speed;
- chakra-assisted movement;
- impact distribution;
- collisions during the fall.

The harmful mechanism is rapid deceleration, not "fall damage" as a special universal category.

## 4.8.17 Chakra-Enhanced Physical Attacks

Chakra may increase:
- force;
- acceleration;
- penetration;
- cutting ability;
- structural reinforcement;
- explosive release;
- precision.

Ruleset 3 defines how the technique or enhancement creates the effect.
Ruleset 4 interprets the physical result.

A chakra-enhanced punch is still primarily blunt/kinetic trauma unless the technique explicitly adds another mechanism.

## 4.8.18 Chakra-Construct and Energy-Like Attacks

Some techniques produce effects that do not map perfectly onto ordinary physics.

These should define an explicit damage profile in their Ruleset 3 records.

A technique should specify whether it primarily:
- cuts;
- burns;
- crushes;
- pierces;
- disrupts chakra;
- erodes;
- explodes;
- produces another specialized effect.

Ruleset 4 should avoid inventing a generic "chakra damage" category when a more concrete mechanism exists.

## 4.8.19 Chakra Network Disruption

Some attacks may specifically interfere with chakra pathways, tenketsu, or chakra circulation.

Possible consequences include:
- reduced chakra flow;
- inability to use certain techniques;
- pain;
- localized dysfunction;
- temporary or lasting pathway damage.

These effects should be tracked separately from ordinary tissue trauma when relevant.

## 4.8.20 Internal Versus External Trauma

Visible surface injury may poorly represent internal severity.

Examples:
- blunt abdominal impact with little skin damage but serious internal bleeding;
- armor stopping a blade but transmitting severe force;
- electrical exposure with limited visible burns but significant neuromuscular effects.

The system should not infer medical severity solely from visible injury.

## 4.8.21 Armor Mitigation Is Mechanism-Specific

Armor should interact differently with different attack types.

Armor may:
- prevent penetration;
- reduce cutting depth;
- distribute blunt force;
- insulate against heat;
- conduct electricity;
- stop fragments.

A material effective against one mechanism may be poor against another.

## 4.8.22 Armor Has Capacity and Failure Modes

Armor can be:
- penetrated;
- cut;
- dented;
- cracked;
- shattered;
- displaced;
- burned;
- electrically compromised.

Protection should depend on:
- material;
- thickness;
- coverage;
- construction;
- attack profile;
- previous damage.

Detailed durability tracking is only needed when continued protection matters.

## 4.8.23 Chakra Reinforcement

Chakra reinforcement may improve the body's resistance to trauma by:
- strengthening tissue;
- stabilizing joints;
- distributing force;
- hardening skin;
- reinforcing bone;
- reducing penetration.

The exact mechanism belongs to the technique or ability.

Reinforcement should reduce exposure before injury is determined rather than simply add temporary HP.

## 4.8.24 Reinforcement Has Limits

Reinforcement may fail when:
- force exceeds its capacity;
- concentration breaks;
- chakra is depleted;
- the attack bypasses the reinforced region;
- the attack uses a mechanism the reinforcement does not protect against.

Even successful reinforcement may still allow:
- knockback;
- pain;
- minor trauma;
- internal force transfer.

## 4.8.25 Natural Durability and Physiology

Attributes, body size, conditioning, species, clan traits, and exceptional physiology can affect how much exposure is required to cause injury.

This should modify thresholds and tissue resilience rather than grant generic damage reduction to every attack.

## 4.8.26 Strength of Attack and Durability of Target Are Relative

The same strike can:
- barely affect a highly reinforced elite;
- seriously injure an ordinary shinobi;
- catastrophically injure a civilian.

This difference should emerge from attack exposure versus target resilience.

## 4.8.27 Damage Thresholds Are Tissue- and Mechanism-Specific

Instead of one universal damage threshold, different tissues and structures should have different vulnerability.

Examples:
- bone resists some blunt force but can fracture;
- eyes are highly vulnerable to penetration;
- tendons can be disabled by relatively localized cuts;
- internal organs are vulnerable to compression and penetration;
- armor changes thresholds for covered areas.

The engine may abstract these into broad regional thresholds rather than model biomechanics in full detail.

## 4.8.28 Trauma Severity Bands

After mitigation, the remaining exposure can be interpreted into broad trauma potential such as:
- negligible;
- minor;
- moderate;
- severe;
- critical;
- catastrophic.

These bands are not injuries themselves.

They indicate the likely severity range of injury created at the affected location.

The exact injury is determined in 4.10.

## 4.8.29 Multiple Damage Components Can Combine

An attack may create several simultaneous trauma mechanisms.

Example:
A lightning-enhanced blade may:
- cut;
- penetrate;
- electrically disrupt tissue.

Each component may produce distinct consequences.

The system should avoid simply adding all components into one number when the mechanisms matter differently.

## 4.8.30 Synergistic Damage

Different mechanisms may amplify one another.

Examples:
- water increasing electrical conductivity;
- broken armor exposing a later cutting attack;
- heat weakening material;
- prior fracture making later blunt trauma more dangerous.

Synergy should be represented when physically or technique-specifically justified.

## 4.8.31 Redundant Damage Does Not Always Add Linearly

Two weak impacts to the same location may:
- accumulate;
- aggravate existing injury;
- produce diminishing effect;
- become more dangerous if they exceed a structural threshold.

The system should evaluate state changes rather than blindly sum damage points.

## 4.8.32 Existing Injuries Alter Resilience

Damaged tissue may be easier to injure again.

Examples:
- partially fractured bone;
- reopened wound;
- burned skin;
- strained joint;
- damaged chakra pathway.

The model should consult existing injury state when determining new trauma.

## 4.8.33 Damage to Equipment and Environment Uses the Same Logic

Weapons, armor, walls, trees, and structures can be affected by:
- blunt force;
- cutting;
- penetration;
- heat;
- explosion;
- specialized jutsu.

Their materials and construction determine resistance.

This avoids needing a wholly separate "object damage" philosophy.

## 4.8.34 Nonlethal Force Is Not a Separate Damage Type

There is no universal "nonlethal damage."

A character attempting restraint chooses:
- less dangerous target areas;
- lower force;
- safer techniques;
- controlled contact.

An attack intended to be nonlethal can still produce serious injury if poorly executed or circumstances change.

## 4.8.35 Excessive Force

If attack exposure greatly exceeds target resilience, consequences may escalate sharply rather than linearly.

Possible results include:
- massive fracture;
- tissue destruction;
- severing;
- catastrophic organ damage;
- extreme knockback;
- structural destruction.

This is how high-tier power disparity becomes physically meaningful without large HP pools.

## 4.8.36 Insufficient Force

An attack can land cleanly yet fail to create meaningful injury if the exposure is too low relative to the target's protection or durability.

The hit may still:
- cause pain;
- move the target;
- reveal information;
- consume defense;
- alter tempo.

A clean hit is not guaranteed injury.

## 4.8.37 No Automatic Minimum Damage

There should be no rule that every successful attack must reduce a health resource.

A weak punch against overwhelming reinforcement may cause effectively no tissue injury.

Its tactical consequences may still matter.

## 4.8.38 No Automatic Maximum Damage Cap

Likewise, the system should not artificially cap injury merely because the victim is important.

If an overwhelming attack lands cleanly on an unprotected vital region, catastrophic injury may result.

Survival must come from actual mitigation, physiology, rescue, or treatment.

## 4.8.39 Randomness After Contact Should Be Limited

Once contact quality, location, attack profile, and mitigation are known, injury should be mostly constrained by those facts.

Randomness may represent uncertain biological variation or ambiguous internal paths, but it should not routinely override obvious physical outcomes.

## 4.8.40 Hidden Numerical Implementation Is Allowed

The engine may internally calculate values such as:
- effective force;
- penetration potential;
- thermal dose;
- electrical severity;
- blast exposure;
- local resilience.

These values do not need to be exposed to the player.

Their purpose is consistency, not turning the game into a visible spreadsheet.

## 4.8.41 Suggested Damage Resolution Pipeline

For a consequential hit:

1. Identify attack profile.
2. Apply contact quality.
3. Identify actual location and path.
4. Apply cover/armor.
5. Apply active reinforcement or defensive mitigation.
6. Apply target physiology/resilience.
7. Account for movement, collision, and environment.
8. Determine remaining exposure components.
9. Determine trauma severity potential for each affected structure/region.
10. Pass the result to the injury system.

## 4.8.42 Damage Output Should Be a Trauma Record, Not an HP Number

A detailed damage result should be able to record:
- affected region;
- mechanism(s);
- effective severity;
- penetration or depth if relevant;
- force transfer;
- thermal/electrical/explosive exposure if relevant;
- armor/reinforcement performance;
- forced movement;
- likely injury severity range.

Example:

**Right forearm — cutting exposure, clean contact, moderate depth, no armor, severe local trauma potential, possible tendon involvement.**

This record then becomes input for injury determination.

## 4.8.43 Compression

During low-detail combat, the engine may collapse the full damage pipeline into a plausible summarized result.

Example:
- several minor blunt impacts;
- one moderate cutting wound;
- no meaningful injury through armor.

If an injury becomes important, the simulation can reconstruct or expand the relevant trauma record.

## Core Design Rule

Damage should represent **the physical stress that reached the target**, not a subtraction from a universal life bar.

The key question is:

**What mechanism reached what body region, with what effective intensity after mitigation, and what trauma can that plausibly create?**
