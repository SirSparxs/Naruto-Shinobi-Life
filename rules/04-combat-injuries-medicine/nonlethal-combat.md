# 4.25 — Nonlethal Combat

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines combat where the objective is to stop, restrain, capture, repel, or defeat an opponent without intentionally killing them.

Nonlethal combat should support:
- controlled force;
- surrender;
- restraint;
- temporary incapacitation;
- disarmament;
- capture;
- sparring and training;
- less-lethal tools and techniques.

The governing rule is:
**Nonlethal intent changes choices. It does not change physics.**

## 4.25.1 Nonlethal Is an Objective, Not a Damage Type

There should be no universal:
“Nonlethal Damage.”

Instead, a character pursuing a nonlethal objective chooses actions that reduce lethal risk.

Examples:
- restraint;
- disarmament;
- takedown;
- controlled strike;
- mobility denial;
- temporary sensory disruption;
- surrender pressure;
- capture techniques.

## 4.25.2 Intent Does Not Guarantee Safety

A character may intend to keep an opponent alive and still cause:
- fracture;
- internal injury;
- brain trauma;
- dangerous bleeding;
- respiratory compromise;
- accidental death.

The simulation should resolve what physically happened.

## 4.25.3 Nonlethal Force Is About Risk Management

A nonlethal attacker attempts to achieve control while minimizing:
- vital-organ exposure;
- excessive force;
- dangerous falls;
- prolonged physiological compromise;
- uncontrolled weapon penetration.

This usually makes the objective harder than simply maximizing destructive output.

## 4.25.4 Capture Usually Requires More Control Than Killing

Lethal combat may end once the opponent is no longer a threat.

Capture usually requires:
1. stopping resistance;
2. preventing escape;
3. securing weapons and techniques;
4. applying restraint;
5. preserving the captive's medical stability;
6. maintaining security afterward.

This makes live-capture missions meaningfully distinct.

## 4.25.5 Controlled Force

A trained character may deliberately reduce:
- strike force;
- penetration;
- follow-through;
- technique output;
- affected area.

Whether this is possible depends on:
- mastery;
- control;
- weapon;
- technique design;
- timing.

Not every technique can be safely downscaled.

## 4.25.6 Technique Minimum Output

Some jutsu may have a practical minimum output.

A technique designed to:
- explode;
- incinerate;
- penetrate deeply;
- destroy large areas

may be unsuitable for close nonlethal use even when the user is highly skilled.

Technique records should define controllability where relevant.

## 4.25.7 Precision Can Reduce Risk

A highly skilled fighter may choose lower-risk targets or angles.

The goal may be to:
- disrupt balance;
- disarm;
- limit mobility;
- force surrender;
- create a restraint opportunity.

Precision should not convert inherently catastrophic anatomy into safe targets.

## 4.25.8 Body Regions Differ in Risk

Attacks to:
- head;
- neck;
- chest;
- abdomen

may carry greater risk of severe injury.

Attacks to limbs can still cause:
- fracture;
- vascular injury;
- nerve injury.

There is no universally safe body target.

## 4.25.9 Knockouts Are Not a Safe Switch

Unconsciousness is a neurological state, not a harmless combat resource.

A forceful impact that causes unconsciousness may also cause:
- concussion;
- intracranial injury;
- airway risk;
- prolonged impairment.

Therefore:
“Knock them out”
should not be treated as a reliably safe generic action.

## 4.25.10 Temporary Incapacitation Has Multiple Forms

A nonlethal objective may be achieved through:
- restraint;
- exhaustion;
- disarmament plus surrender;
- temporary paralysis;
- sensory disruption;
- mobility denial;
- pain compliance;
- controlled unconsciousness from an explicit safe-capability technique;
- environmental isolation.

The cause should determine the risk.

## 4.25.11 Unconscious Does Not Mean Secured

An unconscious opponent may:
- recover;
- still possess weapons;
- still possess active techniques;
- remain medically unstable.

A capture team should still:
- assess;
- disarm;
- restrain;
- monitor.

## 4.25.12 Restraint Is Often the Safest Route

If feasible, restraint may be preferable to repeated injury.

Restraint can include:
- grappling;
- bindings;
- seals;
- terrain control;
- chakra suppression.

Detailed physical restraint belongs to 4.15.

## 4.25.13 Pain Compliance Is Unreliable

Pain may encourage surrender or cooperation.

However, a target may:
- tolerate pain;
- suppress pain;
- accept injury;
- be unable to perceive pain normally.

Pain should not be treated as guaranteed control.

## 4.25.14 Surrender Is Distinct From Incapacitation

A character can stop fighting voluntarily while still physically capable of resistance.

Surrender may result from:
- hopeless position;
- negotiation;
- threat of injury;
- mission failure;
- protection of others;
- command.

Once accepted, the encounter may de-escalate without further injury.

## 4.25.15 Surrender Must Be Maintainable

A surrendering target may still need:
- disarmament;
- restraint;
- escort;
- monitoring.

Trust and context determine how much physical security is necessary.

## 4.25.16 Disarmament

Removing an opponent's weapon may significantly reduce threat without injuring them.

Disarmament may occur through:
- control;
- leverage;
- impact;
- forced release;
- destruction of the weapon.

Detailed mechanics belong to 4.15 and 4.16.

## 4.25.17 Mobility Denial

A character may attempt to prevent escape by:
- blocking routes;
- trapping terrain;
- controlling footing;
- restraining legs;
- creating barriers.

Mobility denial can achieve capture without directly injuring the target.

## 4.25.18 Sensory Disruption

Temporary:
- smoke;
- flash;
- darkness;
- sound disruption;
- sensory jutsu

may reduce an opponent's ability to fight effectively.

These effects should follow 4.14 condition rules.

## 4.25.19 Paralysis and Neuromuscular Control

A technique that temporarily prevents movement may be useful for capture.

However:
- cause;
- duration;
- respiratory effects;
- medical risk

must be explicit.

A generic “paralysis” label is not automatically safe.

## 4.25.20 Sedation and Sleep Effects

Some techniques or medicines may induce:
- drowsiness;
- sedation;
- sleep;
- unconsciousness.

Their onset, dose, duration, and medical risk should be defined through 4.23 and relevant technique records.

There is no universal risk-free sleep effect.

## 4.25.21 Less-Lethal Weapons

Tools may be designed primarily for:
- restraint;
- entanglement;
- blunt control;
- marking;
- sensory disruption.

“Less lethal” means reduced expected lethality under intended use, not incapable of causing death.

## 4.25.22 Blunt Weapons Are Not Automatically Nonlethal

Clubs, staffs, and blunt strikes can still cause:
- fractures;
- internal trauma;
- brain injury.

Weapon mechanism determines risk.

## 4.25.23 Reversed or Flat-Blade Use

A weapon user may sometimes alter technique to reduce cutting or penetration.

This may convert the dominant exposure toward:
- blunt impact;
- lower tissue penetration.

That still carries injury risk.

## 4.25.24 Chakra Output Control

A skilled shinobi may reduce jutsu output when:
- the technique supports modulation;
- their mastery is sufficient;
- the reduced version remains stable.

Poor control may cause:
- overdelivery;
- technique failure;
- unintended injury.

## 4.25.25 Nonlethal Specialist Techniques

Some techniques may be designed specifically for:
- capture;
- restraint;
- suppression;
- immobilization;
- sensory deprivation;
- temporary chakra disruption.

These should generally be safer than repurposing destructive techniques, but still have defined failure modes.

## 4.25.26 Chakra Suppression

A restrained target may remain dangerous if they can still use chakra.

Capture methods may therefore need to:
- block hand seals;
- disrupt chakra flow;
- apply suppression seals;
- limit access to specific techniques.

Ruleset 3 defines the supernatural mechanism.

## 4.25.27 Nonlethal Against Stronger Opponents

A weaker character may be unable to safely restrain a much stronger opponent without:
- teamwork;
- traps;
- terrain;
- specialized bindings;
- targeted disabling tactics.

Nonlethal goals can therefore increase mission difficulty dramatically.

## 4.25.28 Nonlethal Against Faster Opponents

Fast targets may be hard to capture because they can:
- disengage;
- evade restraint;
- break line of control.

Area denial, prediction, traps, and teamwork may be more useful than direct pursuit.

## 4.25.29 Team Capture

A team can divide roles such as:
- pressure;
- route denial;
- disarmament;
- restraint;
- chakra suppression;
- medical monitoring.

This often makes nonlethal capture safer and more reliable.

## 4.25.30 Overwhelming Advantage Can Reduce Injury

If the power or skill disparity is enormous, the stronger combatant may be able to:
- control;
- restrain;
- disarm

with little injury.

This should arise from actual capability disparity, not narrative convenience.

## 4.25.31 Desperation Raises Lethality Risk

Nonlethal control becomes harder when:
- the attacker is exhausted;
- the target is berserk;
- time is running out;
- civilians are threatened;
- lethal force is being used against the captor.

The simulation should reflect how deteriorating circumstances increase accidental harm.

## 4.25.32 Accidental Lethality

A nonlethal encounter may become fatal through:
- bad fall;
- hidden medical condition;
- excessive force;
- structural collapse;
- untreated injury;
- delayed complication.

The engine should not protect targets from these outcomes because the mission said “alive.”

## 4.25.33 Existing Injuries Increase Risk

A technique normally considered relatively safe may become dangerous if the target already has:
- head injury;
- internal bleeding;
- fracture;
- respiratory compromise;
- severe exhaustion.

The attacker may not know this.

This creates real uncertainty in capture missions.

## 4.25.34 Unknown Physiology Increases Risk

Unusual anatomy, regeneration, bloodlines, or medical conditions may change:
- what force is safe;
- what restraints work;
- what toxins or sedatives do.

Lack of knowledge can make nonlethal capture harder.

## 4.25.35 Medical Assessment After Capture

A subdued target may require immediate assessment for:
- head injury;
- breathing problems;
- bleeding;
- restraint injury;
- toxin effects.

A successful capture can still become a failed mission if the prisoner dies afterward.

## 4.25.36 Restraint Monitoring

Bindings can cause:
- circulation problems;
- nerve compression;
- respiratory restriction;
- pressure injury

if applied dangerously or left unchecked.

Long-term restraint should be monitored when medically relevant.

## 4.25.37 Transport Risk

A captive may be:
- injured;
- sedated;
- poisoned;
- restrained.

Transport can worsen their medical state.

Captors are responsible for preserving a viable prisoner if the mission requires live delivery.

## 4.25.38 Nonlethal Combat and Training

Sparring usually has stricter limits than live capture.

Training may restrict:
- technique output;
- weapon type;
- target areas;
- dangerous jutsu;
- environmental destruction.

Protective equipment and supervision may further reduce risk.

## 4.25.39 Sparring Injuries Can Still Occur

Training is not consequence-free.

Possible outcomes include:
- bruises;
- sprains;
- fractures;
- concussion;
- accidental technique injury.

Safety rules reduce probability and severity rather than eliminate them.

## 4.25.40 Stop Conditions in Training

A training bout may end when:
- participant yields;
- instructor stops it;
- defined objective is achieved;
- dangerous injury risk emerges;
- one participant can no longer safely continue.

This prevents sparring from requiring literal incapacitation.

## 4.25.41 Tournament or Exam Rules

Formal combat events may define:
- prohibited techniques;
- surrender rules;
- medical intervention thresholds;
- boundary rules;
- lethal-force restrictions.

These are event rules layered on top of normal combat physics.

## 4.25.42 Law-Enforcement or Arrest Context

Some shinobi roles may prioritize:
- compliance;
- arrest;
- restraint;
- evidence preservation;
- public safety.

The physical mechanics remain the same.
Legal and organizational standards belong to world systems.

## 4.25.43 Mercy and Restraint Are Character Decisions

A character may choose to:
- spare;
- accept surrender;
- refuse a killing opportunity;
- use lower force.

These are agency and personality decisions, not automatic mechanics.

## 4.25.44 Nonlethal Intent Should Be Declared as Goal or Constraint

The engine should know when a character intends:
- capture alive;
- incapacitate without severe injury;
- spar safely;
- drive opponent away.

This informs action selection and risk tolerance.

It does not alter the outcome after contact.

## 4.25.45 Risk Bands for Nonlethal Methods

The engine may internally categorize methods as:
- low expected injury risk;
- moderate injury risk;
- high injury risk;
- inherently severe/lethal risk.

These are contextual assessments, not fixed labels.

A normally low-risk method may become high-risk because of:
- terrain;
- target condition;
- technique intensity.

## 4.25.46 Nonlethal Success Quality

A successful nonlethal action can vary.

Examples:
- target restrained without injury;
- target restrained with minor injury;
- target incapacitated but medically unstable;
- target captured but seriously injured.

Mission success and medical outcome are separate.

## 4.25.47 Mission Evaluation

A live-capture mission may care about:
- target alive;
- target conscious or unconscious;
- injury severity;
- intelligence preserved;
- evidence preserved;
- collateral damage;
- team casualties.

These evaluation criteria belong to mission systems, while Ruleset 4 records the physical outcomes.

## 4.25.48 Compression

Routine low-risk control can be summarized when the outcome is obvious.

Examples:
- elite shinobi safely restrains cooperative civilian;
- surrendered prisoner is bound and escorted.

The simulation should zoom in when:
- target actively resists;
- injury risk is significant;
- hidden techniques exist;
- live capture is the mission objective.

## Core Design Rule

Nonlethal combat should mean:
**choose tactics that minimize lethality while still achieving control.**

It should never mean:
**declare an attack nonlethal and ignore the consequences.**

A successful live capture requires the target to remain both:
**secured** and **medically alive**.
