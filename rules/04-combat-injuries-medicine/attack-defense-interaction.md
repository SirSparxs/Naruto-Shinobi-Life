# 4.5 — Attack & Defense Interaction

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines how offensive actions interact with defensive responses. Ruleset 2 resolves uncertainty and opposed outcomes; Ruleset 4 determines what defensive options are available, how they differ, what they cost tactically, and what consequences follow from the exchange.

## 4.5.1 An Attack Is More Than a Hit Attempt

An attack may attempt to:
- injure;
- force movement;
- break guard;
- interrupt;
- disarm;
- pin;
- restrain;
- create an opening;
- destroy cover;
- pressure a reaction;
- protect another objective.

Not every attack is primarily about damage.

## 4.5.2 Defense Is Chosen, Not Assumed

When a threat is perceived in time, the defender may choose among available responses.

Possible defenses include:
- dodge;
- retreat;
- block;
- parry;
- deflect;
- intercept;
- counterattack;
- substitution;
- defensive jutsu;
- terrain use;
- body reinforcement;
- protecting another person;
- accepting the hit to complete another action.

Available options depend on awareness, timing, position, current commitment, injuries, equipment, technique access, and the attack itself.

## 4.5.3 No Universal Passive Defense Score

A passive defensive baseline may exist internally for trivial or compressed resolution, but important combat should not reduce defense to a single static number.

A character who is excellent at dodging may be poor at blocking.
A heavily armored fighter may accept attacks that another character must evade.
A trapped fighter may know how to dodge but have nowhere to move.

Defense is contextual.

## 4.5.4 The Defender Must Have a Valid Response Window

A defensive option can only be attempted if:
- the threat is detected or anticipated;
- enough time exists;
- the defender is physically capable of the response;
- the response is compatible with their current commitment;
- the battlefield permits it.

A defense that would be excellent in theory may be unavailable in practice.

## 4.5.5 Dodge

A dodge avoids the harmful path of an attack.

Dodge effectiveness depends on:
- available space;
- acceleration and agility;
- perception;
- reaction timing;
- attack size;
- attack tracking;
- terrain;
- current momentum;
- injury;
- prediction.

A successful dodge may still cost position or tempo.

## 4.5.6 Evasion Is Different From Retreat

A dodge may only move enough to avoid contact.

A retreat deliberately creates distance.

A character can evade an attack without successfully disengaging from the attacker.

## 4.5.7 Block

A block accepts contact against a protective surface, weapon, body part, barrier, or reinforced guard.

A block may:
- stop the attack;
- reduce force;
- redirect force;
- protect a vital area;
- still cause trauma through impact;
- damage the blocking object;
- fail if the attack overwhelms the guard.

Blocking is usually more positionally stable than dodging but carries greater risk from superior force.

## 4.5.8 Parry and Deflection

A parry redirects or interferes with an attack rather than absorbing it directly.

It relies heavily on:
- timing;
- weapon or limb control;
- precision;
- reach;
- angle;
- awareness.

Parries may create strong counter opportunities but can be poor against:
- massive area attacks;
- overwhelming force;
- attacks that cannot meaningfully be redirected.

## 4.5.9 Interception

Interception means affecting the attack before it reaches its intended target.

Examples:
- striking an attacker's arm;
- knocking away a projectile;
- placing a barrier in the path;
- tackling an ally out of danger;
- entering the attack path;
- destroying the attack itself.

Interception can be performed by the target or a third party.

## 4.5.10 Counterattack

A counterattack attempts to exploit the attacker's commitment.

A counter may occur:
- before execution;
- during execution;
- immediately after a missed or blocked attack;
- while accepting some risk.

Counterattacking is generally more dangerous than pure defense because the defender may devote fewer resources to avoidance.

## 4.5.11 Defense by Offense

Some attacks can be answered by creating a more urgent threat.

Examples:
- attacking the opponent's exposed hand during seals;
- forcing them to evade instead of completing their strike;
- attacking the support structure of an enemy technique.

This is valid only when timing makes the new threat relevant before the original attack resolves.

## 4.5.12 Substitution

Substitution should not be treated as an automatic teleport or universal escape button.

It should require an established technique, valid timing, chakra expenditure, available substitute conditions where applicable, and a plausible opportunity to execute.

Its exact mechanics belong to Ruleset 3 technique records; Ruleset 4 determines when the combat timing permits its use and what positional state follows.

## 4.5.13 Defensive Jutsu

Defensive jutsu may:
- block;
- absorb;
- redirect;
- nullify;
- obscure;
- displace;
- create terrain;
- escape;
- counter an attack's nature.

Their effectiveness depends on the specific technique rather than a universal defense category.

Powerful defense may still cost:
- chakra;
- setup time;
- mobility;
- visibility;
- terrain;
- future options.

## 4.5.14 Body Reinforcement

Some characters can use chakra, armor, physiology, or techniques to endure attacks rather than avoid them.

Reinforcement may reduce:
- penetration;
- blunt trauma;
- burns;
- structural damage.

It does not necessarily eliminate:
- knockback;
- pain;
- internal effects;
- elemental interactions;
- weak-point attacks.

## 4.5.15 Taking the Hit Is a Valid Tactical Choice

A character may intentionally accept harm to:
- finish a technique;
- protect an ally;
- maintain a grapple;
- preserve position;
- trade attacks;
- prevent an enemy from escaping.

The simulation should resolve the consequences normally rather than treating intentional contact as harmless.

## 4.5.16 Defense Selection Creates Tradeoffs

Different defenses protect different priorities.

Dodge:
- avoids force;
- may lose position.

Block:
- preserves position;
- risks absorbing force.

Parry:
- may create a counter;
- demands timing and precision.

Counterattack:
- may seize tempo;
- exposes the defender.

Defensive jutsu:
- can be powerful;
- costs chakra and may require setup.

There should rarely be one universally optimal defense.

## 4.5.17 Outcome Degree Affects the Exchange

Ruleset 2 outcome degree should influence combat interpretation.

Examples:
- narrow defensive success: attack barely misses, defender loses balance;
- strong defensive success: clean evasion with retained position;
- exceptional defense: evade and gain counter opportunity;
- narrow offensive success: glancing contact;
- strong offensive success: solid contact;
- exceptional offensive success: attack lands with superior placement or timing.

The exact physical consequence still depends on attack type and context.

## 4.5.18 Successful Defense Can Still Have Consequences

A block can succeed but fracture a weapon.
A dodge can succeed but move the defender into a trap.
A parry can succeed but leave the arm numb.
A barrier can stop an attack but consume large chakra.

“Defense succeeded” means the primary threat was sufficiently answered, not that nothing happened.

## 4.5.19 Partial Defense

An attack may be only partly mitigated.

Examples:
- dodge avoids a vital hit but receives a cut;
- block reduces a crushing blow but still causes a fracture;
- barrier weakens a fire technique but lets heat through;
- armor stops penetration but transfers blunt trauma.

Partial defense should be common when offensive and defensive performances are close.

## 4.5.20 Defense Can Fail by Type

A defensive response may be inappropriate even when executed skillfully.

Examples:
- blocking an attack too powerful to absorb;
- parrying an explosion;
- dodging inside an area that is fully saturated;
- using conductive armor against certain electrical attacks.

A poor defensive choice changes the situation before Ruleset 2 evaluates execution.

## 4.5.21 Area Attacks

Area attacks threaten space rather than a single point.

Possible defenses include:
- leave the affected area;
- reach cover;
- interrupt before release;
- shield;
- counter with another technique;
- reduce exposure;
- endure through reinforcement.

The size, shape, propagation speed, duration, and terrain interaction determine whether evasion is possible.

## 4.5.22 Tracking and Homing Attacks

Some attacks can adjust toward a moving target.

Against tracking attacks, a simple initial dodge may not be sufficient.

Defenders may need to:
- break tracking;
- outrun turning capability;
- use cover;
- destroy the attack;
- redirect it;
- conceal themselves;
- force collision with terrain.

Specific behavior belongs to the technique record.

## 4.5.23 Projectile Defense

Projectile defense depends on:
- projectile speed;
- distance;
- warning;
- number of projectiles;
- spread;
- trajectory;
- cover;
- defender perception;
- deflection capability.

A large projectile volley may overwhelm a defender by reducing safe paths rather than by increasing a generic attack bonus.

## 4.5.24 Melee Exchanges

Close combat often creates rapid sequences of:
- attack;
- defense;
- counter;
- reposition;
- renewed attack.

The engine may resolve these at whatever granularity is meaningful.

Routine exchanges can be compressed.
Important openings can be expanded into individual action windows.

## 4.5.25 Guard and Defensive Posture

A character may deliberately adopt a defensive posture.

This can improve:
- readiness;
- reaction quality;
- protection of specific areas;
- interception.

It may reduce:
- offensive pressure;
- movement speed;
- access to complex attacks.

Guard is therefore a tactical commitment, not a flat defense buff.

## 4.5.26 Feints

A feint attempts to provoke a defensive commitment without delivering the apparent attack.

If successful, it may:
- move the defender;
- expose a guard;
- consume a prepared response;
- reveal habits;
- create an opening.

Ruleset 2 resolves deception and reading when uncertain.

## 4.5.27 Defensive Habits Can Be Learned

Repeated combat may reveal patterns.

Examples:
- always dodges left;
- blocks high attacks;
- substitutes after one specific setup;
- protects an injured side.

Perception, intelligence, experience, and familiarity can allow opponents to exploit these habits.

## 4.5.28 Repeated Pressure Narrows Options

Repeated attacks can degrade defense even without direct injury by:
- forcing movement;
- consuming chakra;
- breaking equipment;
- limiting space;
- creating fatigue;
- revealing patterns;
- preventing preparation.

This is how sustained pressure becomes meaningful without arbitrary stacking penalties.

## 4.5.29 Multiple Attackers Create Overlapping Demands

A defender facing multiple attacks must answer each actual threat.

The difficulty comes from:
- different angles;
- overlapping timing;
- divided attention;
- incompatible defenses;
- limited movement space.

The system should not merely add a generic “outnumbered penalty.”

## 4.5.30 Protecting Another Character

A defender may intervene for an ally by:
- intercepting;
- blocking;
- moving the ally;
- counterattacking;
- placing cover;
- using a barrier;
- taking the hit.

This requires awareness, position, timing, and capability.

Protection may expose the protector to additional risk.

## 4.5.31 Defense of Objectives

Characters may defend:
- doors;
- bridges;
- civilians;
- wounded allies;
- scrolls;
- structures;
- escape routes.

An attack can succeed tactically even if it never injures a defender, for example by forcing them away from what they were protecting.

## 4.5.32 Breaking Guard

An attacker may deliberately attempt to compromise defense rather than directly injure.

Methods include:
- overpowering a block;
- attacking balance;
- striking a weapon;
- forcing repeated defense;
- attacking from multiple angles;
- using terrain;
- feinting.

A broken guard creates a temporary opening rather than a permanent condition.

## 4.5.33 Weapon and Limb Interaction

Defenses may involve direct contact between weapons or limbs.

Consequences may include:
- weapon damage;
- disarm;
- numbness;
- joint strain;
- blade binding;
- positional control.

These should be resolved only when consequential.

## 4.5.34 Defense Against Unknown Techniques

A defender may not know what an attack does.

They must respond based on observable information and prior knowledge.

Misjudging an unfamiliar technique can produce a poor defensive choice even if the character is highly skilled.

This reinforces the importance of information and experience.

## 4.5.35 Automatic Defense Is Rare

Highly trained characters may automatically handle trivial threats during compressed resolution.

However, meaningful attacks should not be silently negated solely because the target is high rank.

If the threat could plausibly matter, the simulation should determine what defensive response actually occurred.

## 4.5.36 No Guaranteed Defense Loop

A defender cannot indefinitely avoid danger simply because they possess a strong defensive skill.

Pressure can alter:
- position;
- stamina;
- chakra;
- terrain;
- available reaction windows;
- defensive predictability.

Likewise, an attacker cannot assume repeated attacks must eventually hit if the defender retains superior control.

## 4.5.37 Attack and Defense Records

For detailed exchanges, the engine should be able to identify:
- attacking action;
- intended target/objective;
- attack path or threatened area;
- defender awareness;
- available reaction window;
- chosen defense;
- relevant positioning;
- resolution result;
- outcome degree;
- resulting tactical or physical consequence.

## Core Design Rule

Defense should answer the actual threat through a concrete action.

The simulation should ask:
**What did the attacker attempt? What could the defender perceive? What responses were actually available? Which response was chosen? How did the opposed resolution end? What changed afterward?**

It should not reduce important exchanges to “attack roll versus armor class.”
