# 4.23 — Poison, Toxins, Disease & Antidotes

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines harmful biological, chemical, and infectious processes that alter a character over time.

It covers:
- poisons;
- venoms;
- toxic gases;
- contaminated weapons;
- drugs and harmful compounds;
- disease;
- antidotes;
- detoxification;
- toxin resistance;
- medical treatment.

The system should avoid generic “poison damage per turn.”

A toxic or infectious effect should answer:
**What entered the body, how did it enter, how much exposure occurred, what systems does it affect, how quickly does it progress, and what can stop or reverse it?**

## 4.23.1 Poison Is a Physiological Process

A poison should not simply apply:
“Poisoned: -10” or “5 damage per round.”

It should have:
- substance;
- exposure route;
- effective dose;
- onset;
- target systems;
- progression;
- symptoms;
- duration;
- treatment;
- complications.

## 4.23.2 Poison, Venom, and Toxin

For simulation purposes:

### Poison
A harmful substance entering the body by ingestion, inhalation, absorption, injection, or another route.

### Venom
A toxin actively delivered through a bite, sting, fang, spine, needle, or equivalent mechanism.

### Toxin
The broader harmful substance category.

The distinction is mostly descriptive unless the delivery route matters mechanically.

## 4.23.3 Exposure Route Matters

Relevant routes may include:
- injection;
- ingestion;
- inhalation;
- skin absorption;
- eye/mucosal exposure;
- wound contamination;
- chakra-assisted delivery.

Different routes can alter:
- onset;
- effective dose;
- treatment options;
- local injury.

## 4.23.4 Exposure Does Not Always Equal Effective Dose

A toxin may contact the body without enough entering circulation to create meaningful effects.

Effective exposure depends on:
- concentration;
- quantity;
- duration;
- route;
- barrier protection;
- wound depth;
- immediate cleaning;
- physiology.

## 4.23.5 Dose Matters

Toxic effects should generally scale with effective dose.

Broad exposure bands may include:
- trace;
- low;
- moderate;
- high;
- extreme.

These are not universal thresholds.
Each toxin defines its own potency.

## 4.23.6 Repeated Exposure Can Accumulate

Several small exposures may combine.

Examples:
- repeated poisoned senbon;
- prolonged gas exposure;
- repeated bites;
- chronic contamination.

Accumulation depends on:
- metabolism;
- toxin persistence;
- interval between exposures.

## 4.23.7 Onset Can Be Immediate or Delayed

A toxin may act:
- immediately;
- within seconds;
- over minutes;
- over hours;
- after a long latent period.

Delayed onset can make detection and attribution difficult.

## 4.23.8 Progression Phases

A toxin may use phases such as:

### Latent
Exposure occurred but no obvious symptoms yet.

### Early Effects
Initial symptoms begin.

### Established Toxicity
Primary physiological effects are active.

### Severe Toxicity
Major functional impairment or organ danger develops.

### Critical Toxicity
Vital systems are failing.

### Resolution
Toxin is neutralized, metabolized, removed, or no longer progressing.

Not every toxin needs every phase.

## 4.23.9 Toxins Should Target Actual Systems

Possible target systems include:
- nervous system;
- muscles;
- blood/clotting;
- circulation;
- lungs;
- liver;
- kidneys;
- gastrointestinal system;
- senses;
- chakra network.

The target determines symptoms and danger.

## 4.23.10 Neurotoxins

Neurotoxic effects may include:
- numbness;
- tremor;
- poor coordination;
- weakness;
- paralysis;
- sensory disruption;
- respiratory failure.

Different neurotoxins may affect different pathways.

## 4.23.11 Paralytic Toxins

A paralytic toxin should specify:
- which muscles are affected;
- whether sensation remains;
- whether breathing muscles are threatened;
- progression rate.

Paralysis is a condition produced by the toxin, not the toxin itself.

## 4.23.12 Hemotoxic / Coagulation Effects

Toxins may:
- prevent clotting;
- trigger abnormal clotting;
- damage blood vessels;
- destroy blood cells;
- worsen hemorrhage.

These effects interface with 4.11.

## 4.23.13 Respiratory Toxins

Some substances may:
- irritate airways;
- damage lungs;
- suppress breathing;
- impair oxygen transport.

The resulting medical danger should use normal respiratory deterioration rules.

## 4.23.14 Organ-Toxic Effects

Toxins may primarily damage:
- liver;
- kidneys;
- heart;
- other organs.

These may have delayed consequences even after the initial encounter ends.

## 4.23.15 Sensory Toxins

Some compounds may cause:
- blurred vision;
- hallucination;
- hearing disturbance;
- altered balance;
- numbness.

These feed into 4.14 conditions.

## 4.23.16 Chakra-Affecting Toxins

Naruto-world toxins may interfere with:
- chakra molding;
- chakra circulation;
- tenketsu function;
- nature transformation;
- chakra recovery.

Such effects should be explicitly defined.

They are distinct from simply draining chakra reserves.

## 4.23.17 Local and Systemic Effects

A toxin may cause:
- local injury at the exposure site;
- systemic effects after entering circulation.

Example:
A corrosive venom may damage tissue around a bite and later produce widespread neurological effects.

These should be tracked separately when meaningful.

## 4.23.18 Toxin Progression Is Time-Based

Poison effects should progress according to elapsed time, not arbitrary turns.

Combat action windows can approximate elapsed time while the toxin continues advancing.

Progression continues:
- during combat;
- during travel;
- during sleep;
- during treatment.

## 4.23.19 Activity Can Change Progression

Depending on the toxin, exertion may:
- increase circulation;
- speed distribution;
- worsen respiratory demand;
- accelerate collapse.

Rest may slow some processes but should not universally stop poison.

## 4.23.20 Metabolism and Clearance

A toxin may leave the body through:
- metabolism;
- excretion;
- breakdown;
- medical removal.

Clearance rate depends on:
- substance;
- dose;
- physiology;
- organ function;
- treatment.

## 4.23.21 Resistance

Toxin resistance may come from:
- natural physiology;
- clan traits;
- training;
- repeated controlled exposure;
- medication;
- specialized techniques.

Resistance may reduce:
- absorption;
- effect severity;
- progression speed;
- duration.

It should not automatically grant total immunity.

## 4.23.22 Immunity Must Be Specific

A character should not have:
“Poison Immunity”
unless the actual physiology or ability justifies a very broad effect.

More commonly:
- resistant to certain snake venoms;
- immune to one known compound;
- highly resistant to sedatives.

## 4.23.23 Tolerance Is Not Immunity

Repeated exposure may allow a character to tolerate a substance better.

Tolerance may:
- reduce symptoms;
- increase required dose.

It may not:
- prevent organ damage;
- protect against every related toxin.

The toxin profile determines what tolerance changes.

## 4.23.24 Poisoned Weapons

Weapons may deliver toxins through:
- coated edges;
- needles;
- barbs;
- hollow delivery systems.

Successful contact does not automatically guarantee full dose.

Dose depends on:
- penetration;
- toxin quantity;
- clothing/armor;
- whether the wound actually receives the substance.

## 4.23.25 Senbon and Needles

Small penetrating tools may be efficient toxin-delivery methods because they can:
- puncture exposed skin;
- carry precise compounds;
- target vulnerable anatomy.

Their direct physical trauma may be minor while the delivered substance is the real threat.

## 4.23.26 Gas and Aerosol Toxins

Airborne toxins should define:
- affected area;
- concentration;
- exposure duration;
- ventilation;
- protective equipment;
- persistence.

A character entering a gas cloud for one second should not necessarily receive the same dose as someone trapped inside it for five minutes.

## 4.23.27 Environmental Contamination

A toxin may contaminate:
- water;
- food;
- surfaces;
- enclosed spaces;
- weapons.

Contamination can persist after the original attacker is gone.

## 4.23.28 Detection

Poisons may be discovered through:
- smell;
- taste;
- visible residue;
- symptoms;
- medical testing;
- diagnostic ninjutsu;
- intelligence about the attacker.

Detection follows Ruleset 2 information principles.

## 4.23.29 Hidden Toxins

The engine may know:
- exact substance;
- dose;
- progression.

The character may only experience:
- dizziness;
- numbness;
- weakness.

A medic may initially know only:
“Neurotoxin likely.”

## 4.23.30 Identification Levels

Useful diagnostic outcomes may include:
- toxin exposure suspected;
- toxin class identified;
- likely compound;
- exact compound confirmed.

Treatment confidence improves with better identification.

## 4.23.31 Antidotes

An antidote should define what it actually does.

Possible functions:
- bind toxin;
- neutralize toxin;
- block receptor effects;
- accelerate breakdown;
- counter one physiological consequence.

No universal antidote should cure all poisons unless explicitly designed that way.

## 4.23.32 Antidote Timing

Some antidotes work best:
- before severe symptoms;
- before toxin binds permanently;
- before organ injury occurs.

An antidote may stop further progression without reversing damage already caused.

## 4.23.33 Antidote Dose

Treatment may require:
- one dose;
- repeated dosing;
- dose based on exposure severity.

Insufficient antidote may:
- partially slow progression;
- fail against extreme exposure.

## 4.23.34 Wrong Antidote

Using the wrong treatment may:
- do nothing;
- waste time;
- create side effects;
- interact badly with another compound.

Diagnosis therefore matters.

## 4.23.35 Broad-Spectrum Detoxification

Advanced medical techniques may:
- remove circulating toxins;
- support multiple organs;
- accelerate purification.

Even broad-spectrum methods should define:
- what classes they affect;
- required chakra;
- time;
- limits.

## 4.23.36 Medical Ninjutsu and Poison

Medical ninjutsu may:
- detect toxin;
- slow circulation;
- extract substance;
- repair toxin-damaged tissue;
- support failing organs.

Stopping toxicity and repairing damage are separate objectives.

## 4.23.37 Damage Can Remain After Toxin Is Gone

Neutralizing the poison does not automatically undo:
- nerve damage;
- organ injury;
- bleeding;
- tissue necrosis.

Those injuries must be treated separately.

## 4.23.38 Supportive Care

If no antidote exists, treatment may focus on:
- maintaining breathing;
- supporting circulation;
- controlling seizures;
- managing temperature;
- protecting organs;
- buying time for metabolism.

A patient can survive poison without direct neutralization if vital systems are supported long enough.

## 4.23.39 Poison Specialists

A specialist may be skilled at:
- toxin creation;
- antidote design;
- dose estimation;
- concealed delivery;
- diagnosis;
- resistance training.

Expertise should come from learned skill, not rank alone.

## 4.23.40 Creating Poisons

Poison creation should require:
- knowledge;
- ingredients;
- preparation;
- equipment;
- time.

Potency, stability, and detectability may vary with craftsmanship.

Detailed crafting can belong to a later item/crafting system.

## 4.23.41 Toxin Stability

Some compounds may:
- degrade with time;
- lose potency in heat;
- wash away in water;
- evaporate;
- remain stable for years.

Storage and environment can matter when relevant.

## 4.23.42 Poisoned Weapon Shelf Life

A coated blade may lose toxin through:
- time;
- rain;
- contact;
- heat;
- cleaning.

The game should not assume a weapon remains poisoned forever.

## 4.23.43 Disease Is Different From Poison

Disease generally involves:
- infection or pathological biological process;
- incubation;
- replication or progression;
- immune response;
- transmission.

It should not simply reuse poison timing with a different label.

## 4.23.44 Disease Incubation

Disease may have an incubation period where:
- infection exists;
- symptoms are absent.

Characters may remain unaware while still becoming contagious if the disease allows it.

## 4.23.45 Disease Transmission

Transmission may occur through:
- respiratory droplets;
- blood;
- bodily fluids;
- contaminated surfaces;
- food/water;
- vectors;
- specialized chakra mechanisms.

Each disease should define its route.

## 4.23.46 Infectiousness and Severity Are Separate

A disease may be:
- highly contagious but mild;
- severe but difficult to transmit.

These should remain separate properties.

## 4.23.47 Disease Progression

A disease may move through:
- incubation;
- early symptoms;
- established illness;
- severe illness;
- recovery;
- chronic state;
- death.

Not every disease needs all phases.

## 4.23.48 Immune Response

Natural resistance may depend on:
- prior exposure;
- vaccination-equivalent methods;
- general health;
- physiology;
- immunosuppression;
- bloodline traits.

The simulation should abstract immune biology unless it becomes important.

## 4.23.49 Chronic Disease

Some illnesses may become persistent.

Possible long-term effects:
- reduced endurance;
- recurring pain;
- organ damage;
- sensory changes;
- intermittent flare-ups.

These become persistent medical state.

## 4.23.50 Outbreaks

Disease can become a broader world-state issue.

An outbreak may affect:
- villages;
- hospitals;
- missions;
- travel;
- quarantines;
- medical supply demand.

Ruleset 4 records individual disease state.
Large-scale epidemiology belongs to broader world simulation.

## 4.23.51 Quarantine and Isolation

Some diseases may justify:
- isolation;
- travel restriction;
- protective equipment.

These are world and mission responses to transmission risk.

## 4.23.52 Medicine and Drugs

Therapeutic drugs may:
- relieve pain;
- reduce inflammation;
- treat infection;
- suppress symptoms;
- alter consciousness;
- support organs.

Every useful drug may also have:
- dose limits;
- side effects;
- interactions.

## 4.23.53 Sedatives and Stimulants

Drugs may intentionally alter performance.

Sedatives may:
- reduce anxiety;
- impair reaction;
- induce sleep.

Stimulants may:
- increase wakefulness;
- suppress fatigue perception;
- increase strain.

These are condition-altering substances, not free bonuses.

## 4.23.54 Overdose

A therapeutic compound can become harmful at excessive dose.

Overdose should use the same toxin framework:
- substance;
- dose;
- target systems;
- progression;
- treatment.

## 4.23.55 Drug Interactions

Multiple substances may:
- reinforce effects;
- oppose effects;
- create new complications.

Detailed pharmacology should only be generated when relevant.

## 4.23.56 Addiction and Dependence

Some substances may create:
- tolerance;
- physical dependence;
- withdrawal.

These systems should only be used when a specific substance and repeated exposure justify them.

Detailed behavioral addiction systems belong outside Ruleset 4.

## 4.23.57 Disease and Toxin Knowledge

Characters may know:
- common poisons;
- village-specific toxins;
- known antidotes;
- outbreak symptoms.

Rare or secret substances may require specialist knowledge.

## 4.23.58 Lab and Research Diagnosis

Advanced facilities may identify unknown compounds through:
- samples;
- testing;
- experimentation;
- medical ninjutsu;
- reference records.

This can support antidote development.

## 4.23.59 Antidote Development

Creating a new antidote may require:
- identifying toxin mechanism;
- obtaining a sample;
- testing counteragents;
- medical knowledge;
- time.

In urgent cases, development may become a mission objective.

## 4.23.60 Toxin Record

A meaningful toxin should be able to store:
- identity;
- class;
- delivery route;
- effective dose;
- onset;
- target systems;
- symptoms;
- progression rate;
- duration;
- clearance;
- resistance interactions;
- antidotes;
- supportive treatment;
- permanent injury risk;
- detectability.

## 4.23.61 Disease Record

A meaningful disease should be able to store:
- identity;
- transmission route;
- incubation;
- infectious period;
- symptoms;
- progression;
- severity;
- immunity/resistance;
- treatment;
- complications;
- recovery;
- chronic outcomes.

## 4.23.62 Suggested Toxin Pipeline

1. Determine exposure route.
2. Determine effective dose.
3. Apply resistance/protection.
4. Determine onset.
5. Advance toxin progression over time.
6. Generate symptoms and conditions by affected system.
7. Apply deterioration where vital systems are threatened.
8. Diagnose through 4.18.
9. Apply antidote, detoxification, or supportive care.
10. Update remaining toxin burden.
11. Preserve any structural injury caused.

## 4.23.63 Suggested Disease Pipeline

1. Determine exposure and transmission.
2. Resolve whether infection establishes.
3. Advance incubation.
4. Generate symptoms.
5. Progress disease according to physiology and treatment.
6. Track contagiousness where relevant.
7. Apply complications or organ effects.
8. Diagnose and treat.
9. Resolve recovery, chronic state, or death.

## 4.23.64 Compression

Routine minor illness or known poison treatment can be summarized.

The system should zoom in when:
- diagnosis is uncertain;
- antidote is scarce;
- toxin progression competes with travel or combat;
- vital systems are threatened;
- an outbreak changes mission/world state.

## Core Design Rule

Poisons, toxins, and disease should behave as **specific biological processes unfolding over time**.

Their danger should come from:
**dose + delivery + target system + progression + treatment availability**

—not from generic damage-over-time mechanics.
