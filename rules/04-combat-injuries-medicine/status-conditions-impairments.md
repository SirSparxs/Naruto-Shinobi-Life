# 4.14 — Status Conditions & Impairments

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines temporary and semi-temporary impairments that alter combat function without necessarily constituting a persistent structural injury.

Conditions must remain distinct from:
- injuries;
- fatigue;
- chakra depletion;
- medical deterioration;
- purely narrative emotions.

A condition should answer:
**What caused it? What function does it impair? How severe is it? How long does it persist? What worsens or removes it?**

## 4.14.1 Conditions Are Cause-Based

A status condition should not exist as an unexplained game effect.

Examples:
- paralysis from electrical disruption;
- blindness from eye injury, smoke, darkness, or technique;
- burning from active flame;
- restrained from bindings;
- poisoned from toxin exposure;
- disoriented from head impact or sensory overload.

The engine should preserve the actual source.

## 4.14.2 Condition and Injury Can Coexist

A condition may result from an injury or exist independently.

Examples:
- fractured leg creates impaired mobility through injury;
- electrical jutsu creates temporary motor paralysis without fracture;
- smoke creates temporary visual impairment without eye injury;
- poison creates systemic weakness without a wound beyond delivery site.

The system should not convert every impairment into injury.

## 4.14.3 Conditions Should Affect Specific Functions

Avoid:
**Blinded: -20 to all actions.**

Prefer:
- no ordinary visual targeting;
- reduced tracking;
- impaired distance judgment;
- cannot read visible hand seals;
- must rely on hearing, sensing, memory, or other perception.

Each condition should constrain the functions it actually affects.

## 4.14.4 Severity Can Vary

Many conditions should support multiple severities.

Example — visual impairment:
- blurred;
- heavily obscured;
- one eye unavailable;
- effectively blind.

Example — paralysis:
- tingling;
- reduced motor control;
- partial paralysis;
- complete paralysis.

Severity should derive from the cause.

## 4.14.5 Duration Should Derive From Mechanism

Conditions may last:
- only while the source persists;
- until the character leaves an area;
- for a brief physiological recovery period;
- until treated;
- until a technique is broken;
- until a toxin clears;
- permanently if structural injury occurred.

There should be no universal “3 turns” model.

## 4.14.6 Persistent Source Conditions

Some conditions remain only while exposure continues.

Examples:
- obscured vision inside smoke;
- slowed movement in deep mud;
- burning while clothing remains ignited;
- suppression inside a technique field.

Removing the source ends or changes the condition.

## 4.14.7 Residual Conditions

Some effects persist after the original source is gone.

Examples:
- lingering disorientation after explosion;
- temporary numbness after electrical shock;
- smoke inhalation after leaving a fire;
- residual dizziness after spinning or concussion.

Residual duration depends on severity and physiology.

## 4.14.8 Conditions Can Escalate Into Injury

Temporary impairment may become structural damage if exposure continues.

Examples:
- burning becomes tissue burn injury;
- constriction becomes nerve or vascular injury;
- cold numbness becomes tissue damage;
- prolonged oxygen deprivation becomes neurological injury.

The condition system should hand off to 4.10 when actual injury occurs.

## 4.14.9 Conditions Can Also Result From Injury

Examples:
- eye injury causes blurred vision;
- head injury causes disorientation;
- nerve damage causes numbness;
- chest injury causes breathlessness.

In these cases, the condition is a functional expression of the underlying injury.

Treating the symptom does not necessarily heal the cause.

## 4.14.10 Staggered

A brief disruption of posture or action continuity.

Possible effects:
- interrupted action;
- delayed transition;
- reduced immediate defense options;
- temporary opening.

Detailed handling overlaps 4.9 and should remain lightweight.

## 4.14.11 Knocked Down

The character is no longer in a stable standing posture.

Effects derive from actual posture:
- different movement options;
- need to recover footing;
- altered attack angles;
- vulnerability to certain threats.

No universal prone modifier is required.

## 4.14.12 Disoriented

Disorientation may affect:
- awareness;
- direction;
- timing;
- target tracking;
- decision speed;
- balance.

Causes may include:
- head impact;
- blast;
- sensory overload;
- rapid rotation;
- genjutsu;
- environmental effects.

## 4.14.13 Dazed / Stunned

A stronger short-term neurological or sensory disruption may cause:
- delayed responses;
- interrupted concentration;
- reduced ability to process multiple threats;
- inability to immediately initiate complex actions.

Duration and severity depend on cause.

## 4.14.14 Blinded / Visually Impaired

Sources may include:
- eye injury;
- darkness;
- smoke;
- flash;
- obstruction;
- technique.

The engine should distinguish:
- one-eye impairment;
- blurred vision;
- blocked line of sight;
- complete ordinary visual loss.

Other senses may compensate.

## 4.14.15 Deafened / Hearing Impairment

Effects may include:
- reduced directional awareness;
- missed verbal communication;
- inability to detect sound-based cues;
- impaired balance if inner-ear function is involved.

Sensory techniques may compensate where appropriate.

## 4.14.16 Numbness

Numbness may result from:
- nerve compression;
- electrical exposure;
- cold;
- toxin;
- injury.

Possible effects:
- reduced tactile feedback;
- poor grip regulation;
- missed pain warning;
- reduced fine motor control.

Numbness can be dangerous because the character may unknowingly worsen an injury.

## 4.14.17 Weakness

Temporary weakness may result from:
- fatigue;
- toxin;
- blood loss;
- neurological disruption;
- technique.

It should identify the affected region or whether the weakness is systemic.

## 4.14.18 Paralysis

Paralysis may be:
- localized;
- partial;
- complete;
- transient;
- persistent.

Possible causes:
- nerve injury;
- electricity;
- toxin;
- chakra technique;
- spinal damage.

The condition should specify what movement remains possible.

## 4.14.19 Tremor / Loss of Fine Control

Trembling may impair:
- hand seals;
- medical procedures;
- precise weapon work;
- puppet control;
- delicate tool use.

Causes may include:
- exhaustion;
- nerve disruption;
- fear;
- toxin;
- cold;
- electrical aftereffects.

## 4.14.20 Burning

“Burning” means an active heat source remains on or around the character.

Possible effects:
- continuing thermal exposure;
- pain;
- distraction;
- clothing/equipment damage;
- spreading flame.

The condition ends when the active burning source is extinguished, removed, or consumed.

Any resulting tissue injury remains.

## 4.14.21 Chilled / Frozen

Cold conditions may cause:
- reduced dexterity;
- slowed movement;
- numbness;
- stiffness.

Severe or prolonged cold can create actual injury.

A character physically immobilized by ice is also restrained, not merely “chilled.”

## 4.14.22 Electrically Disrupted

Electrical exposure may create temporary:
- muscle lock;
- tremor;
- numbness;
- slowed motor response;
- paralysis;
- sensory disruption.

The exact state depends on the technique and exposure.

## 4.14.23 Restrained

A restrained character has movement limited by an external constraint.

Sources include:
- rope;
- wire;
- chains;
- grappling;
- earth;
- ice;
- sealing techniques;
- shadow-based restraint;
- other jutsu.

The condition should specify:
- which body parts are restricted;
- how much movement remains;
- what actions can break or escape it.

## 4.14.24 Pinned

Pinned is a stronger positional restraint where:
- movement is severely limited;
- leverage is controlled by another actor or object.

A pinned character may still:
- speak;
- use free limbs;
- use techniques compatible with the position.

## 4.14.25 Grappled

Grappled means another combatant has established direct physical control.

Effects depend on:
- grip location;
- leverage;
- relative strength;
- body position;
- available limbs.

Detailed mechanics belong to 4.15.

## 4.14.26 Immobilized

Immobilized means meaningful movement is currently prevented.

This can result from:
- restraint;
- terrain;
- paralysis;
- technique;
- crushing debris.

The source determines escape options.

## 4.14.27 Slowed

“Slowed” should not be a generic speed debuff.

It should result from a specific constraint such as:
- mud;
- injury;
- ice;
- fatigue;
- binding;
- technique.

The condition should explain which movements are limited and why.

## 4.14.28 Rooted

Some effects may prevent translation while still allowing:
- turning;
- attacking;
- hand seals;
- defense.

This is distinct from full immobilization.

## 4.14.29 Poisoned

“Poisoned” is too broad to be a complete condition by itself.

The toxin record should define actual effects such as:
- weakness;
- paralysis;
- nausea;
- pain;
- sensory disruption;
- bleeding;
- respiratory failure.

Detailed poison rules belong to 4.22.

## 4.14.30 Nauseated / Sickened

Systemic illness or toxin effects may cause:
- reduced concentration;
- vomiting;
- poor balance;
- inability to sustain exertion.

This should only exist when supported by an actual cause.

## 4.14.31 Breathless / Respiratory Distress

Temporary breathing limitation may result from:
- exertion;
- smoke;
- chest trauma;
- choking;
- technique.

Effects may include:
- reduced sustained action;
- slower recovery;
- impaired concentration.

Persistent or worsening respiratory compromise belongs to 4.11.

## 4.14.32 Silenced / Unable to Vocalize

Some effects may prevent:
- speech;
- verbal coordination;
- vocalized technique requirements if any.

The cause might be:
- gag;
- airway injury;
- technique;
- restraint.

This should not affect nonverbal hand-seal use unless the source also restricts it.

## 4.14.33 Hand-Seal Impairment

A character may be unable or less able to form seals because of:
- hand injury;
- restraint;
- numbness;
- paralysis;
- tremor;
- occupied hands.

The effect should specify:
- one hand unavailable;
- reduced speed;
- reduced precision;
- complete inability.

Ruleset 3 determines which techniques actually require seals.

## 4.14.34 Chakra-Flow Impairment

A condition may temporarily interfere with:
- chakra circulation;
- molding;
- output;
- nature transformation.

Causes may include:
- tenketsu disruption;
- sealing effects;
- poisons;
- specialized jutsu.

This should remain distinct from having low chakra reserves.

## 4.14.35 Concentration Disruption

Some conditions may reduce the ability to maintain:
- sustained jutsu;
- medical techniques;
- sensory techniques;
- precise chakra control.

Sources may include:
- pain;
- disorientation;
- sensory overload;
- active interference.

## 4.14.36 Fear and Panic

Fear should not be a generic unavoidable debuff.

It becomes mechanically relevant only when:
- the character experiences a credible fear response;
- it interferes with decision-making or action.

Possible effects:
- hesitation;
- tunnel vision;
- retreat behavior;
- poor coordination.

Willpower, experience, context, and personality matter.

Fear does not belong on every intimidating encounter by default.

## 4.14.37 Mental Conditions From Genjutsu

Genjutsu-specific conditions should be defined by Ruleset 3.

Ruleset 4 tracks their functional combat consequences, such as:
- false sensory information;
- paralysis;
- disorientation;
- perceived terrain;
- impaired targeting.

Do not create a separate generic genjutsu status system here.

## 4.14.38 Marked / Tracked

A character may be temporarily trackable because of:
- scent;
- chakra mark;
- visible dye;
- sealing mark;
- embedded object.

This is primarily an information condition rather than a bodily impairment.

## 4.14.39 Exposed / Revealed

A hidden character may lose concealment.

This is a tactical state, not a medical condition.

It should be tracked when it affects:
- targeting;
- awareness;
- pursuit.

## 4.14.40 Ongoing Damage Conditions Must Name the Mechanism

Avoid:
**Damage over time: 5 per turn.**

Prefer:
- active burning;
- bleeding;
- poison progression;
- corrosive exposure;
- ongoing electrical field.

Each process follows its own deterioration rules.

## 4.14.41 Conditions Can Interact

Examples:
- blinded + surrounded;
- restrained + burning;
- fatigued + poisoned;
- numb hand + hand injury;
- smoke + hearing loss.

Interaction should come from overlapping functional restrictions rather than additive status penalties.

## 4.14.42 Redundant Conditions Should Not Stack Meaninglessly

If two effects both completely prevent ordinary vision, applying a third visual-obscuration effect adds little.

The system should recognize redundancy.

## 4.14.43 One Condition Can Mask Another

Examples:
- numbness masks pain;
- adrenaline masks fatigue;
- genjutsu masks visible injury;
- smoke hides bleeding.

This can create information errors without removing the underlying state.

## 4.14.44 Removing a Condition Does Not Necessarily Remove Its Consequences

Extinguishing fire removes the Burning condition.
It does not remove burn injuries.

Freeing a restrained arm removes restraint.
It does not heal the dislocated shoulder caused during capture.

Breaking a genjutsu removes the false perception.
It does not undo injuries suffered while affected.

## 4.14.45 Condition Removal Should Be Specific

Possible removal methods include:
- leave the hazard;
- break restraint;
- receive treatment;
- wait for physiological recovery;
- dispel technique;
- counter toxin;
- restore circulation;
- extinguish fire.

Avoid generic “cleanse all debuffs” effects unless a specific ability explicitly has that power.

## 4.14.46 Condition Resistance

Characters may resist or reduce conditions through:
- attributes;
- training;
- equipment;
- physiology;
- chakra control;
- specific techniques.

Resistance should affect the actual mechanism.

Example:
- insulation reduces electrical exposure;
- respiratory training helps with smoke tolerance;
- sensory techniques compensate for visual loss.

## 4.14.47 Condition Immunity Must Be Explicit

No character should gain broad immunity simply from rank.

Immunity should come from:
- physiology;
- equipment;
- technique;
- environment;
- explicit special ability.

## 4.14.48 Condition Information Is Per-Observer

The engine may know:
- exact toxin effect;
- exact paralysis severity;
- hidden chakra-flow disruption.

Observers may only notice:
- trembling;
- slow movement;
- confusion;
- inability to form seals.

Ruleset 2 governs what is correctly identified.

## 4.14.49 Condition Record

A meaningful condition should be able to store:
- name;
- source;
- affected function/body region;
- severity;
- onset;
- duration model;
- progression;
- removal conditions;
- functional consequences;
- whether it can escalate into injury;
- information visibility.

## 4.14.50 Suggested Condition Pipeline

1. Identify source.
2. Determine whether exposure or resolution applies the condition.
3. Determine affected function.
4. Determine severity.
5. Apply immediate functional restrictions.
6. Determine duration/progression model.
7. Track interaction with injuries, fatigue, chakra, and environment.
8. Update condition when the source changes.
9. Remove or transform condition when appropriate.
10. Preserve any lasting injury created by the condition.

## 4.14.51 Compression

For low-detail combat, conditions may be summarized as:
- briefly stunned;
- restrained;
- blinded by smoke;
- partially paralyzed;
- poisoned.

If the condition becomes decisive, the simulation should expand the actual functional consequences.

## Core Design Rule

A status condition should never be merely a label with a modifier.

It should represent:
**a specific cause producing a specific temporary impairment with a specific way to persist, worsen, or end.**
