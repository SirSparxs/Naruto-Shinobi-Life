# 4.21 — Surgery & Advanced Medical Care

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines definitive treatment that generally requires a controlled environment, specialized tools, advanced medical knowledge, medical ninjutsu, surgery, or coordinated teams.

Advanced care should be able to:
- repair injuries that field medicine can only stabilize;
- perform invasive treatment;
- restore damaged anatomy;
- manage complex internal injuries;
- replace blood or tissue;
- perform transplantation;
- fit or integrate prosthetics;
- manage complications;
- support prolonged recovery.

It should not eliminate the importance of timing, anatomy, compatibility, or prior damage.

## 4.21.1 Definitive Care Is Different From Field Care

Field medicine primarily:
- preserves life;
- slows deterioration;
- enables transport.

Definitive care attempts to:
- repair underlying structures;
- remove sources of ongoing danger;
- restore long-term function;
- prevent complications;
- optimize recovery.

A patient may arrive stabilized while still requiring extensive treatment.

## 4.21.2 Advanced Care Requires Conditions

Complex treatment may require:
- safe environment;
- clean workspace;
- lighting;
- tools;
- medical supplies;
- blood or fluid support;
- monitoring;
- skilled staff;
- medical chakra;
- time.

Lack of these conditions can reduce what is safely possible.

## 4.21.3 Medical Facilities Have Capability Levels

Not every clinic can perform the same procedures.

Useful broad facility levels may include:

### Basic Clinic
Routine wounds, fractures, medicines, observation.

### Field Hospital
Emergency surgery, blood replacement, moderate trauma care.

### Major Hospital
Advanced surgery, intensive monitoring, specialist teams, complex medical ninjutsu.

### Specialist / Research Facility
Transplants, advanced prosthetics, bloodline-related procedures, experimental treatment.

Capability should come from staff, equipment, supplies, and knowledge rather than a generic building tier alone.

## 4.21.4 Surgery Is Controlled Invasive Treatment

Surgery may be required when treatment needs direct access to:
- internal bleeding;
- damaged organs;
- foreign objects;
- shattered bones;
- severed tendons;
- nerves;
- major vessels;
- internal contamination.

Medical ninjutsu may reduce invasiveness without making all surgery unnecessary.

## 4.21.5 Surgical Access Has Cost

Opening the body creates additional:
- tissue trauma;
- bleeding risk;
- infection risk;
- recovery burden.

A procedure should therefore use the least invasive approach capable of achieving the objective.

## 4.21.6 Surgical Planning

Before a complex procedure, the medical team should ideally determine:
- diagnosis;
- treatment objective;
- access route;
- major risks;
- required specialists;
- expected duration;
- blood/supply needs;
- contingency plans.

Emergency surgery may proceed with incomplete planning when delay is more dangerous.

## 4.21.7 Anesthesia and Sedation

Some procedures may require:
- analgesia;
- sedation;
- unconsciousness;
- paralysis or immobilization.

Methods may include:
- medication;
- chakra techniques;
- genjutsu-like medical methods where appropriate;
- physical restraint in emergencies.

These methods have their own risks and should not be assumed automatic.

## 4.21.8 Patient Monitoring

During advanced treatment, the team may monitor:
- consciousness;
- circulation;
- breathing;
- blood loss;
- temperature;
- chakra flow;
- neurological state;
- treatment response.

Monitoring can detect complications before they become catastrophic.

## 4.21.9 Hemorrhage Control During Surgery

Major surgery may require active control of:
- vessels;
- organ bleeding;
- diffuse tissue bleeding.

Medical ninjutsu may greatly improve precision and speed.

Uncontrolled operative bleeding can itself become a deterioration process.

## 4.21.10 Blood Replacement

Severe blood loss may require replacement through:
- stored blood;
- donor blood;
- setting-appropriate substitutes;
- chakra-supported circulatory methods.

Replacement restores circulating volume or oxygen-carrying capacity.
It does not repair the wound that caused the loss.

## 4.21.11 Blood Compatibility

Transfusion should consider:
- compatible blood;
- unusual physiology;
- bloodline traits;
- prior reactions;
- contamination.

The setting may possess improved compatibility testing, but incompatible transfusion should not be ignored unless a specific technique bypasses it.

## 4.21.12 Organ Repair

Definitive organ treatment may include:
- direct repair;
- removal of damaged tissue;
- vascular reconstruction;
- drainage;
- medical regeneration;
- transplantation.

The required method depends on the organ and degree of destruction.

## 4.21.13 Organ Removal

A badly damaged organ may sometimes need to be removed if:
- it cannot be safely repaired;
- it is causing uncontrolled bleeding;
- dead tissue threatens the patient.

Survival afterward depends on whether the organ is:
- essential;
- replaceable;
- partially redundant;
- supported by another technique.

## 4.21.14 Organ Transplantation

Transplantation may replace:
- organs;
- eyes;
- tissue;
- specialized biological structures.

A transplant requires:
- compatible anatomy;
- blood supply;
- viable donor material;
- surgical expertise;
- postoperative management.

Naruto-specific techniques may improve success without eliminating these principles.

## 4.21.15 Transplant Compatibility

Compatibility may depend on:
- ordinary biology;
- chakra system compatibility;
- bloodline traits;
- immune response;
- donor-recipient relationship;
- artificial suppression methods.

Exceptional compatibility must be explained by the relevant technique or physiology.

## 4.21.16 Rejection and Integration

A transplanted structure may:
- integrate normally;
- function imperfectly;
- trigger rejection;
- disrupt chakra flow;
- require medication or chakra management.

These outcomes may be reduced by advanced medical ninjutsu.

## 4.21.17 Dōjutsu Transplants

Eye transplantation may involve additional issues:
- chakra demand;
- bloodline compatibility;
- neural integration;
- ability activation;
- inability to deactivate;
- long-term strain.

Specific dōjutsu rules belong to Ruleset 3 and relevant bloodline systems.
Ruleset 4.21 handles the medical procedure and biological integration.

## 4.21.18 Limb Salvage

A severely injured limb may sometimes be preserved through:
- vascular repair;
- bone reconstruction;
- nerve repair;
- tendon reconstruction;
- regeneration.

Whether salvage is possible depends on:
- tissue viability;
- elapsed time;
- contamination;
- treatment capability.

## 4.21.19 Amputation

Amputation may be required when a limb is:
- irreversibly destroyed;
- causing uncontrolled systemic danger;
- unsalvageable with available care.

Amputation is a definitive injury outcome, not automatic death or end of character function.

## 4.21.20 Prosthetics

Prosthetics may restore some or substantial function.

Possible types include:
- ordinary mechanical prosthetic;
- advanced shinobi prosthetic;
- chakra-responsive prosthetic;
- biological replacement;
- experimental research technology.

Each should define:
- mobility/function;
- strength;
- sensory feedback;
- chakra interaction;
- maintenance;
- failure modes.

## 4.21.21 Prosthetic Adaptation

Receiving a prosthetic does not guarantee immediate mastery.

The character may require:
- fitting;
- rehabilitation;
- motor retraining;
- chakra-control retraining;
- technique adaptation.

Advanced users may eventually exceed ordinary replacement-level function if the prosthetic is designed for it.

## 4.21.22 Bone Reconstruction

Advanced treatment may require:
- alignment;
- fixation;
- grafting;
- chakra-assisted regeneration;
- reconstruction of shattered sections.

Poorly restored alignment can create chronic weakness or limited movement.

## 4.21.23 Nerve Reconstruction

Advanced nerve repair may attempt to restore:
- motor function;
- sensation;
- fine control.

Even successful reconstruction may require long recovery and rehabilitation.

## 4.21.24 Vascular Reconstruction

Major vessel repair may require:
- direct repair;
- grafting;
- replacement;
- chakra-assisted reconstruction.

Successful repair stabilizes circulation but does not automatically restore lost blood.

## 4.21.25 Complex Chakra-Network Surgery

Some injuries may require direct repair of:
- tenketsu;
- major chakra pathways;
- implanted chakra structures;
- damaged seals interacting with the body.

This may require specialists with both:
- advanced medical skill;
- advanced chakra-network knowledge.

## 4.21.26 Foreign Chakra / Implant Management

Advanced care may need to address:
- transplanted chakra-bearing tissue;
- implanted seals;
- cursed biological material;
- artificial organs;
- foreign chakra signatures.

Treatment may involve both medical and sealing expertise.

## 4.21.27 Medical Teams

Complex surgery may involve multiple roles:
- lead surgeon;
- medical-nin tissue specialist;
- chakra-network specialist;
- anesthesia/sedation support;
- circulation support;
- diagnostic specialist;
- assistants.

The team may accomplish procedures beyond one person's safe limits.

## 4.21.28 Team Coordination

Medical teamwork can improve:
- speed;
- monitoring;
- division of labor;
- fatigue management;
- complication response.

Poor coordination can:
- duplicate effort;
- interrupt delicate procedures;
- create conflicting chakra manipulation.

## 4.21.29 Medical Fatigue

Long procedures may physically and mentally fatigue practitioners.

Medical fatigue can impair:
- precision;
- concentration;
- Chakra Control;
- judgment.

Complex procedures may require rotating staff.

## 4.21.30 Chakra Supply During Surgery

High-level procedures may consume substantial chakra.

A team may use:
- multiple medics;
- chakra transfer;
- stored chakra;
- support techniques.

Running out of chakra mid-procedure can be dangerous depending on what remains incomplete.

## 4.21.31 Complications

Possible complications include:
- renewed bleeding;
- infection;
- organ failure;
- poor wound healing;
- nerve damage;
- clotting problems;
- transplant rejection;
- chakra-network instability;
- anesthesia reaction;
- scarring;
- structural failure.

Complications should arise from plausible risk, not arbitrary drama.

## 4.21.32 Infection Control

Advanced facilities may reduce infection risk through:
- sterile technique;
- cleaning;
- antibiotics or setting-equivalent medication;
- medical ninjutsu;
- isolation.

Contaminated wounds and emergency surgery may still carry greater risk.

## 4.21.33 Postoperative Instability

A successful procedure may leave the patient:
- unconscious;
- weak;
- medically fragile;
- at risk of renewed bleeding;
- unable to use the repaired region.

Procedure success does not equal immediate recovery.

## 4.21.34 Intensive Monitoring

Critical patients may require prolonged observation for:
- breathing;
- circulation;
- neurological changes;
- bleeding;
- transplant function;
- chakra-network instability.

This can prevent a “successful surgery” from becoming a delayed fatality.

## 4.21.35 Revision Surgery

A patient may need additional procedures if:
- initial repair fails;
- complications develop;
- tissue dies;
- prosthetic fit is poor;
- infection develops.

Treatment can therefore be multi-stage.

## 4.21.36 Elective Versus Emergency Procedures

Emergency surgery prioritizes:
- survival;
- speed;
- damage control.

Elective or delayed reconstruction can prioritize:
- optimal function;
- reduced scarring;
- precise repair;
- prosthetic integration.

A rushed lifesaving procedure may intentionally defer perfect reconstruction.

## 4.21.37 Damage-Control Surgery

A critically unstable patient may receive limited surgery focused on:
- stopping bleeding;
- controlling contamination;
- preventing immediate failure.

Definitive reconstruction may occur later after stabilization.

This separates:
**survive now**
from:
**repair fully later.**

## 4.21.38 Scar and Cosmetic Reconstruction

Advanced medicine may reduce:
- disfiguring scars;
- contractures;
- tissue deformity.

Cosmetic reconstruction may matter socially or psychologically even when function is already restored.

## 4.21.39 Rehabilitation Begins Before Full Healing

Advanced care may begin:
- movement retraining;
- strength restoration;
- joint mobility;
- chakra-control exercises

before the injury is fully healed.

The exact recovery system belongs to 4.22.

## 4.21.40 Facility Limits Matter

A remote clinic may be unable to perform:
- transplant;
- complex nerve repair;
- experimental bloodline treatment.

The patient may need transfer to a major center.

This makes geography and infrastructure meaningful.

## 4.21.41 Specialist Availability Matters

A hospital may possess the equipment but lack:
- a chakra-network specialist;
- transplant surgeon;
- toxicologist;
- dōjutsu specialist.

Access to rare expertise can become a mission or political resource.

## 4.21.42 Experimental Treatment

Research facilities may attempt procedures with:
- incomplete evidence;
- high complication risk;
- uncertain long-term consequences.

Experimental care should clearly distinguish known treatment from speculative intervention.

## 4.21.43 Treatment Consent

When circumstances allow, patients may:
- accept;
- refuse;
- choose between risky alternatives.

Emergency incapacity may force others to make decisions according to setting, relationships, law, or mission authority.

Detailed social/legal rules belong elsewhere.

## 4.21.44 Definitive Treatment Can Fail

Failure may result from:
- incorrect diagnosis;
- technical error;
- overwhelming injury;
- insufficient time;
- inadequate chakra;
- incompatibility;
- complication;
- facility limits.

Ruleset 2 handles genuine uncertainty.

## 4.21.45 Procedure Success Can Be Partial

A surgery may:
- save life but fail to preserve limb;
- restore circulation but not nerve function;
- repair organ damage but leave chronic impairment;
- integrate a transplant with reduced function.

Partial success should be common in complex cases.

## 4.21.46 Exceptional Success Remains Within Capability

An exceptional result may produce:
- cleaner repair;
- reduced blood loss;
- shorter procedure;
- less scarring;
- better functional preservation.

It should not make an impossible transplant viable without a mechanism supporting compatibility.

## 4.21.47 Definitive Care Record

A major procedure should be able to store:
- diagnosis;
- procedure;
- facility;
- practitioners;
- start/end time;
- medical techniques used;
- chakra/supplies consumed;
- complications;
- structures repaired or removed;
- implants/transplants;
- immediate outcome;
- postoperative restrictions;
- recovery plan.

## 4.21.48 Suggested Advanced-Care Pipeline

1. Confirm or refine diagnosis.
2. Determine definitive treatment goal.
3. Check facility, specialists, supplies, and compatibility.
4. Stabilize patient enough for procedure.
5. Plan access and major risk points.
6. Begin surgery or advanced treatment.
7. Monitor blood loss, circulation, breathing, chakra, and complications.
8. Ruleset 2 resolves uncertain procedural steps.
9. Repair, remove, replace, or stabilize structures as appropriate.
10. Close or complete treatment.
11. Reassess patient.
12. Record residual injuries, complications, and postoperative restrictions.
13. Transition to recovery and rehabilitation.

## 4.21.49 Compression

Routine definitive care may be summarized when:
- treatment is standard;
- expert capability greatly exceeds difficulty;
- complications are unlikely.

The simulation should zoom in when:
- survival is uncertain;
- rare specialists are required;
- transplant or experimental procedures occur;
- permanent disability is at stake;
- resources are scarce.

## Core Design Rule

Advanced medical care should be where the system can truly repair, reconstruct, replace, or integrate damaged anatomy.

Its power should come from:
**specialist knowledge + controlled conditions + medical ninjutsu + equipment + team coordination + time**

—not from simply declaring severe injuries healed once the patient reaches a hospital.
