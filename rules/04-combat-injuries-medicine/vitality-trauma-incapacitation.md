# 4.9 — Vitality, Trauma & Incapacitation

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines how injuries, physical stress, pain, neurological disruption, fatigue, and shock affect a character's immediate ability to keep functioning in combat.

It must remain distinct from both the injury system and medical severity.

A character can be:
- badly injured but still combat-capable;
- temporarily incapacitated without being critically injured;
- medically unstable while still conscious;
- conscious and medically stable but too exhausted or impaired to continue effectively.

## 4.9.1 Do Not Replace HP With a Renamed HP Bar

The system should not create a universal "Vitality" pool that functions exactly like hit points under another name.

Combat capacity should be derived from the character's current state.

Relevant contributors include:
- consciousness;
- neurological function;
- balance;
- motor control;
- pain;
- structural integrity;
- breathing;
- circulation;
- fatigue;
- chakra exhaustion;
- injury-specific impairment;
- psychological determination where relevant.

The engine may maintain hidden aggregate estimates for compression, but detailed combat should preserve the underlying causes.

## 4.9.2 Combat Capacity and Medical Severity Are Separate Axes

Combat capacity answers:

**Can this character meaningfully continue acting right now?**

Medical severity answers:

**How dangerous is this condition to the character's health or survival?**

These can diverge.

Examples:
- concussion causing immediate unconsciousness but limited long-term danger;
- serious internal bleeding while the character remains mobile;
- broken hand that greatly limits combat but is not life-threatening;
- severe exhaustion that stops effective combat without major injury.

## 4.9.3 Functional Capacity Is Multi-Dimensional

The engine should evaluate important functional domains separately when needed:

### Consciousness
Can the character remain awake and responsive?

### Cognition
Can they understand the situation, make decisions, and maintain technique control?

### Motor Control
Can they move accurately and coordinate limbs?

### Balance
Can they stand, land, turn, and orient normally?

### Structural Function
Can damaged limbs or body regions bear force and perform required actions?

### Respiratory Function
Can they breathe adequately for current exertion?

### Circulatory Stability
Is blood flow adequate enough to sustain activity?

### Pain Burden
Is pain interfering with action, concentration, or willingness to continue?

### Fatigue / Exertion
Can the character sustain further activity?

Not every encounter needs every domain explicitly tracked.

## 4.9.4 Incapacitation Is a Result, Not a Single Condition

A character may become unable to continue for many different reasons.

Possible routes include:
- unconsciousness;
- severe motor impairment;
- inability to stand;
- inability to breathe adequately;
- circulatory collapse;
- overwhelming fatigue;
- extreme pain;
- paralysis;
- restraint;
- loss of critical sensory function;
- catastrophic structural injury;
- voluntary surrender.

The simulation should preserve the actual cause.

## 4.9.5 Stagger

A stagger is a short disruption of posture, orientation, or action continuity.

Possible causes:
- blunt impact;
- sudden pain;
- disrupted footing;
- explosion;
- partial concussion;
- heavy block;
- rapid forced movement.

A stagger may:
- interrupt an action;
- delay the next action;
- create an opening;
- reduce defensive options;
- force repositioning.

A stagger does not automatically imply significant injury.

## 4.9.6 Knockdown

A knockdown means the character loses their standing or stable fighting position.

Causes may include:
- sweep;
- impact;
- balance loss;
- terrain failure;
- collision;
- neurological disruption.

A knocked-down character may still be fully conscious and capable of defending.

The tactical consequence comes from posture and recovery time, not from an automatic generic penalty.

## 4.9.7 Stun / Acute Disorientation

A stun is a temporary period of impaired perception, cognition, or coordinated response.

Possible causes:
- head impact;
- blast;
- sensory overload;
- electrical disruption;
- technique-specific effects.

A stunned character may:
- react late;
- lose track of threats;
- fail to maintain a technique;
- become vulnerable to follow-up pressure.

"Stun" should not become a universal fixed-duration status. Duration depends on cause and severity.

## 4.9.8 Unconsciousness

Unconsciousness can result from:
- neurological trauma;
- oxygen deprivation;
- circulatory compromise;
- technique-specific effects;
- extreme systemic collapse.

Unconsciousness is not synonymous with dying.

Likewise, remaining conscious is not proof that a character is medically safe.

## 4.9.9 Pain

Pain should influence combat when it is intense enough to matter.

Pain may:
- interrupt concentration;
- discourage use of an injured limb;
- slow reactions;
- make precise actions more difficult;
- contribute to surrender or hesitation;
- reveal an injury.

Pain tolerance, training, adrenaline, drugs, and unusual physiology may change functional effect without removing the underlying injury.

## 4.9.10 Pain Suppression Does Not Heal Injury

A character may ignore, suppress, or fail to feel pain.

This can preserve short-term combat capacity while increasing risk of:
- worsening injury;
- overusing damaged tissue;
- missing signs of deterioration.

Pain and structural damage must remain separate.

## 4.9.11 Shock as Systemic Deterioration

The system should distinguish ordinary surprise or emotional shock from physiological shock.

Physiological shock may result from:
- severe blood loss;
- major trauma;
- burns;
- other systemic injury.

Possible effects include:
- weakness;
- altered cognition;
- reduced coordination;
- collapse;
- worsening medical instability.

Detailed progression belongs to later deterioration rules.

## 4.9.12 Blood Loss Affects Function Before Death

Significant bleeding can progressively reduce:
- endurance;
- strength;
- cognition;
- balance;
- reaction quality;
- consciousness.

The system should not wait until a binary "bleed out" event to represent consequences.

## 4.9.13 Breathing Limitation

Breathing impairment may come from:
- chest injury;
- airway injury;
- smoke;
- drowning;
- constriction;
- exhaustion;
- technique effects.

Reduced respiratory function should degrade sustained activity and may eventually threaten consciousness.

## 4.9.14 Structural Failure

Some injuries directly remove specific actions.

Examples:
- a leg cannot bear weight;
- fingers cannot form seals;
- shoulder cannot generate force;
- fractured ribs make deep exertion difficult.

These restrictions should come from the injury record rather than generic health thresholds.

## 4.9.15 Neurological Impairment

Neurological effects may impair:
- consciousness;
- balance;
- sensation;
- coordination;
- limb control;
- fine motor function;
- perception.

This is especially relevant to:
- head trauma;
- nerve injury;
- electrical attacks;
- chakra-network effects;
- certain specialized techniques.

## 4.9.16 Sensory Incapacitation

Loss of vision, hearing, or other combat-relevant senses can severely reduce effective combat capacity without physically preventing movement.

A blinded fighter may still act.
They simply operate with dramatically reduced information unless compensated by another sense or technique.

## 4.9.17 Temporary Incapacitation Versus Persistent Incapacitation

Some forms of incapacity resolve quickly.

Examples:
- brief stun;
- knockdown;
- transient disorientation.

Others persist until the underlying cause changes.

Examples:
- broken weight-bearing leg;
- severe blood loss;
- unconsciousness from major trauma;
- restraint.

The engine should not use one universal "incapacitated for X turns" rule.

## 4.9.18 Combat Capacity Can Recover During a Fight

A character may regain function by:
- recovering balance;
- catching breath;
- ending a painful action;
- receiving brief medical stabilization;
- changing stance;
- freeing a restrained limb;
- ending a disruptive technique.

Short-term recovery should be possible when the limiting factor is reversible.

## 4.9.19 Recovery Does Not Remove Injury

A character who recovers from being staggered after a broken rib still has the broken rib.

Similarly, someone who regains consciousness after a head impact may still require medical assessment.

Temporary functional recovery and medical healing are separate processes.

## 4.9.20 Adrenaline and Determination

High stress may temporarily allow characters to function despite pain, fatigue, or injury.

This should not grant magical structural integrity.

Determination may help a character continue despite:
- pain;
- fear;
- exhaustion;
- discomfort.

It should not let a severed tendon work normally or replace oxygen.

Willpower therefore affects tolerance and persistence, not impossible anatomy.

## 4.9.21 Pushing Beyond Safe Limits

A character may intentionally continue acting despite serious impairment.

Doing so may:
- worsen injuries;
- accelerate fatigue;
- increase bleeding;
- increase collapse risk;
- reduce precision.

This creates meaningful risk when a mission objective is more important than personal safety.

## 4.9.22 Automatic Collapse Should Have a Cause

The system should avoid arbitrary "you reached 0 vitality and collapse" outcomes.

Collapse should normally be traceable to:
- neurological disruption;
- insufficient circulation;
- respiratory failure;
- overwhelming fatigue;
- structural inability;
- technique effect;
- extreme systemic stress.

The narration should be able to explain why the character can no longer continue.

## 4.9.23 Functional Thresholds Can Be Hidden

The engine may use hidden thresholds to determine when cumulative stress produces:
- shaky performance;
- severe impairment;
- collapse;
- unconsciousness.

These thresholds should be derived from the character's attributes, conditioning, injuries, physiology, and current state.

They are implementation tools, not visible HP.

## 4.9.24 Endurance

Endurance should influence:
- tolerance for sustained exertion;
- resistance to fatigue;
- ability to remain functional under physical stress;
- recovery from short-term exertion;
- tolerance of pain and shock within plausible limits.

Endurance should not make critical anatomy invulnerable.

## 4.9.25 Willpower

Willpower may influence:
- continuing despite pain;
- resisting panic;
- maintaining focus;
- refusing surrender;
- pushing through fatigue.

Willpower does not repair tissue or restore blood volume.

It changes how long a character can continue to act effectively despite adverse sensations and stress.

## 4.9.26 Agility and Perception Under Impairment

Loss of balance, dizziness, sensory injury, and neurological disruption may reduce the effective use of Agility and Perception even when the base attributes remain unchanged.

The character has not permanently lost the attribute.
Their current condition limits access to it.

## 4.9.27 Temporary Capacity States

For practical simulation, the engine may summarize current combat capacity with descriptive states such as:

### Unimpaired
No meaningful functional limitation.

### Strained
Some fatigue, pain, or minor impairment is present but full action remains practical.

### Compromised
Meaningful limitations are affecting available actions, precision, tempo, or endurance.

### Severely Impaired
The character can still act but only with major restrictions or escalating risk.

### Incapacitated
The character cannot meaningfully continue ordinary combat.

### Unconscious
The character is not awake or responsive enough to fight.

These states summarize function; they are not medical severity grades.

## 4.9.28 Capacity States Should Be Derived

A character should not become "Compromised" because a meter crossed an arbitrary threshold without explanation.

The state should summarize actual causes such as:
- injured leg;
- moderate blood loss;
- severe fatigue;
- blurred vision;
- broken hand;
- intense pain.

## 4.9.29 Medical Severity Uses a Separate Scale

Later injury and medical sections may classify conditions by danger or urgency.

A character can therefore be:

**Combat Capacity: Compromised**
**Medical Severity: Critical**

or:

**Combat Capacity: Incapacitated**
**Medical Severity: Moderate**

This separation is intentional.

## 4.9.30 Incapacitation Does Not Automatically End Threat

An incapacitated character may still:
- communicate;
- crawl;
- trigger a prepared device;
- perform a very limited action;
- remain dangerous through maintained techniques;
- be rescued;
- recover.

The exact possibilities depend on why they are incapacitated.

## 4.9.31 Unconscious Characters May Have Ongoing Effects

An unconscious shinobi may still have:
- active environmental effects;
- autonomous summons;
- traps;
- previously launched attacks;
- persistent barriers;
- clones depending on technique rules.

Incapacitation affects the character's actions, not necessarily every consequence they previously created.

## 4.9.32 Capture and Restraint

A physically healthy character can be combat-incapacitated through:
- bindings;
- seals;
- paralysis;
- immobilization;
- confinement.

Therefore, "incapacitated" must remain broader than "injured."

## 4.9.33 Surrender

Voluntary surrender should also end active resistance without implying medical incapacity.

A character may surrender because:
- continuing is strategically pointless;
- allies are threatened;
- capture is preferable to death;
- mission objectives changed.

The system should record surrender separately from physical incapacity.

## 4.9.34 Combat Capacity in Compression

For minor/background encounters, the engine may use the descriptive capacity states directly.

Example:
- bandit becomes Compromised after repeated blunt hits;
- one additional clean strike produces Incapacitated.

If the injury becomes important, the simulation can expand the underlying causes.

## 4.9.35 Minimum Functional State

When relevant, the engine should be able to track:
- consciousness;
- balance;
- major motor limitations;
- breathing limitations;
- circulatory instability;
- pain burden;
- fatigue;
- sensory impairment;
- structural restrictions;
- temporary neurological impairment;
- overall combat-capacity summary.

## Core Design Rule

Combat capacity should answer:

**What can this character still physically and mentally do right now, and why?**

It should never become a disguised health bar.

A character stops fighting because a real functional system fails, becomes too impaired, or they choose to stop—not because an abstract number reached zero.
