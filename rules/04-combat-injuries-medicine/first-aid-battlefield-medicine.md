# 4.19 — First Aid & Battlefield Medicine

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines emergency and field treatment performed before definitive medical care.

Battlefield medicine should primarily:
- stop immediate life-threatening processes;
- prevent worsening;
- preserve function;
- support transport;
- buy time;
- reduce avoidable complications.

It should not function as universal healing.

## 4.19.1 First Aid Is Stabilization-Oriented

Field care should usually aim to:
- control bleeding;
- protect airway and breathing;
- immobilize unstable injuries;
- remove ongoing harmful exposure;
- reduce further trauma;
- support evacuation.

The purpose is often:
**keep the patient alive and prevent deterioration long enough to reach better care.**

## 4.19.2 First Aid Does Not Erase Injury

Examples:
- stopping bleeding does not repair the lacerated vessel;
- splinting does not heal the fracture;
- cooling a burn does not restore destroyed tissue;
- freeing an airway does not repair the underlying neck injury.

Treatment changes the medical state while the structural injury remains.

## 4.19.3 Battlefield Medicine Exists Under Tactical Constraints

Field treatment may occur while:
- enemies are active;
- cover is limited;
- terrain is unstable;
- time is short;
- supplies are limited;
- evacuation is difficult.

The safest medical action may not always be tactically possible.

## 4.19.4 Safety Comes Before Treatment

A medic cannot help effectively if:
- the area is still under direct attack;
- the patient remains in fire;
- the structure is collapsing;
- the environment is toxic.

When possible, care begins by:
- controlling the threat;
- moving to cover;
- stopping ongoing exposure.

However, emergency intervention may sometimes have to occur before full safety is achieved.

## 4.19.5 Immediate Threat Prioritization

Field care should prioritize processes most likely to cause rapid death or irreversible deterioration.

Possible priorities include:
- catastrophic external bleeding;
- airway compromise;
- severe breathing impairment;
- profound circulatory collapse;
- rapidly progressive toxin or technique effects.

The exact priority may change depending on the setting and available techniques.

## 4.19.6 Hemorrhage Control

External bleeding may be reduced through:
- direct pressure;
- wound packing;
- compression;
- tourniquet-like control where appropriate;
- sealing methods;
- medical ninjutsu.

The method used should depend on:
- wound location;
- bleeding severity;
- available equipment;
- practitioner skill.

## 4.19.7 Direct Pressure

Direct pressure is a basic method for controlling many external wounds.

It may require:
- continuous application;
- sufficient force;
- access to the wound;
- time for clotting.

If pressure is removed prematurely, bleeding may restart.

## 4.19.8 Wound Packing

Deep wounds may sometimes require material to:
- fill the wound space;
- maintain pressure near the bleeding source.

This should be treated as a stabilization method rather than wound closure.

## 4.19.9 Tourniquet-Style Control

Severe limb bleeding may justify strong proximal compression.

Potential benefits:
- rapid hemorrhage control.

Potential costs:
- pain;
- restricted circulation;
- tissue risk if left too long.

The simulation should not require minute-by-minute real-world protocol detail unless medically consequential.

## 4.19.10 Internal Bleeding Is Harder to Treat in the Field

Ordinary first aid may be unable to directly stop deep internal hemorrhage.

Possible field actions may include:
- reducing movement;
- supporting circulation;
- rapid evacuation;
- specialized medical ninjutsu.

This makes internal bleeding especially dangerous.

## 4.19.11 Airway Support

Field treatment may attempt to:
- clear obstruction;
- position the patient;
- remove blood or debris where possible;
- maintain airway patency;
- use specialized equipment or ninjutsu.

Airway management may require continuous attention.

## 4.19.12 Breathing Support

Breathing problems may be supported through:
- positioning;
- removing compression;
- assisted ventilation where setting-equivalent methods exist;
- sealing chest wounds where relevant;
- medical ninjutsu.

The exact technique depends on cause.

## 4.19.13 Chest Injury Stabilization

Some chest injuries may be temporarily managed by:
- limiting movement;
- protecting the wound;
- reducing air entry through an open wound;
- supporting breathing;
- urgent evacuation.

Definitive repair may still be required.

## 4.19.14 Immobilization

Injured bones, joints, and soft tissue may be stabilized through:
- splints;
- wraps;
- slings;
- improvised rigid supports;
- chakra-assisted stabilization.

Immobilization can reduce:
- pain;
- further tissue damage;
- bleeding;
- instability during movement.

## 4.19.15 Splinting Does Not Restore Full Function

A stabilized limb may become safer to move without becoming combat-ready.

A splinted leg may support transport while remaining unusable for sprinting.

## 4.19.16 Foreign Objects

Embedded weapons or fragments may sometimes help limit bleeding by remaining in place.

Removal can worsen:
- hemorrhage;
- tissue damage.

Field removal should therefore occur only when:
- necessary for immediate survival;
- transport is impossible otherwise;
- the practitioner has a valid reason and skill.

## 4.19.17 Wound Cleaning

Field cleaning may reduce:
- contamination;
- later infection risk.

It does not take priority over immediate life-threatening problems.

Deep definitive cleaning may require more advanced care.

## 4.19.18 Burns

Field burn care may include:
- ending active burning;
- cooling where appropriate;
- removing ongoing heat source;
- protecting damaged tissue;
- supporting hydration and circulation over longer periods.

Burned tissue itself is not instantly restored.

## 4.19.19 Electrical Injury

After electrical exposure, field care may need to address:
- breathing;
- circulation;
- burns;
- motor impairment;
- neurological symptoms.

Removing the electrical source comes before direct treatment.

## 4.19.20 Cold Injury

Field management may involve:
- ending cold exposure;
- controlled warming;
- protecting tissue;
- avoiding additional trauma.

Rapid or inappropriate treatment may sometimes worsen tissue damage depending on the condition.

## 4.19.21 Poison and Toxin Field Care

Field options may include:
- stopping additional exposure;
- identifying the toxin;
- administering antidote;
- slowing absorption;
- supporting breathing/circulation;
- rapid evacuation.

The exact treatment belongs to the toxin profile and 4.23.

## 4.19.22 Chakra-Network Emergency Care

Field care may sometimes stabilize chakra-related dysfunction through:
- restoring flow;
- preventing further disruption;
- suppressing dangerous chakra effects;
- specialized medical techniques.

The exact methods belong to medical ninjutsu and specific pathology.

## 4.19.23 Pain Management

Field pain control may:
- improve cooperation;
- reduce stress;
- allow transport;
- improve limited function.

Pain relief should not:
- hide severe deterioration from the engine;
- restore structural capability;
- erase aggravation risk.

## 4.19.24 Pain Control Can Create Risk

Reducing pain may allow a character to overuse an injured structure.

The patient may feel better while still being vulnerable to:
- reinjury;
- worsening bleeding;
- structural failure.

## 4.19.25 Shock Support

Field care for shock-like deterioration may include:
- controlling the cause;
- limiting exertion;
- protecting temperature;
- positioning where relevant;
- replacing fluids or blood where setting resources permit;
- medical ninjutsu.

The most important intervention is usually correcting the underlying cause.

## 4.19.26 Casualty Positioning

Patient position may matter when:
- breathing is impaired;
- vomiting/aspiration risk exists;
- bleeding control depends on orientation;
- movement worsens injury.

The system should only track position medically when it changes outcome.

## 4.19.27 Moving the Patient

A casualty may need to be:
- dragged;
- carried;
- supported while walking;
- moved on a stretcher;
- transported by summon or vehicle.

Movement method affects:
- speed;
- fatigue;
- medical stability;
- number of rescuers required.

## 4.19.28 Emergency Extraction

Sometimes the tactical threat is greater than the medical risk of movement.

A team may choose to move an unstable casualty immediately.

This can:
- worsen injury;
- restart bleeding;
- increase pain.

But remaining in place may be worse.

The system should support these tradeoffs.

## 4.19.29 Field Stabilization Before Transport

When time permits, common goals before movement may include:
- control major bleeding;
- stabilize fractures;
- secure restraints;
- protect airway;
- reduce dangerous movement.

Better stabilization reduces transport risk.

## 4.19.30 Treatment Under Fire

A medic treating during active combat must divide attention between:
- medical task;
- personal survival;
- patient protection;
- battlefield awareness.

Allies may need to create a protected treatment window.

This creates tactical value for:
- cover;
- suppression;
- barriers;
- evacuation routes.

## 4.19.31 Treatment Requires Time

Even simple care consumes action windows.

Treatment time depends on:
- procedure complexity;
- practitioner skill;
- equipment;
- patient movement;
- battlefield pressure.

A medic cannot perform unlimited care instantly.

## 4.19.32 Treatment Can Be Interrupted

Interruption may:
- delay completion;
- reduce treatment quality;
- restart bleeding;
- waste supplies;
- create new risk.

Some interventions remain partially effective after interruption; others require completion.

## 4.19.33 Medical Actions Have Commitment

A medic applying treatment may have:
- occupied hands;
- reduced mobility;
- reduced defensive readiness;
- concentration requirements.

This integrates directly with 4.3 action commitment.

## 4.19.34 Self-Aid

A conscious injured character may treat themselves if:
- they can reach the injury;
- required limbs remain functional;
- they possess supplies and knowledge;
- the procedure is simple enough.

Self-treatment may be slower or less effective.

## 4.19.35 Buddy Aid

Ordinary teammates may provide simple emergency care without being full medics.

Possible tasks include:
- pressure on wounds;
- basic splinting;
- moving casualty;
- applying known antidote;
- extinguishing fire.

Skill limits what they can safely attempt.

## 4.19.36 Field Medic Role

A trained field medic is better able to:
- identify priorities;
- stabilize complex wounds;
- monitor deterioration;
- choose transport methods;
- use medical equipment;
- apply medical ninjutsu if trained.

This gives medical specialization strong team value.

## 4.19.37 Improvised Treatment

If supplies are unavailable, characters may improvise using:
- cloth;
- sticks;
- clothing;
- belts;
- terrain;
- available tools.

Improvised treatment may be:
- slower;
- less reliable;
- less comfortable;
- more complication-prone.

## 4.19.38 Supplies Matter When Scarce

Relevant supplies may include:
- dressings;
- bandages;
- splints;
- antidotes;
- medicines;
- sterile tools;
- chakra-support equipment.

Track them when scarcity changes decisions.

## 4.19.39 Treatment Quality

Field treatment may be summarized as:
- poor;
- adequate;
- good;
- excellent.

Quality should derive from:
- skill;
- time;
- equipment;
- battlefield conditions;
- patient cooperation;
- procedure difficulty.

The quality influences stabilization, complication risk, and recovery.

## 4.19.40 Failed Treatment

A failed treatment attempt may:
- accomplish nothing;
- only partially stabilize;
- worsen the injury;
- consume supplies;
- delay evacuation.

Failure severity should match the procedure and error.

## 4.19.41 Treatment Success Is Not Binary

A treatment may:
- reduce bleeding without fully stopping it;
- improve breathing without restoring normal function;
- partially stabilize a fracture;
- slow poison progression.

Partial success should be common when circumstances are difficult.

## 4.19.42 Treatment Priorities Can Change

After one threat is controlled, the next becomes the priority.

Example:
- catastrophic bleeding controlled;
- patient now develops respiratory distress.

The medic should reassess continuously.

## 4.19.43 Triage

With multiple casualties, limited time and resources may require prioritization.

Useful triage considerations include:
- immediate survivability;
- rate of deterioration;
- treatment time;
- available resources;
- evacuation access;
- tactical situation.

The system should avoid oversimplifying triage into a single color label when detailed choices matter.

## 4.19.44 Expectant Cases

In mass-casualty situations, a medic may encounter a patient whose treatment needs exceed available resources.

This is a resource-allocation reality, not proof that the injury is intrinsically untreatable.

The broader ethical and command decisions belong to character/world systems.

## 4.19.45 Returning to Combat After First Aid

First aid may sometimes restore enough stability for limited action.

Examples:
- bleeding controlled;
- ankle supported;
- pain reduced.

This does not mean the character is healed.

Returning to combat may:
- reopen wounds;
- worsen fractures;
- consume the stabilization margin.

## 4.19.46 Temporary Combat Restoration

A medically stabilized character may become:
- more mobile;
- more focused;
- less likely to collapse.

Any restored function should follow the treatment's actual effect.

Avoid generic:
“First Aid restores 20 vitality.”

## 4.19.47 Medical Ninjutsu in the Field

Medical ninjutsu can dramatically expand field treatment options.

It may:
- stop bleeding faster;
- repair some tissue;
- stabilize organs;
- restore chakra flow;
- reduce treatment time.

Detailed technique mechanics belong to 4.20 and Ruleset 3.

## 4.19.48 Medical Ninjutsu Still Requires Diagnosis

Powerful healing applied to the wrong problem may:
- be ineffective;
- waste chakra;
- fail to address the real threat.

Assessment and treatment remain separate skills.

## 4.19.49 Evacuation Threshold

At some point, field care should transition to:
**This patient requires definitive care.**

Triggers may include:
- internal injury;
- uncontrolled bleeding;
- major surgery need;
- severe burns;
- serious neurological injury;
- complex toxin;
- unstable chakra-network damage.

The exact threshold depends on available field capability.

## 4.19.50 Definitive Care Is a Different Layer

Hospital or advanced medical care may provide:
- surgery;
- prolonged monitoring;
- advanced medical ninjutsu;
- blood replacement;
- specialized antidotes;
- rehabilitation.

Field medicine should not duplicate those systems.

## 4.19.51 Battlefield Medicine Record

A meaningful treatment action should be able to store:
- practitioner;
- patient;
- procedure;
- target injury/process;
- start time;
- duration;
- equipment used;
- treatment quality;
- effect;
- temporary versus durable stabilization;
- complications;
- supplies consumed;
- follow-up needs.

## 4.19.52 Treatment State Updates

After first aid, the injury record may change from:

**Untreated**
to:
**Temporarily Stabilized**

or:
**Field Stabilized**

while remaining:
**Structurally Injured**

This keeps treatment state and injury state distinct.

## 4.19.53 Suggested Field-Care Pipeline

1. Establish enough safety to act.
2. Perform rapid assessment.
3. Identify immediate threats.
4. Choose the highest-priority intervention.
5. Check required skill, tools, time, and access.
6. Resolve uncertain treatment through Ruleset 2.
7. Apply partial or full stabilization.
8. Update deterioration rate.
9. Reassess patient.
10. Decide whether to continue care, return to combat, or evacuate.
11. Preserve all remaining injuries and follow-up needs.

## 4.19.54 Compression

Routine care can be summarized when:
- injury is minor;
- treatment is obvious;
- no time pressure exists.

Examples:
- small wound cleaned and bandaged;
- simple ankle supported;
- minor burn cooled and dressed.

The system should zoom in when:
- life-threatening deterioration exists;
- resources are limited;
- treatment competes with combat;
- evacuation decisions matter.

## Core Design Rule

First aid should primarily **preserve life, prevent worsening, and buy time**.

It may improve function, but it should not erase serious injuries unless a specific advanced medical technique genuinely repairs them.
