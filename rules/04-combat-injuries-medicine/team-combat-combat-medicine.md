# 4.26 — Team Combat & Combat Medicine

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines coordinated combat between multiple allied shinobi, including:
- squad roles;
- communication;
- formations;
- overlapping attacks;
- rescue actions;
- casualty extraction;
- medic protection;
- friendly-fire risk;
- command;
- coordinated nonlethal capture;
- combat medicine integration.

The governing rule is:
**A team should become stronger because its members create opportunities, cover weaknesses, share information, and divide responsibilities—not because standing near allies grants a generic bonus.**

## 4.26.1 A Squad Is More Than Several Individuals

A team should be represented as a network of:
- roles;
- communication;
- awareness;
- positioning;
- responsibilities;
- shared objectives.

Each member still acts under normal Ruleset 4 mechanics.

Team coordination changes:
- what information is available;
- what threats are covered;
- what actions can overlap;
- who can intervene;
- who has freedom to specialize.

## 4.26.2 Team Advantage Comes From Functional Cooperation

Useful team advantages may include:
- crossfire;
- rescue coverage;
- sensory support;
- route denial;
- setup and follow-up;
- protection of specialists;
- shared target information;
- rotation under fatigue.

Avoid:
“Three allies nearby: +15 combat.”

## 4.26.3 Roles Are Tactical Functions, Not Classes

A shinobi may temporarily fill roles such as:
- point;
- pressure;
- flank;
- overwatch;
- sensor;
- medic;
- capture specialist;
- rear guard;
- command;
- extraction.

Roles can change during combat.

## 4.26.4 Role Assignment Can Be Formal or Emergent

A trained squad may enter combat with planned roles.

An improvised team may develop them dynamically.

The engine should track actual responsibility, not require a formal squad template.

## 4.26.5 Communication Is a Combat Resource

Teams coordinate through:
- speech;
- hand signals;
- radio or communication tools;
- sensory links;
- prearranged signals;
- visual cues.

Communication can be:
- delayed;
- blocked;
- misunderstood;
- intercepted.

## 4.26.6 Shared Information Is Not Automatic

One teammate spotting a hidden enemy does not instantly make every ally aware.

Information must be:
- communicated;
- perceived;
- understood.

Some techniques may allow immediate shared awareness.

## 4.26.7 Communication Under Pressure

Communication may become harder because of:
- noise;
- distance;
- smoke;
- divided attention;
- injury;
- enemy interference.

Simple trained signals may outperform detailed verbal instruction in chaotic combat.

## 4.26.8 Preplanned Signals

Teams may prepare:
- attack cues;
- retreat signals;
- capture signals;
- medical emergency calls;
- formation shifts.

Preparation reduces communication burden.

## 4.26.9 Command

A leader may improve team performance by:
- assigning priorities;
- directing movement;
- coordinating timing;
- recognizing changes;
- reallocating roles.

Command does not directly grant allies better attributes.

Its value is better coordination.

## 4.26.10 Initiative Is Still Individual

Team combat should not use one shared initiative score.

Members may act at different times depending on:
- readiness;
- speed;
- position;
- current commitment.

Coordination may synchronize actions without removing individual timing.

## 4.26.11 Synchronized Actions

Allies may deliberately time actions to occur together.

Examples:
- simultaneous attack from multiple angles;
- barrier raised as ally withdraws;
- distraction immediately followed by capture attempt;
- medic moves as covering fire begins.

Synchronization requires:
- communication;
- prediction;
- compatible timing.

## 4.26.12 Combined Attacks Do Not Need Combo Multipliers

Two attacks arriving together can be stronger because they:
- divide attention;
- restrict escape;
- force incompatible defenses;
- overload reaction capacity.

The advantage comes from threat interaction, not a generic damage multiplier.

## 4.26.13 Setup and Follow-Up

One ally may create an opening for another.

Examples:
- smoke obscures enemy vision;
- Earth Release limits movement;
- sensor identifies hidden target;
- taijutsu pressure prevents hand seals;
- teammate attacks the resulting opening.

The setup changes battlefield state.

## 4.26.14 Crossfire and Multiple Angles

Attacking from different directions may:
- reduce available cover;
- divide attention;
- limit safe dodge paths.

This interfaces with 4.6 threat saturation.

## 4.26.15 Area Denial and Team Movement

One teammate can control space while another:
- advances;
- retreats;
- rescues;
- treats;
- captures.

This creates safe action windows without needing artificial protection bonuses.

## 4.26.16 Overwatch

An ally may deliberately hold attention on:
- enemy movement;
- escape route;
- threatened medic;
- hidden flank.

Overwatch creates potential intervention windows.

It is not a passive automatic attack.

## 4.26.17 Intervention

A teammate may intervene by:
- blocking;
- intercepting;
- counterattacking;
- pulling an ally away;
- creating cover;
- disrupting the attacker.

Whether intervention is possible depends on:
- awareness;
- distance;
- timing;
- current commitment.

## 4.26.18 Protecting a Vulnerable Ally

A team may deliberately protect:
- medic;
- sensor;
- injured ally;
- objective carrier;
- exhausted teammate.

Protection may involve:
- positioning;
- body blocking;
- area denial;
- barriers;
- suppression.

## 4.26.19 Body Blocking Has Consequences

Physically intercepting an attack may:
- redirect contact;
- transfer injury;
- create collision;
- expose the protector.

Protection is a real action, not a free defensive aura.

## 4.26.20 Rotation Under Pressure

Allies may rotate roles to:
- allow recovery;
- preserve stamina;
- protect injured members;
- replace someone whose chakra is depleted.

This directly interfaces with 4.13 fatigue.

## 4.26.21 Team Fatigue Management

A disciplined team can reduce individual exhaustion by:
- alternating frontline pressure;
- sharing carrying load;
- rotating guard duty;
- allowing short recovery windows.

This can create major endurance advantages in prolonged missions.

## 4.26.22 Team Chakra Management

Teams may coordinate chakra-intensive actions so that:
- not everyone exhausts simultaneously;
- one member preserves reserves for medical treatment;
- another preserves escape techniques.

Chakra resource ownership remains individual unless a transfer mechanic exists.

## 4.26.23 Specialists Need Protection

A sensor, medic, or sealing specialist may contribute enormously while being vulnerable during:
- concentration;
- diagnosis;
- treatment;
- seal preparation.

The team may need to create protected working time.

## 4.26.24 Combat Medic Positioning

A combat medic should balance:
- access to casualties;
- personal safety;
- line of movement;
- ability to retreat;
- ability to observe the team.

There is no universally correct position.

## 4.26.25 Medic Protection

Allies may protect a medic through:
- cover;
- barriers;
- suppression;
- escort;
- route control.

Protecting the medic can preserve the squad's future survivability.

## 4.26.26 Medical Reserve

A squad may deliberately preserve:
- chakra;
- medicine;
- antidotes;
- evacuation capacity

for emergencies.

Spending every medical resource early can create severe later risk.

## 4.26.27 Casualty Recognition

A teammate may become:
- injured but combat-capable;
- incapacitated;
- medically unstable;
- near-death.

The team must correctly recognize which state exists.

4.18 governs medical assessment.

## 4.26.28 Rescue Priority

Rescue decisions may compare:
- immediate medical danger;
- tactical accessibility;
- mission objective;
- risk to rescuers;
- remaining team strength.

There is no universal rule that every casualty must be recovered immediately regardless of battlefield reality.

## 4.26.29 Casualty Extraction

Extraction may involve:
- drag;
- carry;
- stretcher;
- summon;
- vehicle;
- movement technique.

Each method changes:
- speed;
- rescuer fatigue;
- patient stability;
- number of people occupied.

## 4.26.30 One-Person Rescue

A single rescuer may need to sacrifice:
- mobility;
- weapon access;
- hand seals;
- defensive ability

while carrying or dragging a casualty.

## 4.26.31 Two-Person Rescue

Two or more teammates may:
- stabilize better;
- move faster;
- reduce individual fatigue.

But more rescuers means fewer active combatants.

## 4.26.32 Rescue Under Fire

A team may need to create a rescue window through:
- suppression;
- smoke;
- barrier;
- distraction;
- forced retreat.

The rescue is then resolved through normal movement and treatment mechanics.

## 4.26.33 Casualty Abandonment Is Possible

A commander or character may decide that immediate recovery is impossible.

The game should permit:
- delayed rescue;
- retreat without casualty;
- leaving a body behind.

The social, ethical, and mission consequences belong to broader systems.

## 4.26.34 Recovering the Dead

Retrieving a confirmed dead teammate may still matter for:
- identification;
- burial;
- intelligence security;
- bloodline protection;
- preventing enemy experimentation;
- emotional reasons.

This can become a tactical objective separate from medical rescue.

## 4.26.35 Team Medical Triage

When several allies are injured, the medic may need to prioritize:
- who is deteriorating fastest;
- who can be stabilized quickly;
- who must be evacuated;
- who can continue fighting.

This interfaces with 4.19.

## 4.26.36 Buddy Aid

Non-medics can help by:
- holding pressure;
- carrying casualties;
- administering known supplies;
- guarding treatment;
- communicating symptoms.

This allows the medic to focus on tasks requiring specialist skill.

## 4.26.37 Distributed Medical Tasks

A trained team may divide care:
- one person holds pressure;
- one maintains security;
- medic diagnoses;
- another prepares equipment.

This reduces medical-action bottlenecks.

## 4.26.38 Medical Communication

The medic may issue concise instructions such as:
- “Do not move him.”
- “Keep pressure here.”
- “She can walk but cannot sprint.”
- “We need evacuation now.”

These instructions translate medical state into team action.

## 4.26.39 Friendly Fire

Team attacks may threaten allies when:
- areas overlap;
- visibility is poor;
- timing fails;
- allies cross trajectories.

Friendly-fire risk should follow actual geometry and information.

## 4.26.40 Area Techniques Require Deconfliction

Large techniques may require allies to:
- clear an area;
- hold position;
- retreat behind cover;
- time movement.

Poor coordination may create accidental exposure.

## 4.26.41 Hidden Allies and Friendly Fire

A teammate whose position is unknown may be endangered by:
- explosives;
- fire;
- collapsing structures;
- area denial.

Shared positional awareness matters.

## 4.26.42 Obstruction Between Allies

Allies can physically interfere with:
- movement;
- line of attack;
- retreat;
- hand signals.

Crowded teamwork can be worse than disciplined spacing.

## 4.26.43 Formation

A formation is a spatial arrangement created for a purpose.

Examples:
- protect medic;
- surround target;
- maintain escape lane;
- screen sensor;
- escort prisoner.

Formation should be described functionally rather than through fixed universal stat bonuses.

## 4.26.44 Formation Breakdown

Formation may collapse because of:
- forced movement;
- terrain;
- casualties;
- panic;
- enemy pressure;
- communication failure.

The team must then adapt.

## 4.26.45 Small-Unit Doctrine

Experienced squads may possess rehearsed procedures for:
- ambush;
- retreat;
- casualty recovery;
- prisoner handling;
- night movement;
- room entry.

Doctrine reduces decision and communication time.

## 4.26.46 Team Familiarity

Familiar teammates may coordinate more efficiently because they know:
- habits;
- signals;
- preferred techniques;
- reaction patterns.

This should reduce coordination friction rather than grant an arbitrary friendship bonus.

## 4.26.47 Improvised Teams

Unfamiliar allies may still cooperate effectively, but may have:
- slower communication;
- duplicated roles;
- conflicting assumptions.

Strong leadership and simple plans can compensate.

## 4.26.48 Team Techniques

Some jutsu explicitly require multiple users.

Their technique records should define:
- number of users;
- timing;
- chakra contribution;
- formation;
- failure conditions.

Ruleset 4 handles tactical execution and interruption.

## 4.26.49 Cooperative Chakra Control

Multiple users may combine chakra for:
- barriers;
- medical procedures;
- sealing;
- large techniques.

Ruleset 3 owns chakra combination mechanics.

Ruleset 4 tracks:
- position;
- interruption;
- vulnerability;
- team consequences.

## 4.26.50 Team Capture

Live capture often benefits from role specialization:
- pressure;
- escape denial;
- restraint;
- suppression;
- medical monitoring.

This interfaces with 4.15 and 4.25.

## 4.26.51 Team Retreat

Retreat may require:
- covering action;
- casualty movement;
- smoke;
- route selection;
- rear guard.

The fastest individual may need to slow down for the group.

## 4.26.52 Rear Guard

A rear guard may deliberately remain exposed longer to:
- delay pursuit;
- protect casualties;
- preserve retreat route.

This is a tactical commitment with real risk.

## 4.26.53 Splitting the Team

A squad may divide into subgroups.

Benefits may include:
- multiple objectives;
- flanking;
- wider search.

Costs include:
- weaker mutual support;
- slower rescue;
- reduced information sharing.

## 4.26.54 Reinforcements

New allies entering combat should inherit the current battlefield state.

They do not:
- reset initiative;
- restore allies;
- erase hazards.

Their arrival changes available options from that moment onward.

## 4.26.55 Command Casualties

If a leader is incapacitated, the squad may:
- transfer command;
- follow preplanned procedure;
- fragment.

A trained team should have succession expectations.

## 4.26.56 Information Specialists

Sensors can support the entire squad by identifying:
- hidden enemies;
- reinforcements;
- chakra changes;
- escape paths.

Their information must still be communicated or linked.

## 4.26.57 Medical Intelligence

A medic may identify exploitable facts about allies or enemies such as:
- injured leg;
- toxin symptoms;
- deteriorating target.

Using that information tactically should depend on whether it can be communicated in time.

## 4.26.58 Team State Record

For consequential team combat, the engine should be able to track:
- members;
- current roles;
- command;
- shared information;
- formation;
- communication status;
- casualties;
- medical resources;
- extraction responsibility;
- current objective;
- retreat route;
- prisoners;
- separated members.

## 4.26.59 Suggested Team-Combat Pipeline

1. Establish team objective and current roles.
2. Determine what each member knows.
3. Track positioning and communication.
4. Resolve individual actions through normal combat rules.
5. Apply setup, intervention, or overlapping threats where justified.
6. Update casualties and role availability.
7. Reassign tasks as needed.
8. Coordinate treatment, rescue, capture, or retreat.
9. Preserve friendly-fire and obstruction risk.
10. Continue until objective, disengagement, surrender, capture, or defeat resolves the encounter.

## 4.26.60 Compression

Routine squad cooperation can be summarized when:
- team training is high;
- opposition is minor;
- no casualty or coordination problem matters.

The simulation should zoom in when:
- synchronized timing is decisive;
- communication fails;
- someone needs rescue;
- the medic is exposed;
- friendly fire is possible;
- the team splits or loses command.

## Core Design Rule

A team becomes effective by **sharing information, dividing responsibilities, controlling space, creating action windows, and protecting vulnerable roles**.

Teamwork should emerge from actual coordination and battlefield state—not from generic party bonuses.
