# 4.16 — Weapons, Armor & Combat Equipment

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines how weapons, armor, ninja tools, ammunition, consumables, and combat equipment interact with the combat and injury systems.

Equipment should matter because of what it physically and tactically enables:
- reach;
- leverage;
- cutting or piercing ability;
- protection;
- concealment;
- ammunition;
- utility;
- durability;
- setup requirements;
- maintenance.

The system should avoid reducing all equipment to generic attack and defense bonuses.

## 4.16.1 Equipment Has Functional Properties

A weapon or armor item should be described by the properties that actually matter.

For weapons, relevant properties may include:
- weapon class;
- reach;
- mass;
- balance;
- striking surface;
- edge or point quality;
- handling;
- durability;
- grip requirements;
- concealability;
- throwing suitability;
- ammunition if applicable;
- special construction;
- chakra compatibility.

For armor, relevant properties may include:
- body coverage;
- material;
- thickness;
- flexibility;
- mass;
- mobility interference;
- penetration resistance;
- cutting resistance;
- blunt-force distribution;
- thermal behavior;
- electrical behavior;
- durability;
- weak points.

Only properties relevant to actual use need to be represented.

## 4.16.2 Weapon Skill and Weapon Properties Are Separate

A superior weapon does not replace skill.

Skill determines:
- timing;
- accuracy;
- efficient movement;
- weapon control;
- feints;
- recovery;
- defensive use.

The weapon determines what the user's movement can physically accomplish.

A master can use an ordinary kunai extremely well.
An untrained person does not become a master swordsman because they hold an exceptional blade.

## 4.16.3 Weapon Reach

Reach affects:
- who can threaten whom first;
- engagement distance;
- interception;
- safe positioning;
- grappling entry;
- confined-space usability.

Longer reach is an advantage only while enough space exists to use it.

A spear may dominate open distance but become awkward in a narrow room.

## 4.16.4 Reach Is Not a Fixed Initiative Bonus

Long reach should not simply grant “attack first.”

It changes:
- the distance at which the weapon becomes threatening;
- what movement the opponent must cross;
- what reaction windows exist.

Speed, timing, terrain, and technique still matter.

## 4.16.5 Mass and Momentum

Weapon mass may increase:
- blunt force;
- momentum;
- guard-breaking potential;
- structural impact.

It may also increase:
- recovery time;
- fatigue;
- commitment;
- difficulty changing direction.

A heavy weapon can therefore be powerful without simply having a higher damage number.

## 4.16.6 Balance and Handling

Two weapons of similar mass may handle very differently.

Balance can affect:
- acceleration;
- recovery;
- directional changes;
- precision;
- repeated attacks.

Well-balanced weapons may be easier to control.
Poorly balanced or unusually weighted weapons may demand specialized training.

## 4.16.7 Edge and Point Quality

Cutting and piercing weapons depend on:
- sharpness;
- geometry;
- material;
- maintenance;
- contact angle.

A dull blade may still cause blunt trauma while losing cutting efficiency.

A damaged point may reduce penetration.

## 4.16.8 Weapon Condition

Weapon condition may be summarized as:
- excellent;
- serviceable;
- worn;
- damaged;
- compromised;
- broken.

The label should derive from actual physical condition.

Routine weapons do not require constant durability tracking unless they suffer meaningful stress.

## 4.16.9 Weapon Failure

Weapons may:
- chip;
- bend;
- crack;
- snap;
- lose an edge;
- loosen at the grip;
- jam where mechanisms exist.

Failure should depend on:
- material;
- construction;
- previous damage;
- impact;
- opposing material;
- technique amplification.

## 4.16.10 Weapon Maintenance

Maintenance may include:
- sharpening;
- cleaning;
- oiling;
- repairing;
- restringing;
- replacing worn components;
- checking seals or mechanisms.

Maintenance should matter mainly over long-term simulation or after significant use.

The game should not require routine busywork for equipment that remains in normal condition.

## 4.16.11 Weapon Quality

Quality can influence:
- reliability;
- edge retention;
- durability;
- balance;
- craftsmanship;
- chakra conductivity;
- concealment.

Quality should not be represented as a universal “+2 weapon” unless a compressed internal abstraction requires it.

## 4.16.12 Material Matters

Materials may differ in:
- hardness;
- toughness;
- flexibility;
- heat resistance;
- conductivity;
- chakra interaction;
- weight.

Special materials should define explicit relevant properties.

## 4.16.13 Weapon Versus Weapon Contact

Parries, blocks, and weapon binds may damage equipment.

The outcome depends on:
- force;
- angle;
- weapon material;
- edge contact;
- reinforcement;
- condition.

A light kunai should not repeatedly block an overwhelmingly powerful reinforced blade without consequence.

## 4.16.14 Weapon Binding

Weapons may become temporarily locked or trapped against one another.

This may create:
- leverage contests;
- openings;
- disarm attempts;
- grappling transitions;
- reduced mobility.

Weapon binding is a positional event, not a generic status bonus.

## 4.16.15 One-Handed and Two-Handed Use

Some weapons can be used:
- one-handed;
- two-handed;
- only with two hands.

Two-handed use may improve:
- leverage;
- control;
- force;
- stability.

It may reduce:
- free-hand availability;
- hand-seal access;
- grappling options.

## 4.16.16 Draw and Ready State

A sheathed or stored weapon is not automatically ready.

Drawing may require:
- time;
- hand availability;
- space;
- access to the weapon.

Readiness interacts with 4.2 and 4.3.

A surprised shinobi may know danger exists while still needing to draw their weapon.

## 4.16.17 Concealed Weapons

Weapons may be hidden:
- in sleeves;
- boots;
- pouches;
- scrolls;
- body compartments;
- terrain.

Concealment affects information and surprise.

Ruleset 2 determines whether hidden equipment is detected.

## 4.16.18 Thrown Weapons

Thrown weapons depend on:
- release speed;
- mass;
- aerodynamics;
- spin;
- distance;
- target movement;
- user skill.

Examples include:
- kunai;
- shuriken;
- senbon;
- improvised objects.

Thrown weapons follow projectile timing and line-of-effect rules from earlier sections.

## 4.16.19 Thrown Weapon Recovery

Thrown weapons remain where they land unless:
- retrieved;
- destroyed;
- moved;
- recovered through a technique.

A character can run out of ordinary thrown tools.

The game should not assume infinite ammunition.

## 4.16.20 Ammunition

Ranged tools may require:
- arrows;
- bolts;
- darts;
- senbon;
- specialized cartridges;
- explosive payloads.

Ammunition should be tracked when scarcity can affect the encounter or mission.

Routine abundance can be abstracted.

## 4.16.21 Ammunition Types

Different ammunition may alter:
- penetration;
- range;
- spread;
- toxin delivery;
- explosive effects;
- signaling;
- restraint.

Special ammunition should define its actual mechanism rather than provide a generic damage bonus.

## 4.16.22 Reloading and Resupply

Reloading may require:
- time;
- free hands;
- access to ammunition;
- reduced movement or concentration.

Resupply matters over extended missions.

The system should track it only when it can affect decisions.

## 4.16.23 Armor Is Location-Specific

Armor protects only what it covers.

An armor record should identify relevant protected regions.

Examples:
- torso;
- shoulders;
- forearms;
- shins;
- face;
- head.

Coverage gaps remain vulnerable.

## 4.16.24 Armor Protection Is Mechanism-Specific

Armor may behave differently against:
- cutting;
- piercing;
- blunt impact;
- heat;
- electricity;
- fragmentation.

A material can be excellent against one mechanism and poor against another.

There should be no single universal armor value for all threats.

## 4.16.25 Armor Can Stop Penetration Without Stopping Trauma

An armor plate may prevent a blade or projectile from entering the body while still transferring:
- blunt force;
- knockback;
- pain;
- fracture risk.

“Armor stopped it” does not mean “nothing happened.”

## 4.16.26 Flexible Armor and Rigid Armor

Flexible armor may offer:
- good mobility;
- cutting resistance;
- some projectile protection.

Rigid armor may offer:
- stronger penetration resistance;
- better force distribution.

It may cost:
- mobility;
- weight;
- noise;
- heat retention;
- flexibility.

Exact properties depend on construction.

## 4.16.27 Armor and Mobility

Armor should affect movement only when its:
- mass;
- rigidity;
- poor fit;
- damage

actually interferes.

Routine shinobi armor should not automatically impose large mobility penalties.

## 4.16.28 Armor Fit

Poorly fitted armor can:
- shift;
- expose gaps;
- interfere with movement;
- create noise;
- reduce comfort.

Proper fit matters especially for specialized or heavy armor.

## 4.16.29 Armor Durability

Armor can be:
- intact;
- worn;
- damaged;
- breached;
- destroyed.

A breached section may no longer provide meaningful protection.

Durability tracking should activate when repeated impacts make it relevant.

## 4.16.30 Layered Protection

Characters may combine:
- clothing;
- flexible armor;
- rigid plates;
- chakra reinforcement;
- defensive techniques.

Each layer interacts with the incoming attack in sequence.

Protection should not simply add into one universal defense score.

## 4.16.31 Armor and Elemental Interaction

Material may matter against elemental techniques.

Examples:
- metal conducting electricity;
- wet clothing affecting conductivity;
- insulation reducing heat transfer;
- heavy armor becoming dangerous in certain environments.

These interactions should follow the specific technique and material.

## 4.16.32 Head Protection

Helmets or forehead protectors may provide limited protection depending on:
- coverage;
- material;
- attack path.

A forehead protector does not count as full head armor.

Its symbolic role and its physical protective role should remain separate.

## 4.16.33 Clothing

Ordinary clothing may influence:
- concealment;
- minor abrasion;
- fire spread;
- cold protection;
- pockets and equipment access.

Routine clothing should not require detailed defensive statistics.

## 4.16.34 Ninja Tool Pouches

Tool pouches affect:
- equipment accessibility;
- readiness;
- storage.

A tool stored in an accessible pouch may be drawn faster than one packed away.

Damage to a pouch may:
- spill contents;
- destroy tools;
- deny access.

## 4.16.35 Explosive Tags

Explosive tags should be treated as equipment that creates an explosive effect.

Ruleset 3 or item records define:
- activation;
- trigger;
- chakra/seal behavior.

Ruleset 4 handles:
- blast;
- heat;
- fragmentation;
- forced movement;
- cover interaction.

## 4.16.36 Smoke Bombs and Obscurants

Smoke tools primarily create:
- concealment;
- targeting disruption;
- escape opportunities;
- breathing irritation where relevant.

They should not simply impose an “accuracy penalty.”

## 4.16.37 Flash and Sensory Tools

Tools may create:
- flash blindness;
- sound disruption;
- signaling;
- distraction.

The condition system determines the resulting impairment.

## 4.16.38 Wire and Traps

Wire may be used for:
- trip lines;
- restraint;
- weapon redirection;
- trap triggers;
- area control.

Its effectiveness depends on:
- placement;
- visibility;
- tension;
- anchoring;
- material strength.

## 4.16.39 Medical Equipment

Combatants may carry:
- bandages;
- splints;
- medicines;
- antidotes;
- surgical tools;
- chakra-assisted medical tools.

These items support later medical sections.

Having equipment does not replace the skill required to use it.

## 4.16.40 Specialized Restraint Equipment

Capture teams may use:
- reinforced bindings;
- chakra-suppression restraints;
- sealing equipment;
- cuffs;
- transport restraints.

Ruleset 4.15 defines physical control.
Specific sealing behavior belongs to Ruleset 3.

## 4.16.41 Specialized Weapons

Unique or named weapons may possess:
- unusual material;
- chakra conductivity;
- special shape;
- transformation;
- sealing functions;
- elemental effects.

Their special abilities belong to Ruleset 3 or item-specific records.

Ruleset 4 handles their ordinary physical interaction.

## 4.16.42 Chakra Conductivity

Some weapons may channel chakra more efficiently than others.

This may influence:
- elemental coating;
- cutting enhancement;
- durability under chakra reinforcement;
- technique compatibility.

Ruleset 3 determines chakra cost and effect.
Ruleset 4 receives the resulting physical profile.

## 4.16.43 Chakra Reinforcement of Equipment

Weapons and armor may sometimes be reinforced with chakra.

Reinforcement may improve:
- durability;
- cutting;
- penetration;
- resistance.

It may also:
- consume chakra;
- require control;
- fail when concentration breaks.

Again, this should modify physical properties rather than create temporary item HP.

## 4.16.44 Improvised Weapons

Characters may use:
- rocks;
- sticks;
- broken tools;
- furniture;
- debris.

Improvised weapons derive properties from:
- size;
- mass;
- shape;
- material;
- handling.

They do not need formal item records unless used repeatedly.

## 4.16.45 Broken Equipment Can Become Hazardous

Damaged equipment may:
- create sharp edges;
- jam;
- expose the wearer;
- restrict movement;
- break during use;
- become unusable.

The new physical condition should determine its effect.

## 4.16.46 Equipment Loss

Weapons and tools may be:
- dropped;
- knocked away;
- stolen;
- destroyed;
- left behind.

Equipment location persists in the battlefield state.

## 4.16.47 Equipment Accessibility

Possessing an item is not the same as having immediate access to it.

An item may be:
- in hand;
- sheathed;
- in pouch;
- inside pack;
- stored in scroll;
- dropped nearby;
- inaccessible because a limb is restrained.

Accessibility affects action timing.

## 4.16.48 Equipment and Hand Occupancy

Hands may be occupied by:
- weapon;
- shield;
- carried ally;
- restraint;
- climbing;
- medical work.

This can affect:
- hand seals;
- tool use;
- grappling;
- weapon switching.

Hand occupancy should be tracked only when it changes options.

## 4.16.49 Equipment and Concealment

Large weapons and armor may be difficult to hide.

Small tools may be easily concealed.

Concealment interacts with:
- social situations;
- ambush;
- searches;
- deception.

Ruleset 2 handles detection.

## 4.16.50 Equipment Quality Does Not Override Power Scale

A superior mundane weapon can improve reliability and effectiveness.

It should not allow an ordinary user to ignore overwhelming differences in:
- speed;
- strength;
- chakra;
- defensive techniques;
- physiology.

Special artifacts may exceed ordinary limits, but their abilities must explicitly explain how.

## 4.16.51 Scarcity and Logistics

Equipment can matter strategically through:
- limited explosive tags;
- limited antidotes;
- lost weapons;
- damaged armor;
- depleted ammunition.

Long missions should preserve meaningful supply state.

Routine low-value consumables can be compressed when scarcity is irrelevant.

## 4.16.52 Equipment Preparation

Before a mission, characters may choose:
- weapon loadout;
- armor;
- specialized tools;
- antidotes;
- restraints;
- explosives.

Preparation can create tactical advantage later without granting automatic success.

## 4.16.53 Equipment Knowledge

Characters may not recognize:
- unusual weapon mechanisms;
- hidden seals;
- toxin-coated blades;
- specialized armor;
- disguised tools.

Information rules determine what is identified.

## 4.16.54 Minimum Weapon Record

A consequential weapon should be able to store:
- class;
- reach;
- mass/handling profile;
- attack mechanisms;
- durability/condition;
- hand requirements;
- ammunition if any;
- chakra compatibility;
- special properties.

## 4.16.55 Minimum Armor Record

A consequential armor item should be able to store:
- protected regions;
- material/construction;
- relevant resistances;
- mobility impact;
- durability/condition;
- weak points;
- elemental/material interactions.

## 4.16.56 Minimum Equipment State

When relevant, the engine should be able to track:
- equipped items;
- item accessibility;
- hand occupancy;
- ammunition/consumables;
- damaged or broken equipment;
- dropped equipment;
- active tools/traps;
- relevant carried medical or restraint gear.

## 4.16.57 Compression

For ordinary equipment, the simulation can use concise descriptors.

Examples:
- standard kunai, serviceable;
- shinobi flak jacket, intact;
- three explosive tags remaining;
- damaged short sword.

Detailed properties should expand only when they affect a consequential interaction.

## Core Design Rule

Equipment should matter because of **what it physically protects, reaches, carries, cuts, pierces, blocks, conceals, or enables**.

The system should prefer concrete item properties and persistent equipment state over generic “weapon damage,” “armor class,” or arbitrary quality bonuses.
