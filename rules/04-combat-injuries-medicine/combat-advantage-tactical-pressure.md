# 4.6 — Combat Advantage & Tactical Pressure

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines how combatants create, exploit, and lose tactical advantages. It covers openings, flanking, blind spots, setup actions, pressure, positional leverage, coordination, and cumulative strain without relying on large stacks of generic numerical modifiers.

## 4.6.1 Advantage Should Change Available Options Before It Changes Numbers

The preferred effect of tactical advantage is to alter:
- what the opponent can safely do;
- which defenses remain available;
- how much time exists to react;
- what spaces are reachable;
- how predictable responses become;
- how much commitment is required to recover.

Flat bonuses may exist internally in compressed resolution, but detailed combat should represent the concrete tactical effect whenever possible.

## 4.6.2 An Opening Is a Temporary Vulnerability

An opening exists when a combatant is temporarily less able to answer a specific kind of threat.

Openings may result from:
- overextension;
- failed attacks;
- recovery lag;
- poor footing;
- disrupted guard;
- divided attention;
- injury;
- environmental restriction;
- being airborne without control;
- reacting to another attacker;
- jutsu preparation;
- weapon binding;
- successful feint.

Openings are contextual and short-lived.

## 4.6.3 Openings Are Not Universal Weakness States

An opening against one kind of attack may not help another.

Examples:
- exposed torso may help a close-range striker but not a distant ally without line of sight;
- lowered guard may matter against a punch but not against a ground-based trap;
- airborne vulnerability may matter against projectiles but less against an attack that cannot aim upward.

The simulation should record what opportunity was actually created.

## 4.6.4 Advantage Can Be Created Deliberately

Characters can intentionally create openings through:
- feints;
- forced movement;
- repeated attacks;
- terrain manipulation;
- threatening multiple outcomes;
- controlling escape routes;
- attacking balance;
- suppressive fire;
- baiting predictable defenses;
- sacrificing position for setup.

This lets weaker attacks contribute tactically even when they are unlikely to cause serious injury.

## 4.6.5 Setup Actions Should Matter

A setup action prepares a later action rather than seeking immediate decisive effect.

Examples:
- placing wire;
- forcing an enemy toward a trap;
- coating terrain with water before Lightning Release;
- marking an escape route;
- creating smoke;
- distracting an enemy;
- breaking cover;
- testing defensive habits.

A successful setup should modify the later situation rather than provide a generic stacking bonus.

## 4.6.6 Pressure Represents Forced Defensive Commitment

Pressure occurs when one combatant repeatedly forces another to spend time, movement, attention, chakra, or positioning on defense.

Pressure can accumulate through:
- repeated attacks;
- area denial;
- pursuit;
- forcing movement;
- preventing preparation;
- denying recovery;
- attacking objectives;
- overlapping threats.

Pressure is tactical, not necessarily physical damage.

## 4.6.7 Pressure Can Accumulate Without a Numeric Meter

The simulation should normally represent pressure through its consequences:
- loss of space;
- degraded footing;
- reduced reaction windows;
- fatigue;
- depleted chakra;
- exposed habits;
- separation from allies;
- inability to prepare techniques;
- damaged equipment;
- increasingly constrained defenses.

A hidden pressure estimate may be used for compression, but the player-facing system should show what is actually changing.

## 4.6.8 Losing Tempo Can Create Cascading Disadvantage

A defender who repeatedly reacts may become easier to pressure because:
- they are moving where the attacker wants;
- their recovery windows shrink;
- their options become predictable;
- they cannot prepare stronger responses;
- they are driven into worse terrain.

This should create momentum without making comeback impossible.

## 4.6.9 Regaining Tempo Requires a Tactical Change

A pressured combatant may regain initiative through:
- successful counterattack;
- creating distance;
- using terrain;
- switching defense type;
- deploying a disruptive jutsu;
- receiving allied support;
- accepting limited damage to complete a stronger action;
- exploiting opponent overcommitment;
- revealing a concealed technique.

Tempo should shift because the tactical state changed, not because turns alternate automatically.

## 4.6.10 Flanking Is About Angles and Attention

Flanking should matter because:
- the defender must monitor multiple directions;
- some forms of cover become ineffective;
- movement options shrink;
- one attacker can exploit reactions to another;
- certain blocks or guards cannot cover both threats.

Flanking is not a universal bonus.

## 4.6.11 Blind Spots Are Contextual

A blind spot exists when a character cannot adequately perceive or respond to a threat from a given angle or information source.

Blind spots can arise from:
- anatomy;
- equipment;
- terrain;
- smoke;
- facing;
- fixation on another target;
- sensory limitations;
- injury;
- technique-specific weaknesses.

Perception abilities may reduce or eliminate some blind spots.

## 4.6.12 Facing Matters Only When It Changes Awareness or Response

The system does not need constant exact facing tracking.

Facing becomes important when:
- line of sight is limited;
- someone attacks from behind;
- a weapon or shield protects only certain directions;
- a character is focused on another threat;
- body orientation affects available movement.

## 4.6.13 Multiple Attackers Create Reaction Conflicts

The danger of multiple attackers comes from overlapping demands.

Examples:
- one attack forces a high block while another targets the legs;
- one enemy attacks while another blocks the escape route;
- one attacker pressures while another prepares a jutsu.

The defender may be physically capable of answering each threat individually but unable to answer all of them at once.

## 4.6.14 Coordination Quality Matters

Multiple attackers are not automatically effective as a team.

Poor coordination may cause:
- blocked attack lanes;
- collisions;
- friendly fire;
- redundant attacks;
- openings for the defender;
- interference with allied jutsu.

Good coordination creates:
- staggered pressure;
- forced movement;
- layered traps;
- protected setup actions;
- timed interceptions;
- controlled escape routes.

## 4.6.15 Team Combos Should Emerge From Timing

A combo is not simply a named damage multiplier.

A coordinated sequence should work because one action changes the state for the next.

Examples:
- water technique creates conductivity for Lightning Release;
- one ally forces a jump while another attacks the aerial path;
- one fighter pins a weapon while another strikes;
- smoke conceals an ally's repositioning;
- a barrier traps a target inside an area attack.

## 4.6.16 Threat Saturation

Threat saturation occurs when too many meaningful threats overlap for a defender to answer with their available awareness, movement, and reaction capacity.

It can result from:
- projectile volleys;
- coordinated attackers;
- traps plus direct attacks;
- large area control;
- clones or summons;
- environmental hazards.

Saturation should emerge from actual overlapping threats rather than a flat penalty.

## 4.6.17 Area Denial Creates Advantage Without Direct Contact

A character can control space by making certain areas too dangerous to enter.

Examples:
- fire;
- traps;
- explosive tags;
- poison clouds;
- sustained projectile fire;
- electrical hazards;
- summon reach.

Area denial can funnel opponents into more predictable paths.

## 4.6.18 Positional Leverage

A combatant has positional leverage when the battlefield gives them disproportionately strong control over likely enemy movement.

Examples:
- guarding the only doorway;
- standing above a narrow bridge;
- forcing an enemy against a wall;
- occupying safe high ground;
- controlling the only escape route.

Leverage disappears if the opponent gains a bypass method.

## 4.6.19 Objective Pressure

Combatants can create pressure by threatening something other than the opponent's body.

Examples:
- attacking a medic;
- threatening civilians;
- damaging a mission objective;
- approaching a seal;
- pursuing a messenger;
- cutting off retreat.

This forces opponents to spend actions protecting priorities.

## 4.6.20 Information Advantage

Knowing more than the opponent can create tactical leverage.

Examples:
- knowing their technique;
- knowing they are injured;
- understanding their preferred defense;
- knowing where traps are placed;
- knowing reinforcements are coming.

Information advantage should alter decision quality and available plans rather than grant automatic combat bonuses.

## 4.6.21 Concealed Capability

A character may deliberately hide:
- speed;
- chakra reserves;
- techniques;
- injuries;
- weapon reach;
- summons;
- traps.

Revealing a concealed capability at the right moment may create a stronger opening because the opponent planned around false assumptions.

## 4.6.22 Conditioning and Pattern Manipulation

A combatant may intentionally establish a pattern to exploit later.

Example:
- repeatedly attack high;
- encourage the defender to block high;
- later attack low.

This is stronger than a simple feint because it develops over several exchanges.

Pattern manipulation depends on:
- opponent learning;
- repetition;
- deception;
- timing;
- willingness to risk predictable behavior.

## 4.6.23 Overcommitment

An attacker can create their own vulnerability by committing too heavily.

Overcommitment may result from:
- high-force attacks;
- missed lunges;
- large jutsu;
- excessive pursuit;
- chasing through unsafe terrain;
- ignoring secondary threats.

Powerful attacks should often carry stronger potential consequences when they fail or are avoided.

## 4.6.24 Baiting Overcommitment

A defender may deliberately appear vulnerable to provoke an excessive response.

Examples:
- pretending to lose balance;
- exposing a target area;
- retreating toward a trap;
- feigning exhaustion.

If successful, the opponent voluntarily creates an opening.

## 4.6.25 Suppression

Suppression limits what an enemy can safely attempt.

Examples:
- constant projectile pressure;
- explosive tags preventing movement;
- attacks aimed at interrupting hand seals;
- sustained area techniques.

Suppression may not seek direct hits.

Its goal is to deny options.

## 4.6.26 Recovery Denial

An attacker can pressure an opponent specifically to prevent:
- regaining stance;
- forming seals;
- treating an injury;
- recovering chakra;
- repositioning;
- rejoining allies.

Recovery denial is especially valuable against techniques or fighters that need setup time.

## 4.6.27 Defensive Advantage

Advantage is not only offensive.

A defender may possess strong tactical leverage through:
- fortified position;
- prepared traps;
- superior cover;
- narrow approaches;
- protected retreat routes;
- hidden allies;
- knowledge of terrain.

An attacker may need to dismantle those advantages before direct assault becomes sensible.

## 4.6.28 Environmental Advantage

Weather and environment may favor certain tactics.

Examples:
- rain improving conductivity;
- darkness helping stealth;
- strong wind affecting projectiles;
- cramped interiors limiting large weapons;
- deep water helping Water Release users.

Environmental advantage should follow actual interaction rather than generic situational bonuses.

## 4.6.29 Advantage Is Perishable

Most tactical advantages disappear when the underlying condition changes.

Examples:
- flanking ends when the defender repositions;
- high ground loses value when the fight moves indoors;
- concealment ends when smoke clears;
- a trap advantage ends once discovered;
- pressure breaks when the attacker is interrupted.

Advantage should not persist merely because it was once earned.

## 4.6.30 Advantage Can Stack Through Different Constraints

Multiple advantages can combine when they affect different parts of the situation.

Example:
- the target is cornered;
- one exit is trapped;
- a teammate pressures from the front;
- smoke limits visibility.

The system should not simply sum four numeric bonuses.
It should determine how these conditions combine to reduce available responses.

## 4.6.31 Diminishing Returns on Redundant Advantage

Multiple advantages affecting the same limitation may add little.

Example:
- three separate effects all make retreat backward impossible.

Once backward retreat is already unavailable, additional effects denying the same route add little tactical value.

This prevents modifier inflation.

## 4.6.32 Tactical Advantage Can Replace Raw Power

A weaker character can threaten a stronger one by creating conditions that reduce the stronger character's usable advantages.

Examples:
- narrow terrain limiting speed;
- poison reducing endurance;
- traps restricting movement;
- hostage protection forcing defensive behavior;
- concealment denying line of sight;
- teamwork creating reaction conflicts.

This does not erase power disparity. It explains how preparation can matter.

## 4.6.33 Rank Does Not Grant Tactical Immunity

High-rank characters are usually better at:
- recognizing setups;
- avoiding traps;
- reading patterns;
- preserving escape routes;
- managing pressure;
- coordinating teams.

They are not immune to tactical disadvantage if the situation legitimately creates one.

## 4.6.34 Advantage and Ruleset 2

Ruleset 2 remains responsible for translating contextual advantage into probability and outcome degree.

Ruleset 4 supplies the actual context:
- reduced reaction window;
- lost movement route;
- divided attention;
- unstable footing;
- predictable defense;
- superior angle;
- protected setup.

Ruleset 2 should use those facts rather than receiving a generic "advantage token" whenever possible.

## 4.6.35 Compression

For low-detail combat, tactical state may be compressed into broad descriptors such as:
- dominant position;
- moderate pressure;
- surrounded;
- fortified;
- compromised defense.

These summaries must derive from actual battlefield conditions.

## 4.6.36 Minimum Tactical State

When relevant, the engine should be able to track:
- current tempo;
- active openings;
- constrained movement routes;
- flanking/blind-angle threats;
- objective pressure;
- prepared setups;
- known patterns;
- active suppression;
- major information asymmetries;
- team coordination state.

## Core Design Rule

Tactical advantage should primarily matter because it changes what the opponent can safely perceive, choose, reach, or respond to.

The system should prefer **concrete constraints and opportunities** over stacks of generic numerical bonuses.
