# 4.1 — Core Combat Philosophy

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 4 governs the physical and tactical consequences of combat after Ruleset 2 determines how an action resolves.

## Core Principles

1. **Combat is consequence-driven, not HP-driven.**
   Combat should primarily matter because attacks can cause meaningful injuries, impairment, positional loss, exhaustion, capture, or death. A large universal health pool should not be the main reason characters survive attacks.

2. **Resolution and consequence are separate.**
   Ruleset 2 determines whether and how well an attack, defense, contest, or maneuver resolves. Ruleset 4 translates that result into contact quality, physical effect, injury, status, displacement, or other combat consequences.

3. **Combat uses layered abstraction.**
   The engine should simulate only as much detail as the situation requires. Routine exchanges can remain abstract; important wounds, precise attacks, medical emergencies, and decisive moments may invoke more detailed simulation.

4. **The same underlying rules apply to PCs and NPCs.**
   Important NPCs do not receive artificial boss durability, and player characters do not receive hidden immunity from consequences. Exceptional survivability must come from actual attributes, skills, abilities, equipment, physiology, circumstances, or treatment.

5. **Power disparity matters without creating absolute immunity.**
   A vastly superior shinobi should dominate weaker opponents through speed, perception, technique, defense, durability, experience, and tactical options. However, weaker characters may still threaten stronger ones when circumstances create a legitimate opening.

6. **Successful attacks are not automatically identical in effect.**
   Outcome degree, attack type, force, target location, defense, armor, environment, and existing injuries can all change the consequence of a successful hit.

7. **Defense is active and contextual.**
   Avoidance, blocking, parrying, interception, substitution, defensive jutsu, movement, cover, and tactical preparation should matter. Defense should not collapse into one passive defense score.

8. **Speed influences tempo, not extra turns without limit.**
   Faster characters should gain meaningful advantages in initiative, positioning, interception, reaction windows, chaining actions, and denying openings, but the system should prevent speed from producing uncontrolled exponential action economy.

9. **Lethality comes from circumstances and anatomy, not arbitrary death rolls.**
   Death should normally emerge from sufficiently destructive injuries, catastrophic circumstances, accumulated trauma, or failed treatment rather than a detached random death check.

10. **Injury and combat capacity are distinct.**
    A character may suffer a serious injury while continuing to fight, or become incapacitated without having a life-threatening wound. Immediate function, medical severity, and long-term recovery are separate concepts.

11. **Medical consequences persist beyond the encounter.**
    Wounds, fatigue, blood loss, treatment, scars, rehabilitation, and permanent impairment may continue affecting a character after combat. Ending a fight does not reset the body.

12. **Information may be incomplete.**
    Characters should not automatically know an opponent's exact condition, remaining capability, hidden injury, chakra state, or medical danger. Observable symptoms and successful assessment determine what is known.

13. **Narrative description follows mechanics rather than replacing them.**
    The simulation may describe combat cinematically, but descriptions must remain consistent with the underlying resolved state. Narrative flair cannot secretly grant or remove mechanical effects.

14. **Exceptional abilities modify the shared system.**
    Regeneration, jinchūriki resilience, clan physiology, body modification, medical techniques, and similar abilities should alter normal injury and recovery rules through explicit exceptions rather than bypassing the combat model entirely.

15. **Important choices should create tradeoffs.**
    Aggressive attacks, defensive commitment, protecting allies, conserving chakra, pursuing a fleeing opponent, treating the wounded, taking prisoners, and using high-risk techniques should involve meaningful opportunity costs.

16. **Combat should remain playable.**
    The simulation should preserve Naruto-style tactical depth and physical consequences without requiring constant tracking of every bruise, heartbeat, milliliter of blood, or anatomical detail. Granularity increases only when it changes a meaningful outcome.

## Consequence Chain

The default conceptual chain is:

**Intent → Action → Resolution → Contact/Outcome Quality → Physical or Tactical Effect → Injury/Condition → Functional Consequence → Deterioration, Treatment, or Recovery**

Not every action must use every stage. The system may compress stages when no meaningful decision or uncertainty would be lost.

## Three Persistent Layers

Combat consequences should generally be represented through three distinct layers:

- **Combat Capacity:** what the character can currently do.
- **Injury State:** what physical damage actually exists.
- **Medical State:** whether that damage is stable, worsening, treated, healing, or producing complications.

These layers may interact, but they should not be collapsed into one number.

## Design Standard

A mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- exists only to protect an important character from a legitimate consequence;
- makes rank function as invulnerability rather than capability;
- duplicates a mechanic already owned by Rulesets 1–3;
- requires excessive bookkeeping for routine combat;
- allows narrative wording to override resolved state;
- makes medical treatment erase meaningful consequences without appropriate cost, skill, or limitation;
- produces obviously unrealistic Naruto-world outcomes solely because of a universal damage formula.
