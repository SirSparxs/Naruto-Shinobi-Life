# 4.10 — Injury System

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines how trauma becomes persistent bodily injury.

Ruleset 4.8 determines the physical exposure and trauma potential.
Ruleset 4.10 determines what injury actually exists afterward.

The injury system must preserve:
- location;
- tissue or structure affected;
- injury type;
- severity;
- functional effect;
- bleeding or other ongoing processes;
- stability;
- aggravation risk;
- treatment state;
- healing state;
- permanent consequences when applicable.

## 4.10.1 Injury Is a Persistent State

An injury remains part of the character until it:
- heals;
- is surgically repaired;
- is medically resolved;
- becomes a permanent condition;
- is otherwise altered by a specific ability.

Ending combat does not remove injury.

## 4.10.2 Injury Severity Is Not the Same as Trauma Potential

Ruleset 4.8 may produce:
- minor;
- moderate;
- severe;
- critical;
- catastrophic trauma potential.

The actual injury may fall within or near that range depending on:
- exact anatomy;
- penetration path;
- tissue struck;
- biological variation;
- prior injuries;
- mitigation;
- special physiology.

Trauma potential is the input.
Injury severity is the actual medical result.

## 4.10.3 Injury Severity Should Use Descriptive Bands

A useful general severity structure is:

### Minor
Limited tissue damage with little functional loss.

### Moderate
Meaningful injury that affects function or requires treatment but is not immediately life-threatening under normal circumstances.

### Serious
Substantial damage with major functional consequences, significant complication risk, or need for professional treatment.

### Critical
Immediately dangerous or unstable injury with significant risk of death, collapse, or permanent loss without timely treatment.

### Catastrophic
Extreme destruction of tissue or vital structures, potentially immediately fatal or permanently disabling even with treatment.

These bands summarize severity; they do not replace the actual injury record.

## 4.10.4 Medical Urgency Is Separate From Injury Severity

A severe injury may be stable.
A moderate injury may become urgent because of location or complication.

Each meaningful injury should therefore distinguish:
- structural severity;
- current medical urgency;
- current stability.

## 4.10.5 Injury Records Should Be Anatomically Grounded

A meaningful injury record should identify:
- body region;
- specific structure if relevant;
- injury mechanism;
- injury type;
- severity;
- functional impairment;
- ongoing effects;
- treatment status;
- healing status.

Example:

**Right Forearm**
- Deep laceration
- Flexor tendon partially severed
- Moderate arterial bleeding
- Serious severity
- Grip weakened
- Fine finger control impaired
- Untreated

## 4.10.6 Broad Injuries Can Remain Broad

Not every injury needs organ-level detail.

Examples:
- superficial bruising;
- minor forearm cut;
- strained ankle;
- mild burn.

The simulation should only refine anatomy when the distinction changes:
- combat function;
- treatment;
- prognosis;
- deterioration;
- permanent outcome.

## 4.10.7 Soft-Tissue Injuries

Soft-tissue injuries include:
- bruising;
- contusion;
- strain;
- sprain;
- muscle tear;
- tendon injury;
- ligament injury;
- laceration;
- puncture.

These may affect:
- strength;
- range of motion;
- precision;
- stability;
- pain;
- bleeding.

## 4.10.8 Bruising and Contusion

Blunt trauma may cause:
- localized pain;
- swelling;
- reduced motion;
- deeper muscle injury.

Minor bruising may have little mechanical relevance.
Severe contusion may significantly impair a limb or indicate deeper trauma.

## 4.10.9 Muscle Injury

Muscle injuries may range from:
- strain;
- partial tear;
- major tear;
- rupture.

Possible consequences include:
- reduced force;
- pain on movement;
- weakness;
- reduced range;
- inability to perform explosive actions.

## 4.10.10 Tendon and Ligament Injury

Tendon and ligament damage may create disproportionate functional loss.

Examples:
- damaged finger tendon limiting seals;
- torn knee ligament destabilizing movement;
- Achilles-type injury preventing normal sprinting.

These injuries may be less visually dramatic than large wounds while being tactically severe.

## 4.10.11 Lacerations and Open Wounds

Open wounds may involve:
- skin;
- muscle;
- tendon;
- nerve;
- vessel;
- deeper structures.

Important properties include:
- depth;
- length;
- location;
- contamination;
- bleeding;
- tissue loss.

## 4.10.12 Puncture and Penetrating Wounds

Penetrating wounds require attention to:
- entry point;
- depth;
- path;
- structures crossed;
- retained object;
- exit wound;
- internal bleeding;
- contamination.

The visible wound may underestimate severity.

## 4.10.13 Fractures

Fractures may be:
- hairline/stress;
- simple;
- displaced;
- comminuted;
- open;
- crush-related.

Possible effects include:
- pain;
- instability;
- inability to bear weight;
- reduced force;
- risk to nearby nerves or vessels;
- further damage if used.

Exact fracture terminology should be used only when it changes mechanics.

## 4.10.14 Joint Injuries

Joint injuries may involve:
- dislocation;
- cartilage damage;
- ligament injury;
- fracture involving the joint.

Consequences may include:
- instability;
- restricted range;
- inability to bear force;
- repeated failure under stress.

## 4.10.15 Head Injury

Head injuries may include:
- scalp wound;
- concussion;
- skull fracture;
- intracranial injury;
- brain trauma.

Possible functional effects include:
- disorientation;
- memory problems;
- poor balance;
- nausea;
- loss of consciousness;
- impaired judgment;
- seizures;
- neurological deficit.

The simulation should avoid overdiagnosing exact brain pathology without sufficient medical assessment.

## 4.10.16 Neck and Spine Injury

Injury may involve:
- soft tissue;
- airway;
- vessels;
- vertebrae;
- spinal cord;
- peripheral nerves.

Possible consequences include:
- pain;
- restricted movement;
- breathing risk;
- paralysis;
- catastrophic neurological injury.

Spinal damage should be treated as high consequence but should not be assumed from every neck impact.

## 4.10.17 Chest Injury

Chest trauma may affect:
- ribs;
- sternum;
- lungs;
- heart;
- major vessels.

Possible consequences include:
- pain with breathing;
- reduced ventilation;
- internal bleeding;
- lung collapse;
- cardiac injury.

Exact internal diagnosis may remain hidden until medically assessed.

## 4.10.18 Abdominal and Pelvic Injury

Abdominal trauma may involve:
- liver;
- spleen;
- kidneys;
- bowel;
- bladder;
- major vessels;
- pelvis.

Possible consequences include:
- internal bleeding;
- pain;
- impaired movement;
- delayed deterioration;
- infection risk after penetrating injury.

## 4.10.19 Eye Injury

Eye injury may range from:
- irritation;
- corneal damage;
- blunt trauma;
- penetrating injury;
- globe destruction;
- optic nerve injury.

Consequences can include:
- blurred vision;
- partial loss;
- complete loss;
- dōjutsu impairment where relevant.

## 4.10.20 Ear and Balance Injury

Ear injury may affect:
- hearing;
- equilibrium;
- orientation.

Even nonlethal damage can create major combat impairment.

## 4.10.21 Nerve Injury

Nerve damage may cause:
- numbness;
- weakness;
- impaired coordination;
- partial paralysis;
- complete loss of function;
- neuropathic pain.

Nerve injury may persist after visible wounds close.

## 4.10.22 Vascular Injury

Damage to blood vessels may produce:
- external bleeding;
- internal bleeding;
- impaired circulation;
- rapidly worsening shock.

Vascular injury severity depends strongly on:
- vessel size;
- location;
- whether bleeding is controlled.

Detailed deterioration belongs to 4.11.

## 4.10.23 Organ Injury

Internal organs may be:
- bruised;
- lacerated;
- punctured;
- ruptured;
- crushed.

Organ-level detail is generated only when it materially affects:
- deterioration;
- treatment;
- prognosis;
- function.

## 4.10.24 Burns

Burns should account for:
- depth;
- body surface area;
- location;
- airway involvement;
- continuing heat source.

Consequences may include:
- pain;
- tissue loss;
- fluid loss;
- infection risk;
- impaired movement;
- scarring.

The system should avoid requiring exact real-world burn-percentage calculations unless medically consequential.

## 4.10.25 Electrical Injury

Electrical injuries may include:
- localized burns;
- nerve disruption;
- muscle injury;
- temporary paralysis;
- cardiac effects;
- sensory impairment.

Visible burn severity may not represent internal effect.

## 4.10.26 Cold Injury

Cold-related injury may include:
- numbness;
- frost-like tissue damage;
- reduced dexterity;
- systemic cooling.

The injury record should distinguish temporary cold impairment from actual tissue injury.

## 4.10.27 Blast Injury

Blast injury may combine:
- pressure injury;
- hearing damage;
- blunt trauma;
- fragmentation wounds;
- burns;
- structural collapse injuries.

These components should remain separable when consequences differ.

## 4.10.28 Chakra-Network Injury

Chakra-network injuries may involve:
- blocked flow;
- damaged pathways;
- disrupted tenketsu;
- localized control loss;
- reduced technique reliability;
- pain or functional interference.

These injuries may coexist with normal tissue injuries.

Medical-ninjutsu interaction will be developed later.

## 4.10.29 Poison and Chemical Injury

Poisons and toxins may create physiological effects without an initial wound beyond the delivery site.

The injury/state record may include:
- toxin identity if known;
- route;
- dose estimate;
- onset;
- current systemic effects.

Detailed poison rules belong to 4.22.

## 4.10.30 Functional Effects Should Be Specific

An injury should impair what the damaged structure actually supports.

Examples:
- injured wrist affects grip and seals;
- knee injury affects sprinting and turning;
- rib injury affects deep breathing and explosive exertion;
- eye injury affects depth perception and targeting.

Avoid generic “injured: -10 to all actions.”

## 4.10.31 Severity and Function Can Diverge

A medically moderate hand injury can be tactically devastating for a seal-dependent shinobi.

A serious torso wound may initially have relatively little combat impairment.

Therefore:
- medical severity;
- functional severity;
- combat impact

must remain distinguishable.

## 4.10.32 Hidden Injuries

Some injuries may not be immediately obvious.

Examples:
- internal bleeding;
- concussion;
- organ injury;
- small fracture;
- nerve damage;
- chakra-pathway injury.

The engine may know the injury exists while the character only knows symptoms.

## 4.10.33 Uncertain Injuries

Sometimes even the simulation may preserve uncertainty until additional information is generated.

Example:
- severe abdominal trauma with possible organ involvement.

Medical assessment can later refine the record.

This can be useful when exact internal anatomy was not necessary at the moment of impact.

## 4.10.34 Immediate and Delayed Effects

Injuries may produce:
- immediate impairment;
- delayed swelling;
- delayed bleeding;
- progressive neurological symptoms;
- infection;
- delayed tissue failure.

Not every consequence needs to appear at the moment of impact.

## 4.10.35 Aggravation

Using an injured structure can worsen it.

Examples:
- running on a damaged knee;
- blocking with a fractured forearm;
- continuing seals with torn fingers;
- exerting with rib or lung injury.

Aggravation should depend on:
- action intensity;
- injury type;
- stabilization;
- repeated use.

## 4.10.36 Reinjury

Partially healed tissue may remain vulnerable.

A later attack or excessive exertion can:
- reopen wounds;
- refracture bone;
- tear healing tissue;
- restart bleeding;
- extend recovery.

Recovery state therefore matters in later combat.

## 4.10.37 Injury Stacking

Multiple injuries to the same body region should not simply add severity numbers.

They may:
- remain separate;
- interact;
- merge into a more complex injury;
- worsen function multiplicatively.

Example:
A cut and a fracture in the same forearm create different problems that may interact.

## 4.10.38 Bilateral Injury

Damage to both sides of the body can create greater functional consequences.

Examples:
- both hands injured;
- both legs compromised;
- vision lost in one eye plus severe injury to the other.

The system should recognize combined functional dependence.

## 4.10.39 Permanent Injury

Permanent consequences may result when tissue cannot fully recover.

Examples:
- lost limb;
- destroyed eye;
- permanent nerve injury;
- severe scarring;
- chronic joint instability;
- permanent chakra-network damage.

Permanent outcomes should arise from actual injury severity, treatment limits, complications, and exceptional physiology.

## 4.10.40 Permanent Does Not Mean Untreatable in All Contexts

Naruto's world includes:
- advanced medical ninjutsu;
- prosthetics;
- transplants;
- body modification;
- regeneration;
- specialized techniques.

A permanent natural injury may later be compensated for or altered by another system.

The original injury still matters historically.

## 4.10.41 Scarring

Significant skin and soft-tissue injury may leave scars.

Scarring may be:
- cosmetic;
- restrictive;
- sensory;
- socially meaningful.

Most scars need no combat effect.

## 4.10.42 Chronic Consequences

Some injuries may heal incompletely and create:
- chronic pain;
- reduced range of motion;
- weakness;
- recurring instability;
- sensory loss;
- reduced endurance.

These consequences may become persistent character state.

## 4.10.43 Infection and Complications

Open wounds, burns, surgery, and poor treatment may create complications.

Possible complications include:
- infection;
- delayed healing;
- tissue death;
- worsening systemic illness.

Detailed disease mechanics should remain lightweight unless relevant.

## 4.10.44 Stabilized Injury

An injury can be stabilized without being healed.

Stabilization may mean:
- bleeding controlled;
- fracture immobilized;
- airway secured;
- dangerous movement stopped;
- toxin progression slowed.

This distinction is essential for battlefield medicine.

## 4.10.45 Treated Injury

Treatment status may include:
- untreated;
- improvised first aid;
- stabilized;
- professionally treated;
- surgically repaired;
- medically healed/accelerated;
- rehabilitating.

Treatment state modifies deterioration and recovery but does not erase history.

## 4.10.46 Healing State

An injury may progress through broad healing phases such as:
- acute;
- stabilized;
- early healing;
- recovering;
- rehabilitating;
- healed;
- healed with lasting consequence.

Exact biology can remain abstract unless required.

## 4.10.47 Injury Knowledge Is Per-Observer

The engine may know:
- exact injury;
- hidden internal state.

The injured character may know:
- pain;
- weakness;
- visible wound.

A medic may know more after assessment.

Other combatants may only infer from behavior.

Injury information should follow Ruleset 2 information principles.

## 4.10.48 Injury Records Must Support Medicine

A medically meaningful injury record should provide enough information for later systems to answer:
- what needs treatment;
- how urgent it is;
- what treatment is possible;
- what happens if untreated;
- how long recovery may take;
- what complications are possible.

## 4.10.49 Injury Records Must Support Combat

The same record must also answer:
- what movements are impaired;
- what techniques are restricted;
- what body regions are vulnerable;
- what actions risk aggravation;
- what pain or sensory effects exist.

## 4.10.50 Injury Generation Pipeline

For a consequential trauma record:

1. Read affected body region and mechanism.
2. Read trauma severity potential from 4.8.
3. Identify structures plausibly exposed.
4. Resolve any remaining anatomical uncertainty.
5. Generate the actual injury or injuries.
6. Determine structural severity.
7. Determine functional effects.
8. Determine bleeding or ongoing processes.
9. Determine current stability and urgency.
10. Record hidden versus observable information.
11. Feed functional effects into 4.9 combat capacity.
12. Feed ongoing risks into 4.11 deterioration.
13. Feed treatment needs into later medical sections.

## 4.10.51 Injury Compression

For low-detail combat, injury records may remain broad.

Examples:
- minor bruising;
- moderate leg wound;
- serious torso injury;
- severe burns.

If the injury later becomes narratively, medically, or mechanically important, the simulation can refine it into more specific anatomy consistent with the original trauma.

## 4.10.52 No Random Injury Table Without Context

The system should not use a generic table where the same attack can randomly produce unrelated injuries.

Injury generation should be constrained by:
- mechanism;
- location;
- severity;
- attack path;
- mitigation.

Randomness may choose among plausible outcomes, not physically unrelated ones.

## 4.10.53 No Automatic Healing Between Scenes

An injury persists according to actual treatment and recovery time.

Time skips must advance:
- healing;
- deterioration;
- complications;
- rehabilitation

rather than erase injury by scene transition.

## 4.10.54 Minimum Injury Record

A meaningful injury should be able to store:

- body region;
- specific structure if needed;
- injury type;
- structural severity;
- functional effect;
- bleeding/ongoing effect;
- medical urgency;
- stability;
- treatment state;
- healing state;
- aggravation risk;
- permanence or long-term consequence;
- information visibility.

## Core Design Rule

An injury should answer three questions:

**What structure was damaged?**
**How does that damage affect function right now?**
**What will happen medically if it is left alone, treated, aggravated, or allowed to heal?**

The injury record is the persistent bridge between combat and medicine.
