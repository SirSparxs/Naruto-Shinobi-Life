# 4.15 — Grappling, Restraint & Capture

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

This section defines grabs, clinches, takedowns, throws, pins, limb control, restraint, escapes, and live capture.

It should preserve the broader Ruleset 4 principles:
- combat is consequence-driven;
- positioning and timing matter;
- control is specific rather than binary;
- stronger characters are not automatically immune to leverage or restraint;
- weaker characters should not overpower stronger ones without a plausible positional or technical advantage;
- capture should be more difficult than simply inflicting lethal harm.

## 4.15.1 Grappling Is a Control Relationship

A grapple exists when one combatant establishes direct physical control over another.

The important questions are:
- what body part is controlled;
- how strong the control is;
- who has leverage;
- what posture each character has;
- which limbs remain free;
- whether either character can move;
- what escape routes exist.

“Grappled” alone is not enough information.

## 4.15.2 Grappling Has Degrees of Control

Useful broad states may include:

### Contact
Physical contact exists, but neither side has meaningful control.

### Grip Established
One character controls a limb, clothing, weapon, or body position.

### Clinch
Both are in close mutual control and movement is constrained.

### Dominant Control
One character has superior leverage, posture, or limb control.

### Pin
One character is substantially immobilized by body position, terrain, or external force.

### Fully Restrained
Movement needed for ordinary resistance is effectively prevented.

These are descriptive control states, not universal modifiers.

## 4.15.3 Control Is Body-Part Specific

A character may control:
- wrist;
- elbow;
- shoulder;
- torso;
- neck;
- leg;
- weapon arm;
- clothing;
- entire body position.

Controlling one wrist does not automatically control the rest of the body.

The simulation should track only the control points that matter.

## 4.15.4 Free Limbs Remain Usable

A restrained arm does not remove:
- the other arm;
- legs;
- head movement;
- speech;
- chakra control;
- techniques compatible with the remaining freedom.

A character should retain every option the actual position still allows.

## 4.15.5 Grappling Is Strongly Positional

Effectiveness depends on:
- stance;
- center of gravity;
- footing;
- wall or ground contact;
- elevation;
- nearby obstacles;
- available space;
- body orientation.

A physically weaker fighter may gain control through superior leverage.
A physically stronger fighter may break poor control through raw force.

## 4.15.6 Strength Matters, But It Is Not Everything

Strength influences:
- grip breaking;
- lifting;
- resisting forced movement;
- maintaining crushing control;
- overpowering poor leverage.

Other relevant factors include:
- Agility;
- Endurance;
- technique;
- balance;
- leverage;
- body size;
- injuries;
- surprise;
- current fatigue.

Ruleset 2 resolves uncertain contests using the actual relevant factors.

## 4.15.7 Skill Can Convert Position Into Control

A skilled grappler can exploit:
- joint alignment;
- balance;
- momentum;
- posture;
- leverage;
- timing.

This allows technique to compensate for some raw Strength difference without making Strength irrelevant.

## 4.15.8 Size and Mass Matter Contextually

Larger mass can help with:
- resisting displacement;
- maintaining top pressure;
- carrying or dragging;
- absorbing collisions.

Smaller size may help with:
- slipping through space;
- changing angle;
- avoiding certain grips.

Size should matter through actual mechanics rather than a universal size bonus.

## 4.15.9 Entry Into a Grapple

A grapple usually requires closing distance and establishing control.

Possible entries include:
- catching a limb;
- body lock;
- tackle;
- clinch;
- intercepting a weapon arm;
- grabbing during a failed attack;
- using terrain to trap movement.

The entry itself can be opposed or interrupted.

## 4.15.10 Grappling Creates Mutual Risk

Closing into physical control often reduces space for both combatants.

Possible consequences include:
- reduced weapon freedom;
- restricted dodging;
- easier access to throws;
- exposure to hidden weapons;
- reduced visibility of other threats.

A grappler may gain control over one opponent while becoming vulnerable to another.

## 4.15.11 Takedowns

A takedown attempts to remove stable footing and move the opponent to the ground or another disadvantaged surface.

It may rely on:
- leverage;
- momentum;
- sweep;
- trip;
- body rotation;
- impact;
- terrain.

A successful takedown changes posture and position.
It does not automatically incapacitate.

## 4.15.12 Throws

A throw intentionally redirects the opponent's body through space.

Potential consequences include:
- knockdown;
- collision;
- forced movement;
- separation;
- loss of weapon;
- injury if impact is severe.

The damage model applies only when the resulting impact is physically significant.

## 4.15.13 Pins

A pin uses:
- body weight;
- leverage;
- terrain;
- limb control;
- external objects

to prevent meaningful movement.

A pin should specify:
- which limbs are trapped;
- which directions are blocked;
- what actions remain possible;
- what would be required to escape.

## 4.15.14 Joint Control

Joint-based control can threaten:
- pain;
- loss of movement;
- dislocation;
- ligament injury;
- fracture if excessive force is applied.

The purpose may be:
- restraint;
- compliance;
- disarmament;
- forced movement.

If the hold is pushed beyond safe control, it transitions into injury resolution.

## 4.15.15 Choke and Neck Control

Neck control may be used to:
- restrict movement;
- disrupt posture;
- threaten breathing or circulation;
- force surrender.

Because the consequences can become medically dangerous, prolonged or severe neck control should transition into the respiratory/circulatory systems from 4.11 and 4.12 rather than use a generic “choke meter.”

## 4.15.16 Pain Compliance

Some restraints rely primarily on pain and threat of injury.

Pain compliance may work poorly against:
- extremely determined opponents;
- reduced pain perception;
- berserk states;
- characters willing to accept injury.

Therefore, pain-based control is not equivalent to mechanical immobilization.

## 4.15.17 Weapon Control

A grappler may attack:
- wrist;
- hand;
- weapon arm;
- weapon itself.

Possible results include:
- redirecting the weapon;
- trapping it;
- preventing a strike;
- disarming;
- turning the weapon toward the wielder;
- creating an opening.

Weapon control should depend on actual grip and leverage.

## 4.15.18 Disarmament

A disarm can result from:
- grip failure;
- wrist control;
- impact;
- leverage;
- weapon binding;
- pain;
- deliberate release.

The weapon then becomes a battlefield object whose location persists.

## 4.15.19 Grappling and Hand Seals

Grappling may interfere with hand seals by:
- trapping one or both hands;
- occupying the hands with grips;
- disrupting posture;
- forcing rapid defense.

A character with one-hand seals, seal-less techniques, or alternative casting methods may remain dangerous.

Ruleset 3 determines technique requirements.

## 4.15.20 Grappling and Chakra Techniques

Close control does not automatically stop chakra use.

A restrained character may still use:
- techniques requiring no free limbs;
- body-based chakra enhancement;
- some sensory techniques;
- specific escape jutsu.

The restraint only limits what it physically or mechanically prevents.

## 4.15.21 Grappling and Substitution

Substitution or escape techniques are possible only if:
- the technique's requirements are met;
- timing allows execution;
- the restraint does not prevent required actions;
- the user has a valid positional outcome.

Grappling should not be automatically defeated by vague “teleport away” logic.

## 4.15.22 Grappling and Clones

Clones can complicate control by:
- adding bodies;
- breaking holds;
- creating reaction conflicts;
- replacing a target where technique rules permit.

Their action-economy limits still apply.

## 4.15.23 Multiple Grapplers

Several characters can combine control.

Advantages may include:
- controlling more limbs;
- restricting escape routes;
- distributing effort;
- maintaining restraint while another applies bindings.

Poor coordination may also:
- interfere with grips;
- create openings;
- obstruct techniques.

## 4.15.24 Team Capture

A trained team may separate roles such as:
- pressure;
- limb control;
- binding;
- overwatch;
- medical monitoring;
- escape denial.

Capture becomes safer when these roles are coordinated.

## 4.15.25 Restraint Tools

Physical restraint may involve:
- rope;
- wire;
- chains;
- cuffs;
- cloth;
- specialized shinobi bindings;
- sealing tools.

Each restraint should define:
- what it controls;
- strength/durability;
- application requirements;
- escape methods;
- whether chakra use is restricted.

## 4.15.26 Sealing Restraints

Sealing-based restraint may interfere with:
- movement;
- chakra flow;
- technique use;
- specific body parts.

The seal's actual behavior belongs to Ruleset 3.
Ruleset 4 tracks the resulting combat restrictions.

## 4.15.27 Terrain-Based Restraint

Characters may be trapped by:
- debris;
- earth;
- ice;
- walls;
- collapsed structures;
- narrow terrain.

The condition may resemble a pin or immobilization even without another combatant maintaining it.

## 4.15.28 Maintaining Restraint Requires Commitment

Holding someone may consume:
- attention;
- limbs;
- stance;
- movement;
- physical effort.

A character restraining one opponent may be less able to:
- defend against others;
- move quickly;
- form seals;
- use weapons.

Control therefore creates opportunity cost.

## 4.15.29 Grappling Is Physically Fatiguing

Maintaining strong control can accumulate fatigue, especially when:
- the opponent actively resists;
- the size difference is unfavorable;
- the hold is prolonged;
- footing is poor.

This directly interfaces with 4.13.

## 4.15.30 Escape Attempts

Escape may involve:
- breaking grip;
- changing angle;
- slipping a limb free;
- reversing leverage;
- attacking the controller;
- using terrain;
- using a technique;
- receiving allied intervention.

The correct defense depends on the actual control state.

## 4.15.31 Escape Is Not Always One Contest

A complex restraint may require several positional improvements.

Example:
- free wrist;
- recover posture;
- create space;
- break clinch.

The system can compress this if the outcome is obvious.

## 4.15.32 Reversal

A defender may turn an escape into control of their own.

A reversal should emerge from:
- timing;
- leverage;
- opponent overcommitment;
- superior technique.

It should not be a generic critical-success effect.

## 4.15.33 Restraint Can Be Partial

A character may be:
- one arm restrained;
- legs bound;
- torso pinned;
- weapon arm trapped;
- hands secured while still mobile.

Partial restraint is often more interesting than binary immobilization.

## 4.15.34 Capture Is an Objective, Not a Damage Type

Capturing an opponent alive usually requires:
- reducing their escape options;
- controlling limbs;
- limiting chakra use;
- applying restraints;
- keeping them medically alive.

There is no “capture damage.”

## 4.15.35 Capture Is Usually Harder Than Lethal Force

A captor may need to:
- avoid vital anatomy;
- avoid excessive force;
- prevent escape;
- manage hidden weapons;
- prevent suicide or self-harm where relevant;
- monitor dangerous injuries;
- transport the target.

This should make live-capture missions meaningfully difficult.

## 4.15.36 Surrender and Capture Are Different

A surrendered character may cooperate without being physically restrained.

However, captors may still choose to:
- disarm;
- bind;
- search;
- suppress chakra.

Surrender changes intent and resistance, not physical capability.

## 4.15.37 Incapacitation Before Capture

Some captures occur after:
- knockout;
- exhaustion;
- paralysis;
- injury;
- poison.

The captor must still consider whether the target:
- may recover;
- can still use techniques;
- requires treatment.

## 4.15.38 Restraint After Incapacitation

A knocked-out target should not remain permanently safe simply because they are unconscious.

If capture matters, the captor should secure them before recovery.

This creates a distinction between:
- defeated;
- incapacitated;
- secured.

## 4.15.39 Searching a Captive

A secured captive may still possess:
- hidden weapons;
- explosive tags;
- poison;
- communication tools;
- sealing items.

Search effectiveness belongs to perception/information rules.
Ruleset 4 tracks what remains physically accessible.

## 4.15.40 Transporting Captives

Transport may require:
- escort;
- carrying;
- walking restraint;
- specialized bindings;
- medical attention.

Injured captives can deteriorate during transport.

Capture therefore connects to fatigue, medicine, and mission logistics.

## 4.15.41 Captive Escape

Escape opportunities may arise from:
- damaged restraints;
- distracted guards;
- poor knots or seals;
- hidden tools;
- allies;
- terrain;
- recovery from impairment.

The simulation should preserve restraint condition over time rather than treat capture as permanent state.

## 4.15.42 Nonlethal Failure

A capture attempt can accidentally cause serious or fatal injury through:
- excessive force;
- dangerous fall;
- prolonged respiratory restriction;
- poorly controlled weapon use;
- aggravation of existing injuries.

Intent does not guarantee safety.

## 4.15.43 Grappling Against Extreme Strength

If raw physical strength is overwhelmingly different, some forms of control may become unsustainable.

A weaker grappler may still succeed through:
- superior leverage;
- joint control;
- restraint tools;
- terrain;
- teamwork;
- chakra-assisted technique.

But the system should not let ordinary grips ignore massive strength disparity without justification.

## 4.15.44 Grappling Against Extreme Speed

Speed is most valuable before stable control is established.

Once bodies are tightly connected, acceleration and free movement are reduced.

However, speed still matters for:
- entries;
- grip changes;
- reversals;
- escape timing;
- attacking exposed limbs.

This prevents speed from functioning as automatic grapple immunity.

## 4.15.45 Grappling Against Unusual Physiology

Characters with:
- extra limbs;
- extreme flexibility;
- body transformation;
- unusual joints;
- regeneration;
- nonhuman anatomy

may require modified control rules.

Their ability record should explicitly describe relevant differences.

## 4.15.46 Injury During Grappling

Existing injuries can affect:
- grip strength;
- balance;
- ability to resist force;
- safe range of motion.

Grappling can also aggravate injuries through:
- twisting;
- compression;
- forced weight bearing.

## 4.15.47 Grappling Information

Combatants do not automatically know:
- opponent's hidden weapon;
- joint injury;
- unusual flexibility;
- concealed escape technique.

Information and experience therefore matter strongly in close control.

## 4.15.48 Grappling State Record

For consequential grappling, the engine should be able to track:
- who controls whom;
- controlled body parts;
- posture;
- leverage advantage;
- free limbs;
- weapon access;
- mobility remaining;
- restraint source;
- escape options;
- fatigue cost;
- injury risk;
- nearby third parties.

## 4.15.49 Suggested Grappling Pipeline

1. Establish contact or entry.
2. Determine attempted control point.
3. Determine available defense or counter.
4. Ruleset 2 resolves uncertain control.
5. Record resulting grip, posture, and leverage.
6. Determine remaining actions and movement.
7. Resolve escape, reversal, takedown, pin, strike, or restraint attempts.
8. Apply fatigue and injury consequences where relevant.
9. Update control state after every major positional change.
10. End the grapple when stable physical control no longer exists.

## 4.15.50 Compression

Routine grappling can be summarized when the disparity is overwhelming.

Examples:
- elite shinobi restrains untrained civilian;
- two guards secure an unconscious prisoner.

The system should zoom in when:
- escape is plausible;
- hidden weapons matter;
- restraint method matters;
- injury risk matters;
- the target has unusual techniques.

## Core Design Rule

Grappling should represent **specific physical control over specific parts of the body and position**.

Capture should result from:
**control + restraint + escape denial + continued security**

—not from reducing a special capture meter to zero.
