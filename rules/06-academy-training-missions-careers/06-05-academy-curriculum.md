# 6.5 — Academy Curriculum

**Ruleset:** 6 — Academy, Training, Missions & Careers  
**Status:** Provisional

## 6.5.1 — Purpose

The Academy curriculum defines what students are exposed to, taught, practiced, and expected to understand before graduation.

Curriculum establishes:

- Subject areas
- Instructional sequence
- Required competencies
- Practical exposure
- Safety limits
- Local doctrinal priorities
- Electives and advanced opportunities

Curriculum does not directly grant Attributes, Skills, or techniques. It creates structured opportunities for learning and practice, which other rulesets resolve.

---

## 6.5.2 — Curriculum Is Competency-Based

The Academy should teach toward practical competencies rather than merely requiring completion of named classes.

A student may complete a course while still being weak in its subject.

Conversely, a student with substantial prior training may already exceed the curriculum.

The engine should distinguish:

- Exposure
- Instruction received
- Practice completed
- Demonstrated competency
- Formal completion

These are not identical.

---

## 6.5.3 — Core vs Local Curriculum

Each Academy curriculum contains two layers:

### Core Shinobi Foundation
Subjects broadly necessary for functioning as a professional shinobi.

### Local Curriculum
Subjects emphasized because of:

- Village doctrine
- Geography
- Climate
- Military history
- Available instructors
- Clan influence
- Local enemies
- Political culture
- Current leadership
- Wartime needs

A desert village, island village, forest village, and mountain village should not train identically.

---

## 6.5.4 — Curriculum Progression

Curriculum should progress from:

1. Foundational literacy and physical readiness
2. Basic shinobi knowledge
3. Controlled practical training
4. Applied teamwork and fieldcraft
5. Integrated mission-like exercises
6. Graduation-level competency

Advanced content should generally build on prior foundations rather than appearing because the student reached a certain age.

---

## 6.5.5 — General Academics

Academy students should receive ordinary academic education sufficient for professional life.

Possible areas include:

- Reading
- Writing
- Arithmetic
- Geography
- History
- Civics
- Basic science
- Recordkeeping

Shinobi still need to read orders, calculate distance, interpret maps, write reports, understand contracts, and communicate clearly.

Academic weakness can therefore have real career consequences even for physically talented students.

---

## 6.5.6 — Shinobi History and Military History

Students may study:

- Village founding
- Major wars
- Famous missions
- Alliances
- Historic enemies
- Tactical failures
- Important shinobi
- Institutional development

The content may contain bias.

Different villages can teach the same historical event differently.

The simulator should distinguish historical truth from institutional curriculum.

---

## 6.5.7 — Civics, Law, and Chain of Command

Students should learn the rules governing shinobi service.

Possible topics include:

- Village authority
- Mission authorization
- Chain of command
- Client obligations
- Confidentiality
- Classified information
- Civilian protection
- Property restrictions
- Rules regarding prisoners
- Desertion
- Unauthorized violence
- Inter-village law and treaties

Exact doctrine varies by village and era.

---

## 6.5.8 — Professional Conduct

Academies may teach expected behavior such as:

- Following lawful orders
- Mission discipline
- Team responsibility
- Reporting requirements
- Handling classified information
- Interacting with clients
- Protecting village interests
- Conduct around civilians

The institution's ideals and actual behavior may differ.

---

## 6.5.9 — Physical Conditioning

Physical education develops the foundations needed for later shinobi activity.

Training may include:

- Running
- Sprinting
- Climbing
- Balance
- Mobility
- Strength exercises
- Endurance work
- Flexibility
- Coordination

Ruleset 1 governs actual Attribute and Skill growth.

Academy conditioning supplies structured training opportunities.

---

## 6.5.10 — Movement Fundamentals

Students should learn practical body movement before advanced combat.

Examples include:

- Falling safely
- Rolling
- Landing
- Balance
- Direction changes
- Obstacle movement
- Basic evasive movement
- Safe climbing
- Body positioning

These fundamentals support later taijutsu, stealth, and mobility skills.

---

## 6.5.11 — Taijutsu Fundamentals

Academy-level taijutsu should emphasize:

- Stance
- Guard
- Distance
- Footwork
- Basic strikes
- Basic blocks
- Grappling awareness
- Controlled sparring
- Safe disengagement

The Academy is not expected to produce taijutsu masters.

Its goal is baseline competence and a foundation for later development.

---

## 6.5.12 — Weapons Safety and Handling

Students should learn safe and competent handling of common shinobi tools.

Possible equipment includes:

- Kunai
- Shuriken
- Training blades
- Wire
- Basic traps
- Utility tools

Instruction should include:

- Safety
- Grip
- Carrying
- Maintenance
- Throwing fundamentals
- Range judgment
- Controlled use

Advanced weapon specialization occurs later unless the Academy or student has unusual access.

---

## 6.5.13 — Chakra Theory

Students should learn conceptual foundations such as:

- What chakra is
- Physical and spiritual energy
- Chakra circulation
- Chakra expenditure
- Basic molding concepts
- Exhaustion risks
- Control versus reserves
- General nature transformation theory where age-appropriate

Ruleset 3 owns the deeper chakra system.

Academy instruction provides the student's theoretical exposure.

---

## 6.5.14 — Chakra Control Foundations

Practical chakra education should develop basic control.

Exercises may include age- and stage-appropriate versions of:

- Chakra awareness
- Controlled output
- Sustained output
- Precision exercises
- Simple concentration drills

Higher-level exercises such as tree climbing or water walking should appear only where appropriate to the local curriculum and student level.

The Academy should not automatically grant Basic Chakra Control merely for attendance.

---

## 6.5.15 — Ninjutsu Fundamentals

Academies may teach a small collection of foundational techniques.

The exact list is village- and era-specific.

Konoha-style programs may include techniques such as:

- Transformation
- Clone Technique
- Substitution-related fundamentals

Other villages may prioritize different techniques.

The important rule is:

> Academy techniques should serve foundational professional competencies rather than act as a universal spell list.

Ruleset 3 determines prerequisites, execution, chakra cost, and mastery.

---

## 6.5.16 — Hand Seals and Technique Procedure

Students should learn:

- Common hand seals
- Seal recognition
- Sequence accuracy
- Coordination
- Safe practice
- Technique preparation discipline

Handsign speed is a developed Skill, not something automatically granted by learning seal names.

---

## 6.5.17 — Genjutsu Awareness

Even where students are not taught meaningful offensive genjutsu, they should receive basic education about:

- What genjutsu is
- Common warning signs
- Basic disruption concepts
- Avoiding obvious triggers
- Reporting suspected manipulation

Academy education should prepare students to survive encounters with abilities they cannot personally use.

---

## 6.5.18 — Stealth and Concealment

Students may learn:

- Quiet movement
- Visual concealment
- Cover and shadow use
- Basic camouflage
- Noise discipline
- Concealing equipment
- Avoiding obvious tracks

Local terrain should influence emphasis.

---

## 6.5.19 — Observation and Perception

Training may include:

- Noticing environmental changes
- Identifying suspicious behavior
- Recognizing tracks
- Detecting ambush indicators
- Remembering visual details
- Basic surveillance awareness

This should train relevant Skills rather than provide a flat "shinobi awareness" bonus.

---

## 6.5.20 — Tracking and Countertracking

Basic tracking may cover:

- Footprints
- Broken vegetation
- Disturbed soil
- Travel direction
- Camp evidence
- Basic scent or environmental clues

Countertracking may include minimizing obvious trails and recognizing when one is being followed.

Advanced trackers require specialized training beyond the Academy.

---

## 6.5.21 — Fieldcraft and Survival

Students should learn survival appropriate to their likely operating environment.

Possible topics include:

- Water
- Shelter
- Fire
- Food safety
- Navigation
- Weather
- Exposure
- Camp security
- Basic foraging
- Environmental hazards

Curriculum emphasis should reflect local geography.

---

## 6.5.22 — Navigation and Geography

Students may train with:

- Maps
- Compass-equivalent tools
- Landmarks
- Distance estimation
- Route planning
- Political borders
- Terrain recognition

Professional shinobi must be able to travel without relying on omniscient navigation.

---

## 6.5.23 — Tactics and Strategy

Early tactical education should emphasize simple principles:

- Positioning
- Objective priority
- Retreat
- Ambush awareness
- Numerical advantage
- Terrain use
- Communication
- Protecting noncombatants
- Mission constraints

Advanced strategy belongs to later professional development.

---

## 6.5.24 — Teamwork

Students should practice operating with others.

Training may include:

- Shared objectives
- Role assignment
- Communication
- Mutual support
- Conflict management
- Coordinated movement
- Simple formations
- Protecting an injured teammate

Team exercises should create opportunities for Ruleset 7 relationships to matter.

---

## 6.5.25 — Communication and Signals

Students may learn:

- Hand signals
- Call signs
- Written messages
- Basic codes
- Emergency signaling
- Message security
- Reporting formats

Higher-level cryptography and intelligence communications remain specialist subjects.

---

## 6.5.26 — Intelligence Fundamentals

Academy-level intelligence education may include:

- Separating observation from assumption
- Recording useful details
- Protecting sensitive information
- Recognizing misinformation
- Basic questioning
- Mission reporting

Students should understand that information can be incomplete or wrong before they ever encounter a misclassified mission.

---

## 6.5.27 — First Aid and Casualty Response

Every prospective shinobi should receive basic emergency care training appropriate to the era.

Possible competencies include:

- Bleeding control
- Stabilization
- Moving an injured person
- Recognizing serious injury
- Calling for medical support
- Basic field hygiene

This is not equivalent to medical-nin training.

Ruleset 4 governs actual medical resolution.

---

## 6.5.28 — Mission Fundamentals

Older students should learn the structure of mission work.

Topics may include:

- Reading a mission brief
- Identifying objectives
- Client interaction
- Preparation
- Equipment selection
- Travel planning
- Mission discipline
- Debriefing
- Written reports

This prepares students for Genin service without making them fully qualified independent operatives.

---

## 6.5.29 — Ethics, Doctrine, and Indoctrination

Academies inevitably transmit values.

Instruction may include beliefs about:

- Duty
- Loyalty
- Sacrifice
- Violence
- Village identity
- Enemies
- Civilian obligations
- The meaning of being a shinobi

These lessons can range from healthy civic education to overt indoctrination depending on village and era.

The engine should model what is taught without assuming the institution's claims are objectively correct.

---

## 6.5.30 — Electives and Optional Study

Where resources allow, older students may access electives such as:

- Medical fundamentals
- Advanced weapons
- Tracking
- Sealing theory
- Strategy
- Languages
- Intelligence
- Barrier theory
- Communications

Electives provide early exposure, not full professional specialization.

---

## 6.5.31 — Restricted Curriculum

Some knowledge should not be available to ordinary Academy students.

Restricted content may include:

- Forbidden techniques
- Classified intelligence methods
- Advanced assassination doctrine
- Dangerous sealing procedures
- Sensitive clan techniques
- High-level interrogation methods

Access requires appropriate institutional or personal justification.

---

## 6.5.32 — Curriculum by Developmental Stage

A useful default structure is:

### Early Academy
Academics, physical coordination, discipline, basic shinobi culture, safety.

### Middle Academy
Chakra foundations, weapons, taijutsu, stealth, survival, teamwork.

### Late Academy
Technique execution, tactics, mission fundamentals, field exercises, integrated assessments.

This is a framework rather than a universal timetable.

---

## 6.5.33 — Curriculum Does Not Guarantee Mastery

Completing instruction means a student had the opportunity to learn.

It does not mean every student reaches the same competency.

Two graduates may have dramatically different strengths and weaknesses while both satisfying minimum graduation requirements.

---

## 6.5.34 — Curriculum Review and Change

Academy curricula may change over time because of:

- War
- New leadership
- Lessons from casualties
- New technologies
- New threats
- Political reform
- Instructor availability
- Budget changes

The curriculum itself is part of world state.

---

## 6.5.35 — NPC Curriculum Exposure

NPC Academy students receive the same curriculum available to the player.

Background simulation may summarize exposure and resulting development, but NPCs should not receive unexplained techniques or competencies merely because they are needed later.

Important unusual skills should have plausible educational or personal origins.

---

## 6.5.36 — Governing Principle

Academy curriculum should answer:

> **What does this village believe every future shinobi needs to know, and what educational opportunities does it realistically provide to teach those things?**

The Academy creates foundations.

It does not manufacture identical shinobi.

Students leave with different talents, weaknesses, experiences, relationships, techniques, and interests despite passing through the same institution.
