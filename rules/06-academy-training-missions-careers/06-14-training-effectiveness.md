# 6.14 — Training Effectiveness

**Ruleset:** 6 — Academy, Training, Missions & Careers  
**Status:** Provisional

## 6.14.1 — Purpose

Training effectiveness determines how productive a specific training session or training block is likely to be.

It should reflect the interaction between:

- The trainee
- The training target
- The training method
- The challenge level
- The instructor
- The environment
- Available resources
- Physical and mental condition
- Motivation
- Feedback quality
- Recovery
- Prior experience

Ruleset 6 defines these conditions.

Ruleset 1 determines actual long-term growth.

Ruleset 2 resolves uncertain execution where necessary.

---

## 6.14.2 — Effectiveness Is Contextual

There should be no universal training value such as:

> One hour = 10 XP

One hour of practice may be:

- Extremely productive
- Moderately useful
- Barely useful
- Completely wasted
- Actively harmful

depending on what the character is doing and under what circumstances.

---

## 6.14.3 — Effectiveness Is Not Just Effort

Trying harder does not automatically make training better.

Effective training requires a productive combination of:

- Appropriate difficulty
- Correct method
- Sufficient concentration
- Useful feedback
- Adequate recovery
- Appropriate prerequisites

A character can work extremely hard while practicing incorrectly.

That may create fatigue without much development.

---

## 6.14.4 — Baseline Capability

Current competence strongly affects training effectiveness.

Beginners may improve quickly from simple practice because they have many obvious weaknesses.

Advanced characters often require:

- More precise drills
- Greater challenge
- Better feedback
- Specialized instruction
- More realistic conditions

Training that once produced rapid improvement may later become too basic.

---

## 6.14.5 — Potential

Potential from Ruleset 1 affects how readily a character can develop in a domain.

Potential should influence:

- Learning speed
- Ease of adaptation
- Long-term ceiling behavior
- Response to advanced training

Potential does not replace training.

High Potential without time, access, or effort should not automatically create mastery.

Likewise, lower Potential does not prevent meaningful improvement.

---

## 6.14.6 — Relevant Attributes

Attributes may affect how effectively certain forms of training can be performed.

Examples:

- Agility may support movement drills.
- Intelligence may support complex theory.
- Endurance may support sustained conditioning.
- Perception may support observation training.
- Willpower may support difficult concentration.
- Chakra may influence chakra-intensive practice.

Attributes should influence training through the demands of the activity rather than applying a generic global bonus.

---

## 6.14.7 — Relevant Skills

Existing Skills can improve the quality of practice.

A trainee with strong fundamentals may:

- Recognize mistakes faster
- Perform more useful repetitions
- Attempt more advanced drills
- Integrate feedback more effectively

However, strong existing Skill also means easy training may produce fewer gains.

---

## 6.14.8 — Prerequisite Quality

Meeting a prerequisite barely and exceeding it substantially should not always produce identical training conditions.

A character who only just meets the requirements for an advanced exercise may struggle to benefit fully.

A stronger foundation may allow:

- Better execution
- Safer practice
- Greater repetition quality
- More attention to advanced details

This should matter without turning prerequisites into hard numerical gates everywhere.

---

## 6.14.9 — Method Quality

Training method matters greatly.

A good method:

- Targets the intended weakness
- Produces useful repetitions
- Provides relevant challenge
- Allows error correction
- Fits the trainee's current level

A bad method may be:

- Too easy
- Too difficult
- Poorly targeted
- Unsafe
- Reinforcing incorrect technique
- Unrelated to the actual problem

Method quality should emerge from training design, not a universal hidden quality stat.

---

## 6.14.10 — Specificity

Training tends to improve what is actually practiced.

Examples:

- Practicing hand-seal speed primarily improves hand-seal execution.
- Long-distance running primarily develops endurance-related capability.
- Studying tactics improves tactical knowledge, not physical strength.
- Sparring improves applied combat skills more than raw theory.

Some transfer exists, but training effects should remain grounded in the activity.

---

## 6.14.11 — Challenge Level

Productive training generally requires meaningful challenge.

Training that is too easy may provide:

- Maintenance
- Warm-up
- Minor refinement

Training that is too difficult may produce:

- Repeated failure
- Poor technique
- Frustration
- Injury risk
- Little useful feedback

The most effective zone usually lies between trivial and overwhelming.

Ruleset 6.15 develops this in detail.

---

## 6.14.12 — Successful Repetition Quality

Not all repetitions are equal.

A session with 50 technically correct repetitions may be more useful than 200 rushed and incorrect repetitions.

The engine should consider:

- Accuracy
- Consistency
- Feedback
- Fatigue
- Attention
- Difficulty

Training volume matters only when the repetitions are actually meaningful.

---

## 6.14.13 — Error Correction

One of the largest benefits of good training is identifying and correcting mistakes.

Effective correction may come from:

- Instructor feedback
- Training partner feedback
- Self-observation
- Video or equivalent review where technology permits
- Repeated comparison
- Controlled drills

Uncorrected errors can become habits.

---

## 6.14.14 — Feedback Speed

Immediate feedback is often more useful than delayed feedback.

A student corrected after one incorrect repetition can adjust quickly.

A student corrected after practicing incorrectly for three weeks may need to unlearn the habit.

Feedback timing should therefore matter where relevant.

---

## 6.14.15 — Instructor Effectiveness

Instructor contribution depends on:

- Subject mastery
- Teaching Skill
- Diagnostic ability
- Attention
- Compatibility
- Communication
- Demonstration quality

A great teacher improves the quality of the training process.

They do not directly award bonus progress.

---

## 6.14.16 — Instructor Attention

One-on-one training may allow:

- Frequent correction
- Customized drills
- Rapid adaptation
- Better pacing

Group instruction may provide:

- Peer examples
- Competition
- Partner practice
- Less individualized attention

Neither is universally superior.

The activity and student determine which is more useful.

---

## 6.14.17 — Training Partner Quality

Partners influence training through:

- Skill level
- Cooperation
- Unpredictability
- Feedback
- Effort
- Style

A partner with different strengths may expose weaknesses that familiar practice does not.

Repeatedly training against only one style may eventually limit adaptability.

---

## 6.14.18 — Variety

Training variety can prevent stagnation and broaden application.

Useful variation may include:

- Different opponents
- Different terrain
- Different drills
- Different speeds
- Different tactical conditions
- Different levels of pressure

Too much variation can also reduce focused repetition.

Training should balance consistency and novelty.

---

## 6.14.19 — Deliberate Practice

Highly effective training should often be deliberate.

Deliberate practice involves:

1. Identifying a specific weakness
2. Selecting an exercise targeting that weakness
3. Attempting it near the edge of current ability
4. Receiving useful feedback
5. Adjusting
6. Repeating

This should generally outperform unfocused repetition of already-comfortable skills.

---

## 6.14.20 — Concentration

Training quality falls when attention is poor.

Possible causes include:

- Fatigue
- Stress
- Distraction
- Boredom
- Hunger
- Emotional conflict
- Pain

Some Skills may tolerate distracted repetition better than others.

Complex technical learning generally requires greater concentration.

---

## 6.14.21 — Motivation

Motivation can influence:

- Persistence
- Attention
- Willingness to attempt difficult drills
- Compliance with feedback
- Training consistency

Motivation should not become a magical performance multiplier.

A highly motivated character can still use an ineffective method.

---

## 6.14.22 — Discipline

Discipline differs from motivation.

Motivation asks:

> Do I want to train?

Discipline asks:

> Will I follow the plan even when I do not feel like it?

High discipline can support:

- Consistency
- Proper pacing
- Completion of difficult drills
- Recovery adherence

Both should matter differently.

---

## 6.14.23 — Confidence

Confidence can influence training performance.

Too little may cause:

- Hesitation
- Avoidance
- Reduced intensity

Too much may cause:

- Recklessness
- Poor attention to correction
- Unsafe progression

Healthy confidence supports experimentation and challenge.

Ruleset 7 may influence confidence-related emotional states.

---

## 6.14.24 — Fatigue

Fatigue may reduce:

- Technical accuracy
- Memory
- Reaction speed
- Concentration
- Error recognition

Some training intentionally uses fatigue to improve performance under exhaustion.

In that case, reduced technical quality may be acceptable because endurance under fatigue is the intended target.

Effectiveness must always be judged against the actual objective.

---

## 6.14.25 — Chakra Depletion

Low chakra may affect chakra-intensive training through:

- Reduced repetitions
- Poorer control
- Increased exhaustion
- Inability to attempt advanced exercises

Training near depletion may sometimes serve a specific endurance purpose.

It should not automatically produce superior growth.

---

## 6.14.26 — Injury and Pain

Pain or injury can alter movement and create compensatory habits.

Training through injury may:

- Reduce quality
- Increase reinjury risk
- Teach incorrect mechanics

Alternative training may be more productive.

Ruleset 4 determines medical limits.

---

## 6.14.27 — Stress and Emotional State

Moderate stress may improve realism for pressure-based training.

Excessive stress may reduce:

- Focus
- Memory
- Coordination
- Willingness to experiment

The usefulness of stress depends on whether the training objective involves performance under pressure.

---

## 6.14.28 — Environment

The training environment may increase or reduce effectiveness.

Examples:

- Quiet classroom for theory
- Training yard for basic drills
- Forest for tracking
- River for water-based chakra exercises
- Crowded environment for distraction resistance

An environment is useful when it supports the objective.

---

## 6.14.29 — Equipment and Resources

Good equipment may improve:

- Safety
- Repetition quality
- Measurement
- Feedback
- Access to advanced exercises

Poor equipment may limit training.

Improvised equipment may sometimes create useful alternatives but should not always equal specialized resources.

---

## 6.14.30 — Novelty

New training methods can sometimes create rapid progress because they expose previously unchallenged weaknesses.

Novelty alone is not inherently valuable.

A new drill that does not address a real need may simply waste time.

---

## 6.14.31 — Prior Session Carryover

Training effectiveness can depend on what happened earlier.

Examples:

- Prior conditioning creates fatigue.
- Recent theory study improves understanding.
- Previous feedback guides today's practice.
- Recent injury restricts options.

Sessions should not be isolated from surrounding history.

---

## 6.14.32 — Recovery Quality

Development occurs across both training and recovery.

Poor recovery may reduce future training quality.

Good recovery may include:

- Sleep
- Nutrition
- Rest
- Medical treatment
- Reduced workload

Ruleset 6.20 develops fatigue and overtraining in greater detail.

---

## 6.14.33 — Short-Term Performance vs Long-Term Learning

A character performing well during a session does not always mean they learned efficiently.

Examples:

- An easy drill can produce excellent performance but little growth.
- A difficult drill may produce many mistakes but valuable adaptation.
- An instructor may temporarily guide performance that the student cannot yet reproduce alone.

The engine should distinguish immediate performance from durable development.

---

## 6.14.34 — Breakthrough Sessions

Occasionally, several conditions align to produce unusually meaningful progress.

A breakthrough may arise from:

- Correcting a major misconception
- Finally understanding an instructor explanation
- Integrating multiple prerequisites
- Successful experimentation
- Overcoming a long plateau

Breakthroughs should emerge from accumulated conditions and uncertainty rather than arbitrary rewards.

---

## 6.14.35 — Ineffective Sessions

Some training sessions may produce little or no useful progress.

Possible reasons include:

- Wrong method
- Insufficient prerequisites
- Excessive fatigue
- Poor concentration
- Too little challenge
- Excessive challenge
- Repeated incorrect technique
- Lack of feedback

A session can still produce insight about why it failed.

---

## 6.14.36 — Harmful Practice

Training can occasionally make performance worse.

Examples include:

- Reinforcing bad technique
- Developing compensatory movement
- Building unsafe habits
- Learning incorrect theory
- Practicing under chronic exhaustion

These effects should be uncommon but possible when conditions justify them.

---

## 6.14.37 — Training Effectiveness Profile

For engine purposes, a significant session may be summarized internally using factors such as:

- Target relevance
- Method suitability
- Challenge suitability
- Repetition quality
- Feedback quality
- Instructor contribution
- Trainee readiness
- Physical condition
- Mental condition
- Environment
- Recovery context

This profile informs Ruleset 1 growth.

It should not be exposed as one exact percentage unless Ruleset 13 explicitly permits such meta-information.

---

## 6.14.38 — No Universal Multiplier Formula

The engine should avoid reducing the entire system to:

> Base XP × Talent × Teacher × Motivation × Facility

That approach would hide too much causal information and encourage optimization around numbers rather than believable training.

Instead, each factor should alter the actual training conditions and the resulting opportunities for growth.

---

## 6.14.39 — NPC Training Effectiveness

NPCs use the same principles.

Their training quality should reflect:

- Access
- Methods
- Teachers
- Time
- Motivation
- Condition
- Challenge
- Recovery

Background NPCs may be abstracted, but the abstraction should approximate these factors.

---

## 6.14.40 — Governing Principle

Training effectiveness should answer:

> **Given what this character is practicing, how they are practicing it, and the condition they are practicing under, how much useful development would this session plausibly create?**

Effective training is not simply longer, harder, or more exhausting.

The most productive training is the training that creates the right challenge, with the right method, at the right time, for the right character.
