# 6.7 — Academy Instructors & Educational Quality

**Ruleset:** 6 — Academy, Training, Missions & Careers  
**Status:** Provisional

## 6.7.1 — Purpose

Academy instructors determine how effectively curriculum is delivered.

Educational quality should depend on more than the instructor's combat rank or technical mastery.

Relevant factors may include:

- Subject knowledge
- Teaching skill
- Communication
- Patience
- Feedback quality
- Class management
- Observation
- Adaptability
- Student compatibility
- Class size
- Facilities
- Equipment
- Administrative support
- Instructor workload

The Academy provides opportunities to learn, but the quality of those opportunities can vary substantially.

---

## 6.7.2 — Teaching Ability Is Separate From Combat Ability

A powerful shinobi is not automatically a strong teacher.

Likewise, an average combatant may be an excellent instructor.

The engine should distinguish:

- Technical competence
- Teaching competence
- Subject familiarity
- Practical experience
- Ability to diagnose mistakes
- Ability to explain concepts
- Ability to manage students

These traits may overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

---

## 6.7.3 — Subject Competence Matters

An instructor cannot reliably teach beyond their own understanding.

Subject competence establishes:

- What concepts they can explain
- What errors they can identify
- What demonstrations they can perform
- What advanced questions they can answer
- What training methods they know

An instructor may be highly effective in one subject and mediocre in another.

---

## 6.7.4 — Teaching Skill

Teaching skill governs how effectively an instructor converts knowledge into student learning opportunities.

It may involve:

- Clear explanation
- Demonstration
- Correction
- Pacing
- Questioning
- Feedback
- Motivation
- Lesson design
- Adaptation

Teaching skill should itself be a learnable professional Skill rather than an invisible rank bonus.

---

## 6.7.5 — Diagnostic Ability

Strong instructors can identify why a student is struggling.

Possible causes include:

- Missing prerequisite
- Poor technique
- Fear
- Fatigue
- Misunderstanding
- Bad habit
- Physical limitation
- Chakra-control issue
- Inappropriate training method

Correct diagnosis allows targeted remediation.

Poor diagnosis may waste time or even reinforce the problem.

---

## 6.7.6 — Communication Style

Different instructors may teach through:

- Verbal explanation
- Demonstration
- Repetition
- Analogy
- Guided practice
- Questioning
- Independent problem solving
- Strict drilling

Students may respond differently to each style.

The engine should allow instructional compatibility without turning it into a rigid personality-match bonus.

---

## 6.7.7 — Student-Instructor Compatibility

Compatibility may be influenced by:

- Learning style
- Personality
- Trust
- Prior relationship
- Communication
- Cultural background
- Motivation
- Instructor expectations

Good compatibility can make feedback easier to understand and accept.

Poor compatibility may slow development without making learning impossible.

Ruleset 7 governs the relationship itself.

---

## 6.7.8 — Instructor Expectations

Teachers form expectations about students.

Expectations may affect:

- Attention
- Opportunities
- Difficulty of assignments
- Recommendations
- Feedback
- Patience
- Advanced placement

Expectations can be accurate or wrong.

The engine should avoid making teacher perception equal objective truth.

---

## 6.7.9 — Bias and Favoritism

Instructor behavior may be affected by:

- Clan reputation
- Family history
- Social class
- Personal relationships
- Prior incidents
- Political pressure
- Personality conflict
- Favoritism

Bias should emerge from actual character traits and social context.

It should not be randomly inserted merely to create drama.

---

## 6.7.10 — Instructor Attention

Teacher attention is limited.

An instructor with many students cannot provide the same amount of individualized correction as one teaching a small group.

Attention allocation may depend on:

- Class size
- Student need
- Student behavior
- Instructor priorities
- Exceptional talent
- Struggling students
- Favoritism
- Administrative demands

This creates natural inequality in educational opportunity.

---

## 6.7.11 — Class Size Effects

Class size should influence education through concrete mechanisms.

Large classes may cause:

- Less individual feedback
- Longer waits for correction
- Fewer demonstrations
- Reduced practical repetitions
- Greater competition for equipment

Small classes may allow:

- More individualized instruction
- Faster correction
- More questions
- Better monitoring

Class size should not simply apply a universal XP multiplier.

---

## 6.7.12 — Instructor Workload

Instructors may also have responsibilities outside teaching.

Examples include:

- Missions
- Administration
- Grading
- Department duties
- Family obligations
- Training
- Security assignments

Heavy workload may reduce preparation, availability, or patience.

Workload is part of the simulation rather than a hidden teaching penalty.

---

## 6.7.13 — Fatigue and Instructor Condition

Teachers can be:

- Tired
- Injured
- Distracted
- Ill
- Stressed
- Overworked

Their condition may temporarily affect teaching quality.

A normally excellent instructor can have a poor day.

---

## 6.7.14 — Primary Instructors

A primary instructor typically oversees:

- Cohort progress
- General academic development
- Conduct
- Attendance
- Coordination with specialists
- Guardian communication
- Recommendations

Primary instructors may become especially influential because of repeated contact.

---

## 6.7.15 — Specialist Instructors

Some subjects may require specialist teachers.

Examples include:

- Medical fundamentals
- Sealing theory
- Weapons
- Tracking
- Strategy
- Communications
- Chakra theory

Specialist instruction may provide better technical quality but less knowledge of each individual student.

---

## 6.7.16 — Guest Instructors

Active shinobi may occasionally teach short modules.

Guest instructors can provide:

- Current field experience
- Specialized knowledge
- Demonstrations
- Career exposure
- Unusual techniques

A guest instructor's prestige should not automatically make the lesson effective.

Teaching ability still matters.

---

## 6.7.17 — Assistant Instructors

Assistants can improve educational quality by:

- Supervising drills
- Answering questions
- Correcting technique
- Monitoring safety
- Supporting struggling students

Assistant instructors are especially valuable in practical classes and large cohorts.

---

## 6.7.18 — Substitute Instructors

Absence may require replacement teachers.

Substitutes may differ in:

- Subject knowledge
- Teaching style
- Familiarity with students
- Discipline
- Expectations

Short substitutions usually create small effects.

Long-term instability can meaningfully disrupt learning.

---

## 6.7.19 — Instructor Turnover

Frequent teacher changes may reduce:

- Continuity
- Trust
- Accurate assessment
- Long-term lesson planning

But a new instructor may also improve a poor situation.

Turnover should therefore create circumstances rather than automatic negative modifiers.

---

## 6.7.20 — Curriculum Fidelity

Instructors may differ in how closely they follow the official curriculum.

Possible approaches include:

- Strict adherence
- Flexible interpretation
- Extra emphasis on personal specialties
- Skipping weakly understood material
- Adding practical experience
- Teaching outdated doctrine

This can produce meaningful differences between cohorts in the same Academy.

---

## 6.7.21 — Instructional Quality

Educational quality should emerge from several factors rather than one Teaching Quality score.

Important factors include:

- Instructor competence
- Teaching skill
- Student attention
- Class size
- Facilities
- Equipment
- Curriculum quality
- Time available
- Student condition
- Instructor condition
- Practice opportunities

The engine can summarize these factors internally when needed, but should preserve their causes.

---

## 6.7.22 — Facilities

Academy resources may include:

- Classrooms
- Training yards
- Sparring spaces
- Obstacle courses
- Libraries
- Medical rooms
- Weapons ranges
- Field-training areas

Facilities determine what training can realistically occur.

Poor facilities may restrict opportunity without making learning impossible.

---

## 6.7.23 — Equipment Quality

Training quality may depend on access to:

- Practice weapons
- Targets
- Protective equipment
- Maps
- Books
- Medical supplies
- Specialized tools

Scarce equipment can limit practice time.

Well-funded Academies may provide substantially more hands-on exposure.

---

## 6.7.24 — Safety Standards

Academies establish acceptable risk.

Safety quality depends on:

- Instructor supervision
- Protective equipment
- Training design
- Medical access
- Class size
- Institutional culture

Poor safety can create injury risk.

Ruleset 4 handles actual injuries.

Ruleset 6 determines the training environment that created the risk.

---

## 6.7.25 — Administrative Support

Academy leadership influences teaching quality through:

- Staffing
- Scheduling
- Funding
- Curriculum planning
- Instructor evaluation
- Discipline policies
- Resource allocation

A strong teacher can still struggle inside a badly managed institution.

---

## 6.7.26 — Instructor Evaluation

Academies may evaluate instructors through:

- Student outcomes
- Observations
- Complaints
- Peer review
- Exam performance
- Safety records
- Administrative review

Instructor evaluation can itself be imperfect or political.

---

## 6.7.27 — Professional Development

Instructors may improve through:

- Teaching experience
- Mentorship
- Formal training
- Specialist study
- Reviewing failures
- New curriculum
- Observing other teachers

Educational quality can therefore change over time.

---

## 6.7.28 — Poor Instruction

Weak instruction may lead to:

- Slower development
- Persistent misconceptions
- Poor habits
- Uneven competency
- Reduced motivation
- Safety problems

Poor instruction should not permanently lock a character into failure.

Better teaching later can correct previous deficiencies.

---

## 6.7.29 — Exceptional Instruction

Exceptional teaching can provide:

- Faster diagnosis
- Better feedback
- More efficient practice
- Stronger conceptual understanding
- Better adaptation to student needs

It does not bypass prerequisites, Potential, physical limits, or time.

Even the best teacher cannot instantly produce mastery.

---

## 6.7.30 — Unequal Educational Opportunity

Students in the same village may receive different educational opportunities because of:

- Different instructors
- Different class sizes
- Advanced placement
- Remediation
- Clan tutoring
- Specialist access
- Favoritism
- Resource allocation

This inequality should emerge naturally from circumstances.

---

## 6.7.31 — Small Village Academies

Small or poor villages may have:

- Few instructors
- Mixed-age classes
- Limited equipment
- Narrower curriculum
- Heavy dependence on local specialists

They may also gain advantages such as:

- Smaller cohorts
- Closer community relationships
- More individualized attention

Small institutions should not automatically be inferior in every respect.

---

## 6.7.32 — Wartime Educational Quality

War may alter instructional quality through:

- Instructor casualties
- Larger cohorts
- Accelerated schedules
- Reduced resources
- More experienced field instructors
- Greater practical emphasis

Wartime education may become simultaneously more realistic and less complete.

---

## 6.7.33 — Educational Quality and NPCs

NPC students should be affected by the same instructional environment.

Students from strong cohorts may develop better foundations.

Students from poorly staffed or disrupted Academies may develop gaps.

Important NPC competency should have plausible educational history.

---

## 6.7.34 — Hidden Information

Students may not know:

- Their teacher's full evaluation
- Why one student receives extra attention
- Internal staffing problems
- Political pressure
- Instructor disciplinary history
- Administrative concerns

Ruleset 13 determines what is surfaced to the player.

---

## 6.7.35 — No Flat Teaching Multiplier

The engine should avoid reducing teaching to:

> Good Teacher = +25% XP

Instead, instruction should improve the actual mechanisms of learning:

- Better feedback
- Better exercise selection
- Earlier error correction
- More useful demonstrations
- Appropriate challenge
- Better access to advanced material

Ruleset 1 converts those improved training conditions into development.

---

## 6.7.36 — Governing Principle

Academy instructors should answer:

> **How effectively is this material being taught to this student under these circumstances?**

Teaching quality should emerge from people, resources, time, and institutional conditions.

A famous Jōnin may be a poor teacher.

A modest Chūnin may transform a student's education.

The quality of the Academy should matter without replacing the character-growth systems that already govern actual improvement.
