# 7.18 — Intimidation, Coercion, Commands & Interrogation

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.18 defines fear-based influence, legitimate command, coercive pressure, blackmail, detention, and interrogation.

The governing principle is:

**Compliance caused by fear, authority, or constrained choice is not the same as genuine agreement, trust, loyalty, or consent.**

## 7.18.1 Four Distinct Mechanisms

The simulation should distinguish:

### Intimidation
Influence through fear of consequences.

### Command
Influence through recognized authority, duty, or hierarchy.

### Coercion
Influence through pressure severe enough to meaningfully restrict free choice.

### Interrogation
Structured effort to obtain information from a guarded, resistant, uncertain, or deceptive target.

These may overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

## 7.18.2 Intimidation

Intimidation attempts to change behavior by making danger, punishment, or negative consequence psychologically salient.

Possible outcomes:
- compliance;
- retreat;
- silence;
- disclosure;
- hesitation;
- deterrence.

It primarily operates through Fear.

## 7.18.3 Intimidation Methods

Methods may include:
- physical presence;
- explicit threat;
- implied threat;
- reputation;
- demonstration of power;
- targeted psychological pressure;
- threat to status or resources.

The method determines relevant attributes and context.

## 7.18.4 Credibility

A threat should be evaluated by:
- capability;
- opportunity;
- intent;
- immediacy;
- target knowledge;
- prior behavior.

An incredible threat should have little effect.

## 7.18.5 Specificity

Threats can be:
- vague;
- specific;
- conditional;
- immediate;
- delayed.

Specific threats may be more credible but create clearer evidence and accountability.

## 7.18.6 Fear Response Variety

Fear may produce:
- compliance;
- flight;
- freezing;
- concealment;
- appeasement;
- aggression;
- seeking protection;
- retaliation planning.

Intimidation does not guarantee obedience.

## 7.18.7 Successful Intimidation

Success means the threat meaningfully changed the target's behavior within the plausible outcome space.

It may also increase:
- Fear;
- Resentment;
- Hostility;
- desire for revenge;
- future avoidance.

## 7.18.8 Intimidation Failure

Failure may mean:
- target refuses;
- target calls bluff;
- target becomes angry;
- target attacks;
- target seeks help;
- target reports the threat.

Failure should not simply mean "nothing happens."

## 7.18.9 Repeated Threats

Repeated intimidation may:
- increase Fear;
- normalize the threat;
- increase Resentment;
- reduce credibility if never acted upon;
- provoke resistance.

Threat spam should not create infinite escalating bonuses.

## 7.18.10 Bluffing Threats

A threat may itself be deceptive.

Then:
- Intimidation governs psychological impact;
- Deception governs whether the target believes the threat can or will be carried out.

## 7.18.11 Command

Command is an instruction backed by recognized authority or duty.

Sources may include:
- military rank;
- mission assignment;
- office;
- clan hierarchy;
- legal authority;
- parental authority where age-appropriate.

## 7.18.12 Legitimate Authority

Command strength depends on whether the target recognizes:
- the speaker's authority;
- jurisdiction;
- legitimacy;
- relevance.

A commander may have authority during a mission but not over unrelated private life.

## 7.18.13 Routine Orders

Routine, lawful, expected orders may require no roll.

Example:
A jonin tells their genin team to regroup at the designated point.

If no meaningful resistance exists, simply resolve compliance.

## 7.18.14 Contested Orders

A roll or deeper decision may be needed when an order:
- carries high risk;
- conflicts with values;
- contradicts another authority;
- seems unlawful;
- threatens loved ones;
- violates duty;
- comes from distrusted leadership.

## 7.18.15 Command Responses

Targets may:
- obey enthusiastically;
- obey professionally;
- obey reluctantly;
- ask clarification;
- challenge;
- delay;
- reinterpret;
- secretly disobey;
- openly refuse.

Authority changes the decision environment.
It does not erase agency.

## 7.18.16 Illegal or Illegitimate Orders

An order outside legitimate authority may function more like:
- request;
- intimidation;
- coercion;
- manipulation.

The engine should not automatically treat rank as universal command power.

## 7.18.17 Conflicting Authority

NPCs may receive conflicting orders from:
- two commanders;
- clan vs village;
- family vs mission;
- law vs superior.

Resolution depends on:
- legitimacy;
- priority;
- loyalty;
- values;
- expected consequences.

## 7.18.18 Coercion

Coercion occurs when pressure meaningfully constrains the target's freedom to choose.

Possible methods:
- blackmail;
- credible threat of serious harm;
- detention;
- economic control;
- threat against loved ones;
- abuse of institutional authority.

Coercion differs from ordinary persuasion because refusal carries severe imposed cost.

## 7.18.19 Coerced Compliance

Coerced compliance should be tracked as:
- behavior obtained;
- willingness absent or limited;
- pressure source known.

This may later create:
- Resentment;
- Hostility;
- Fear;
- betrayal motives;
- escape attempts.

## 7.18.20 Blackmail

Blackmail uses threatened disclosure or exposure as leverage.

Track:
- secret;
- threatened consequence;
- credibility;
- target vulnerability;
- whether exposure can actually be carried out.

Blackmail may produce temporary compliance while severely damaging Trust.

## 7.18.21 Coercion Escalation

Increasing coercive pressure may:
- increase compliance;
- increase resistance;
- increase desperation;
- trigger violence;
- cause false cooperation.

Escalation should carry growing consequences.

## 7.18.22 Power Imbalance

Coercion becomes more likely when one side controls:
- freedom;
- employment;
- medical access;
- housing;
- money;
- legal status;
- physical safety;
- institutional power.

The engine should recognize structural pressure even without an explicit threat.

## 7.18.23 Captivity

Captivity changes the social context dramatically.

A captive may:
- cooperate strategically;
- lie;
- resist;
- stall;
- provide partial truth;
- attempt escape.

Apparent cooperation under captivity should not be treated as ordinary willingness.

## 7.18.24 Interrogation Purpose

Interrogation seeks:
- facts;
- confirmation;
- contradiction;
- timeline;
- motive;
- names;
- locations;
- plans.

It should generally be multi-stage.

## 7.18.25 Interrogation Approaches

Possible approaches include:
- rapport;
- direct questioning;
- evidence presentation;
- contradiction testing;
- strategic silence;
- repeated timeline reconstruction;
- incentives;
- controlled pressure.

Interrogation is not automatically torture or intimidation.

## 7.18.26 Rapport-Based Interrogation

Rapport may improve:
- willingness to talk;
- emotional safety;
- disclosure;
- correction of misunderstandings.

It can rely on:
- Trust;
- empathy;
- shared identity;
- respectful treatment.

## 7.18.27 Evidence-Based Interrogation

Presenting evidence may:
- expose contradictions;
- reduce confidence in lies;
- force revision;
- encourage confession.

Evidence quality remains governed by Ruleset 7.6.

## 7.18.28 Contradiction Testing

An interrogator may compare:
- earlier statements;
- witness statements;
- records;
- timeline;
- physical evidence.

Contradictions increase Suspicion but do not automatically reveal truth.

## 7.18.29 Pressure-Based Interrogation

Pressure may include:
- urgency;
- consequences;
- prolonged questioning;
- controlled intimidation.

It may increase:
- disclosure;
- compliance;
- false statements;
- hostility;
- memory errors.

## 7.18.30 False Confessions

Under severe pressure, a target may confess falsely to:
- stop questioning;
- avoid worse harm;
- satisfy perceived expectations;
- protect someone else.

Therefore:
**confession is evidence, not automatic proof.**

## 7.18.31 Information Limits

Interrogation can only extract:
- what the target knows;
- what the target believes;
- what the target invents;
- what the target remembers.

It cannot produce objective truth beyond the target's information state.

## 7.18.32 Partial Disclosure

Targets may reveal:
- some facts;
- outdated facts;
- selected truth;
- misleading truth;
- speculation.

Interrogation outcomes should preserve this granularity.

## 7.18.33 Resistance

Resistance may come from:
- Loyalty;
- ideology;
- Fear of others;
- training;
- shame;
- self-protection;
- distrust;
- desire to protect a secret.

Resistance may weaken or strengthen over time.

## 7.18.34 Interrogation Training

Training may improve:
- resisting pressure;
- maintaining cover stories;
- recognizing tactics;
- controlling disclosure.

Ruleset 1 owns actual progression.

## 7.18.35 Fatigue

Long questioning may create:
- reduced concentration;
- mistakes;
- irritability;
- memory confusion.

Ruleset 4 supplies physical fatigue where appropriate.

Pressure should not be modeled as a guaranteed truth amplifier.

## 7.18.36 Torture Interface

If torture exists in the setting:
- Ruleset 4 owns physical injury and pain;
- Ruleset 7 owns Fear, compliance, relationship consequences, and information behavior.

Torture may produce unreliable information.

It should not be treated as a superior truth-extraction mechanic.

## 7.18.37 Threats Against Others

Threatening family, friends, or teammates may be more powerful than self-directed threats.

Effect depends on:
- Affection;
- Loyalty;
- perceived credibility;
- alternatives.

It may also create extreme long-term Hostility.

## 7.18.38 Intimidation and Reputation

A feared reputation may make intimidation easier.

But reputation may also:
- trigger immediate resistance;
- cause avoidance;
- bring authorities.

Reputation modifies context, not raw skill.

## 7.18.39 Authority and Reputation

A respected commander may gain easier compliance.

A commander known for incompetence or cruelty may face:
- hesitation;
- low Trust;
- secret disobedience.

Formal rank and social legitimacy remain separate.

## 7.18.40 Coercion and Relationship State

Successful coercion may:
- increase Fear;
- reduce Trust;
- increase Resentment;
- increase Hostility;
- weaken Affection;
- create revenge goals.

Short-term success can create long-term instability.

## 7.18.41 Coercion and Loyalty

A loyal target may comply under coercion to protect others.

This does not mean loyalty transferred to the coercer.

## 7.18.42 Commands and Loyalty

High Loyalty may make difficult orders easier to accept.

But loyalty still competes with:
- values;
- stronger loyalties;
- self-preservation;
- law;
- family.

## 7.18.43 Romantic and Sexual Context

Fear, rank, captivity, employment dependence, or other power imbalance can alter the meaning of apparent romantic or sexual compliance.

Arousal or attraction does not erase coercive context.

Seduction cannot convert coercion into freely chosen willingness.

## 7.18.44 Adult Consent Under Coercive Context

For adults, apparent agreement obtained through:
- serious threat;
- captivity;
- blackmail;
- severe abuse of authority;

should not be treated as equivalent to freely given consent.

The simulation should preserve the coercive context explicitly.

## 7.18.45 Interrogation and Deception

Targets may lie during interrogation.

The interrogator may:
- detect inconsistencies;
- remain uncertain;
- believe the lie;
- misidentify truth as deception.

No automatic lie detection exists.

## 7.18.46 Interrogation Progress

Complex interrogation may track hidden progress toward:
- rapport;
- disclosure;
- contradiction;
- exhaustion of cover story.

Progress must correspond to concrete developments.

## 7.18.47 Interrogation Failure

Failure may result in:
- no disclosure;
- false information;
- target shutting down;
- hostility;
- target learning the interrogator's priorities.

Failure should carry information consequences.

## 7.18.48 Interrogation Success

Success may produce:
- truthful disclosure;
- partial truth;
- useful contradiction;
- verified fact;
- confession.

Success quality still depends on what the target actually knows.

## 7.18.49 Exceptional Interrogation Success

Exceptional success may:
- reveal deeper detail;
- expose inconsistency;
- reduce deception;
- improve verification.

It cannot:
- create nonexistent knowledge;
- make false memory accurate;
- guarantee truth under coercion.

## 7.18.50 Long-Term Consequences

Intimidation and coercion may create:
- fear-based compliance;
- hidden resistance;
- revenge;
- avoidance;
- betrayal;
- reputational damage;
- trauma-related memory.

Immediate obedience may therefore be strategically costly.

## 7.18.51 NPC-to-NPC Use

NPCs may:
- intimidate;
- command;
- coerce;
- interrogate;

other NPCs off-screen.

These interactions should produce persistent consequences even without player presence.

## 7.18.52 Player Agency

Mundane NPC intimidation should not directly choose the player's voluntary action.

Instead it may create:
- fear;
- pressure;
- credible consequences;
- impaired options;
- urgency.

The player chooses their response unless another system explicitly removes agency.

## 7.18.53 Design Standard

A mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- treats Fear as automatic obedience;
- treats rank as universal command power;
- treats coerced compliance as genuine agreement;
- assumes confession equals truth;
- makes torture a perfect information tool;
- lets interrogation extract unknown facts;
- ignores false confessions;
- ignores long-term resentment;
- turns intimidation into mind control;
- treats sexual compliance under serious coercion as equivalent to free consent.

## Governing Rule

**Fear, authority, pressure, and interrogation can alter behavior and disclosure, but the simulation must always preserve why the target complied, what they actually believed, what information they truly possessed, and what social consequences the pressure created.**
