# 7.16 — Persuasion, Requests & Long-Term Influence

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.16 defines persuasion, requests, motivational influence, belief change, and longer-term attempts to shift another person's position.

The governing principle is:

**Persuasion changes willingness or interpretation within the target's plausible decision space; it does not overwrite identity, values, knowledge, or agency.**

## 7.16.1 Persuasion Is Outcome-Specific

Persuasion should always be tied to a concrete desired outcome.

Examples:
- convince someone to wait;
- convince someone to provide aid;
- convince someone to reconsider an accusation;
- convince someone to accept a plan;
- convince someone to hear another person out.

Do not resolve vague intentions such as:
- "make them like me";
- "change their mind about everything";
- "convince them I'm right."

## 7.16.2 Requests vs Persuasion

A request is simply asking.

Persuasion begins when resistance, uncertainty, or conflicting motivation must be addressed.

Example:

"Can you pass me the scroll?"
- likely just a request.

"Can you risk disciplinary action to hide this scroll?"
- may require persuasion.

## 7.16.3 Cooperation vs Belief Change

These are separate outcomes.

An NPC may:
- believe the speaker is wrong but cooperate anyway;
- believe the speaker is correct but refuse to help;
- remain uncertain but agree temporarily.

The system should track:
- belief;
- willingness;
- action;

separately when relevant.

## 7.16.4 Immediate Cooperation

Immediate persuasion may alter:
- willingness;
- priority;
- perceived risk;
- perceived benefit;
- interpretation.

It is best suited for:
- concrete choices;
- short-term decisions;
- bounded requests.

## 7.16.5 Belief Change

Changing a belief should depend on:
- existing confidence;
- evidence;
- source credibility;
- expertise;
- worldview;
- emotional investment;
- bias;
- identity implications.

Strongly held beliefs should usually require stronger evidence or repeated exposure.

## 7.16.6 Value Change

Deep values should almost never change from one successful Persuasion check.

Values change through:
- major experiences;
- repeated contradiction;
- trusted relationships;
- long-term reflection;
- identity development;
- trauma;
- social environment.

Persuasion may begin that process.

It should not instantly rewrite core values.

## 7.16.7 Motivational Appeals

Persuasion may appeal to:
- duty;
- family;
- loyalty;
- ambition;
- fear of consequences;
- compassion;
- pride;
- justice;
- self-interest;
- reputation;
- hope.

An appeal works best when it matches motives the target actually possesses.

## 7.16.8 Misaligned Appeals

A persuasive technique can fail even when delivered skillfully if it targets the wrong motive.

Example:
Offering fame to someone who values privacy may decrease receptiveness.

## 7.16.9 Evidence-Based Persuasion

Evidence can reduce the amount of social influence required.

Strong evidence may:
- increase credibility;
- reduce uncertainty;
- force reconsideration.

Persuasion controls how effectively evidence is presented.

It does not manufacture evidence.

## 7.16.10 Weak Evidence

Weak evidence may still influence someone if:
- they already suspect the claim;
- they trust the source;
- it fits existing beliefs.

This does not make the conclusion objectively reliable.

## 7.16.11 Emotional Persuasion

An emotional appeal may influence:
- urgency;
- empathy;
- willingness;
- priorities.

Emotional persuasion does not automatically mean manipulation.

It may be completely sincere.

## 7.16.12 Logical Persuasion

Logical persuasion may emphasize:
- consistency;
- evidence;
- consequences;
- probability;
- contradictions.

It is not automatically stronger than emotional persuasion.

Different NPCs respond differently.

## 7.16.13 Moral Persuasion

An actor may appeal to:
- duty;
- honor;
- compassion;
- justice;
- promises;
- principles.

This is most effective when the target genuinely values the principle.

## 7.16.14 Self-Interest Appeals

An actor may highlight:
- safety;
- money;
- advancement;
- convenience;
- reputation;
- opportunity.

Self-interest may motivate cooperation without changing belief.

## 7.16.15 Social Proof

NPCs may care what relevant others believe or do.

Examples:
- respected teammates already support the plan;
- clan elders approved;
- trusted medic agrees.

Social proof should depend on:
- source relevance;
- target respect;
- group identity.

## 7.16.16 Authority Appeals

An actor may cite:
- rank;
- law;
- mission orders;
- clan expectations;
- institutional policy.

This may create compliance through legitimacy rather than persuasion.

The engine should distinguish the mechanism.

## 7.16.17 Reframing

Persuasion may change how the target interprets the same facts.

Example:
A dangerous mission may be reframed from:
- reckless risk;

to:
- necessary rescue.

Reframing is most effective when both interpretations are plausible.

## 7.16.18 Clarification

Sometimes "persuasion" succeeds by correcting misunderstanding.

If resistance comes from a false assumption, accurate clarification may remove it without needing strong influence.

## 7.16.19 Incremental Persuasion

Large position changes should often occur in steps.

Example:
1. Agree to hear evidence.
2. Admit uncertainty.
3. Accept investigation.
4. Delay action.
5. Eventually change belief.

This is often more believable than one check causing a complete reversal.

## 7.16.20 Long-Term Influence

Long-term influence may occur through:
- repeated conversations;
- ongoing relationship;
- mentorship;
- shared experiences;
- evidence over time;
- identity change;
- repeated reinforcement.

The engine may track trajectory rather than one decisive roll.

## 7.16.21 Influence Progress

For major long-term objectives, hidden progress may represent:
- openness;
- reconsideration;
- ideological softening;
- willingness to compromise.

Progress must be tied to real developments.

It should not function as generic persuasion XP.

## 7.16.22 Resistance Hardening

Repeated failed attempts may harden resistance.

Possible effects:
- annoyance;
- suspicion;
- reactance;
- stronger commitment to prior position.

This discourages argument spam.

## 7.16.23 Reactance

Some NPCs resist when they feel controlled.

Reactance may increase when:
- pressure is obvious;
- autonomy is threatened;
- the same argument repeats;
- authority is resented.

A technically persuasive message may fail because of delivery.

## 7.16.24 Face-Saving

People may resist public persuasion because changing position would embarrass them.

A private route may be easier.

Negotiation may allow:
- dignified retreat;
- shared credit;
- compromise.

## 7.16.25 Concessions

Persuasion may succeed partially.

Examples:
- target agrees to wait but not support;
- agrees to investigate but not believe;
- agrees to help once but not long-term.

Partial concessions should be common.

## 7.16.26 Conditional Agreement

An NPC may agree only if:
- certain evidence appears;
- another person participates;
- payment is provided;
- risk is reduced;
- secrecy is guaranteed.

Conditions become persistent commitments if accepted.

## 7.16.27 Persuasion and Trust

High Trust may:
- reduce skepticism;
- improve willingness to hear the actor;
- increase weight of unsupported testimony.

Low Trust may:
- require evidence;
- increase suspicion.

Trust should not make impossible claims believable.

## 7.16.28 Persuasion and Respect

High Respect may cause the target to:
- take advice seriously;
- consider expertise;
- defer on relevant matters.

Respect for combat ability does not necessarily improve medical credibility.

Domain matters.

## 7.16.29 Persuasion and Affection

Affection may increase willingness to:
- help;
- listen;
- forgive;
- take emotional appeals seriously.

But affection can also increase resistance when the requested action feels dangerous.

## 7.16.30 Persuasion and Loyalty

Loyalty may make difficult requests more plausible.

It should not erase moral boundaries or competing loyalties.

## 7.16.31 Persuasion and Fear

Fear may cause compliance.

That should not be labeled persuasion if the actual mechanism is intimidation or coercion.

## 7.16.32 Persuasion and Obligation

A favor owed may reduce resistance.

The NPC may agree because:
- "I owe you."

This may not increase affection or belief.

## 7.16.33 Persuasion and Resentment

Resentment may cause:
- skepticism;
- refusal;
- hostile interpretation.

A valid argument may still be rejected because of the speaker.

## 7.16.34 Persuasion and Reputation

Known expertise or honesty can improve credibility.

Known manipulation or dishonesty can increase resistance.

Reputation changes context, not raw skill.

## 7.16.35 Persuasion Under Time Pressure

Urgency may:
- reduce deliberation;
- simplify arguments;
- increase reliance on trusted sources;
- increase mistake risk.

Long arguments may be impossible during crisis.

## 7.16.36 Persuasion Through Writing

Persuasion may occur through:
- letters;
- reports;
- propaganda;
- written appeals.

The absence of immediate feedback may make adaptation harder.

## 7.16.37 Persuasion of Groups

Group persuasion should consider:
- shared identity;
- leaders;
- factions;
- audience emotion;
- conformity pressure;
- dissenters.

A successful speech may shift overall sentiment without changing every individual.

## 7.16.38 Persuasion Through Intermediaries

An actor may use:
- trusted friends;
- respected leaders;
- family;
- messengers;
- experts.

The intermediary's relationship with the target matters.

## 7.16.39 Persuasion and Deception

Persuasion may contain lies.

If so:
- Persuasion governs influence;
- Deception governs whether the false component is believed.

A good argument built on a detected lie may collapse.

## 7.16.40 Persuasion and Manipulation

Manipulative persuasion may hide:
- true motive;
- relevant cost;
- social pressure.

It may succeed short-term while damaging Trust if exposed.

## 7.16.41 Persuasion and Threats

If the primary mechanism is fear of harm or punishment, use Intimidation or Coercion rather than Persuasion.

Do not relabel threats as persuasion merely because they were phrased politely.

## 7.16.42 Persuasion and Romance

Persuasion may communicate relationship desires or discuss:
- expectations;
- exclusivity;
- reconciliation;
- plans.

It should not be used to override lack of Romantic Interest.

Romance develops through the dedicated romance systems later in Ruleset 7.

## 7.16.43 Persuasion and Adult Sexual Interaction

Persuasion can be used to communicate:
- preferences;
- boundaries;
- expectations;
- concerns.

It cannot be used to convert sexual refusal into consent.

If willingness is absent, the valid outcome space ends there.

## 7.16.44 Moral and Ideological Limits

Some requests directly conflict with:
- core values;
- identity;
- ideology;
- sacred duty;
- defining loyalty.

These may be highly resistant or currently impossible.

A Master Persuasion character does not automatically dismantle another person's identity.

## 7.16.45 Desperation

Desperation can temporarily make previously unacceptable actions plausible.

Examples:
- protecting a child;
- avoiding execution;
- saving a village.

This change comes from the target's altered priorities, not because persuasion became magical.

## 7.16.46 Persuasion Failure

Failure may mean:
- refusal;
- continued uncertainty;
- argument rejected;
- trust damaged;
- irritation;
- target ends conversation.

Failure should not always create hostility.

## 7.16.47 Strong Failure

Strong failure may occur when:
- argument insults target values;
- manipulation is exposed;
- pressure crosses a boundary;
- false evidence is discovered.

Consequences may outlast the immediate conversation.

## 7.16.48 Persuasion Success

Success means the actor achieved the plausible desired influence.

Examples:
- target agrees;
- target delays action;
- target reconsiders;
- target agrees to investigate.

Success does not necessarily mean enthusiasm.

## 7.16.49 Exceptional Success

Exceptional success may create:
- stronger commitment;
- lower requested concessions;
- improved trust in relevant judgment;
- future openness.

It cannot jump beyond the plausible outcome ceiling.

## 7.16.50 Persuasion Memory

Major persuasive interactions may become memorable if they:
- change beliefs;
- alter life decisions;
- reveal values;
- occur during crisis;
- involve betrayal or reconciliation.

## 7.16.51 Long-Term Relationship Effects

Repeated persuasive behavior may influence how an NPC sees the actor.

Examples:
- respected adviser;
- manipulative talker;
- trusted confidant;
- exhausting debater.

This becomes pattern memory.

## 7.16.52 NPC-to-NPC Persuasion

NPCs use the same rules with each other.

This enables:
- political persuasion;
- team disagreements;
- family influence;
- ideological recruitment;
- mentor guidance.

The player need not be present.

## 7.16.53 Design Standard

A persuasion mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- treats cooperation and belief as the same thing;
- lets one check rewrite core values;
- ignores evidence;
- ignores target motives;
- allows repeated identical arguments to reroll freely;
- treats emotional appeal as inherently manipulative;
- uses fear-based threats as ordinary persuasion;
- converts explicit sexual refusal into willingness;
- makes strong persuasion equivalent to mind control;
- assumes the target must admit defeat when persuaded.

## Governing Rule

**Persuasion should work by giving an NPC reasons, evidence, framing, or motivation that matter to them, producing only the degree of change that their existing beliefs, values, goals, relationships, and circumstances make plausible.**
