# 7.21 — Rumors & Information Spread

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.21 defines how unverified, partially verified, distorted, secret, and socially transmitted information moves through the world.

The governing principle is:

**Information should spread through actual people, networks, places, and communication channels rather than becoming globally known because an event occurred.**

## 7.21.1 Rumor Defined

A rumor is socially transmitted information whose truth has not been fully verified by the recipient.

A rumor may be:
- true;
- partly true;
- false;
- outdated;
- intentionally fabricated;
- accidentally distorted.

Rumor status describes verification, not truth.

## 7.21.2 Information Packet

A meaningful rumor may internally contain:
- core claim;
- source;
- current teller;
- original source if known;
- confidence;
- emotional tone;
- timestamp;
- geographic origin;
- audience relevance;
- distortions;
- sensitivity.

This allows the engine to trace how information changes.

## 7.21.3 Source Chain

Where useful, preserve:

**Origin -> Teller -> Recipient -> Next Teller**

A recipient may know:
- the original source;
- only the immediate teller;
- no source at all.

Source chains may become incomplete over time.

## 7.21.4 Firsthand vs Secondhand

Information should distinguish:
- firsthand observation;
- direct testimony;
- secondhand report;
- distant hearsay.

Each step away from the original event may:
- reduce source clarity;
- create distortion;
- alter confidence.

But repeated independent sources can increase confidence.

## 7.21.5 Transmission Requires Opportunity

An NPC can only spread information when they have:
- contact;
- communication access;
- time;
- motive;
- opportunity.

No automatic global propagation occurs.

## 7.21.6 Communication Channels

Rumors may spread through:
- face-to-face conversation;
- family;
- team communication;
- clan networks;
- merchants;
- taverns;
- workplaces;
- hospitals;
- academy;
- mission traffic;
- messenger systems;
- written letters;
- official reports;
- criminal networks.

Special communication techniques belong to their relevant rulesets but may accelerate transmission.

## 7.21.7 Social Network Reach

Each NPC has a practical social radius.

They are more likely to spread information to:
- frequent contacts;
- family;
- teammates;
- coworkers;
- friends;
- trusted associates.

Rarely connected people require an actual path.

## 7.21.8 Geographic Spread

Distance should affect speed and reach.

Rumors may move quickly within:
- one household;
- one squad;
- one neighborhood.

They may move more slowly between:
- villages;
- countries;
- isolated settlements.

Travel, trade, missions, and communication infrastructure matter.

## 7.21.9 Transmission Speed

Speed depends on:
- urgency;
- sensational value;
- network density;
- travel;
- communication technology;
- institutional interest;
- public visibility.

Not every rumor should spread at the same rate.

## 7.21.10 Motivation to Share

NPCs may repeat information because of:
- concern;
- excitement;
- fear;
- gossip;
- status-seeking;
- loyalty;
- warning others;
- political motive;
- revenge;
- entertainment.

They may also deliberately keep silent.

## 7.21.11 Motivation to Withhold

An NPC may withhold information because of:
- secrecy;
- loyalty;
- shame;
- fear;
- professional duty;
- uncertainty;
- lack of interest;
- risk.

Knowing a rumor does not guarantee repeating it.

## 7.21.12 Salience

Rumors spread more easily when they are:
- surprising;
- emotionally intense;
- threatening;
- personally relevant;
- scandalous;
- useful;
- entertaining.

But salience should not override actual communication paths.

## 7.21.13 Credibility

Recipients evaluate rumor credibility using:
- source trust;
- specificity;
- plausibility;
- repetition;
- corroboration;
- expertise;
- motive;
- prior belief.

A rumor may spread even when poorly believed.

## 7.21.14 Confidence Transfer

A teller's confidence may influence the recipient but should not transfer perfectly.

Example:
- Teller: certain.
- Recipient: only mildly convinced.

Each recipient forms their own belief.

## 7.21.15 Repetition Effect

Hearing the same claim repeatedly can increase familiarity and perceived credibility.

However:
- repeated reports from one source are not independent corroboration;
- coordinated propaganda may create artificial repetition.

The engine should distinguish repeated source chains where important.

## 7.21.16 Independent Corroboration

Multiple genuinely independent sources should usually increase credibility more strongly than repeated retelling of one source.

This is especially important for investigations.

## 7.21.17 Rumor Mutation

Rumors may change through:
- simplification;
- exaggeration;
- omitted context;
- mistaken inference;
- emotional framing;
- translation;
- memory errors;
- deliberate alteration.

Mutation should be plausible rather than random.

## 7.21.18 Core Preservation

Even when a rumor mutates, some central claim may remain stable.

Example:
Original:
- "Rex argued with a chunin outside the hospital."

Later:
- "Rex threatened a chunin."

Later:
- "Rex attacked a village officer."

The engine may preserve the chain when relevant.

## 7.21.19 Simplification

Complex stories often become shorter.

Nuance may disappear first:
- motive;
- uncertainty;
- context;
- chronology.

This can make rumors more categorical over time.

## 7.21.20 Exaggeration

Tellers may increase:
- number of participants;
- severity;
- danger;
- certainty;
- emotional intensity.

Exaggeration may be intentional or accidental.

## 7.21.21 Sanitization

Information may also become less severe.

Examples:
- embarrassing detail omitted;
- politically sensitive element removed;
- family member softened story.

Rumors do not only escalate.

## 7.21.22 Framing

Two people may transmit the same facts with different framing.

Example:
- "Karn disobeyed orders to save civilians."
- "Karn abandoned his mission objective."

Both may describe the same event while creating different reputational effects.

## 7.21.23 Rumor Branching

One rumor may split into multiple versions.

Different networks may develop different narratives.

These branches need not reconverge.

## 7.21.24 Competing Rumors

Several explanations may circulate simultaneously.

Examples:
- accident;
- assassination;
- internal betrayal;
- enemy sabotage.

NPCs may:
- favor one;
- remain uncertain;
- switch as evidence changes.

## 7.21.25 Rumor Correction

Correction can occur through:
- direct witness;
- evidence;
- trusted authority;
- confession;
- public clarification.

Correction does not guarantee immediate disappearance of the old rumor.

## 7.21.26 Persistence of False Rumors

False rumors may persist because:
- they are memorable;
- corrections reach fewer people;
- people distrust the correction;
- the rumor fits existing bias;
- some actors benefit from maintaining it.

This should emerge from the information network.

## 7.21.27 Secret Leakage

Secrets may escape through:
- careless speech;
- overheard conversation;
- documents;
- romantic or family disclosure;
- intercepted communication;
- bribery;
- interrogation;
- betrayal.

A leaked secret first becomes known to specific people, not instantly public.

## 7.21.28 Leakage Severity

The effect of a leak depends on:
- number of recipients;
- their networks;
- their willingness to repeat;
- credibility;
- sensitivity;
- strategic value.

One leak can remain contained.
Another can cascade.

## 7.21.29 Leak Tracing

When important, the simulation may trace:
- who knew;
- who told whom;
- when;
- which version spread.

This enables counterintelligence and investigations.

## 7.21.30 Information Cascades

A rumor may accelerate when:
- several high-connectivity NPCs repeat it;
- an institution confirms it;
- it reaches a crowded hub;
- it aligns with an existing public narrative.

Cascades should arise from network conditions.

## 7.21.31 Social Hubs

Some NPCs or locations spread information faster.

Examples:
- merchants;
- innkeepers;
- messengers;
- academy students;
- hospital staff;
- clan gatherings;
- mission offices.

Hubs increase reach, not truth.

## 7.21.32 Gatekeepers

Some people control access to networks.

Examples:
- clan elder;
- editor or announcer;
- mission dispatcher;
- intelligence officer;
- gang leader.

Gatekeepers may:
- suppress;
- amplify;
- redirect;
- verify information.

## 7.21.33 Institutional Amplification

Organizations may deliberately distribute:
- warnings;
- wanted notices;
- commendations;
- propaganda;
- official findings.

Institutional amplification can increase speed and credibility.

It may still be false or misleading.

## 7.21.34 Institutional Suppression

Organizations may attempt to suppress information through:
- classification;
- censorship;
- intimidation;
- controlled messaging;
- limited access.

Suppression reduces channels but does not guarantee secrecy.

## 7.21.35 Rumor and Reputation

A rumor becomes reputation when:
- enough relevant people adopt a persistent belief;
- the claim becomes associated with the person;
- it survives beyond the immediate event.

Ruleset 7.20 owns the resulting reputation state.

## 7.21.36 Reputation Feedback

Once a reputation exists, new rumors may be interpreted through it.

Example:
A person known as reckless may face quick belief in a new reckless-behavior rumor.

This can create reinforcing loops.

## 7.21.37 Rumor and Relationship Spillover

Rumors may affect personal relationships only when:
- the NPC hears them;
- considers them relevant;
- gives them some credibility.

Relationship changes should reflect belief, not objective truth.

## 7.21.38 Rumor and Fear

Threatening rumors may spread quickly because people warn others.

This may increase Fear before direct contact.

## 7.21.39 Romantic Rumors

Rumors may concern:
- dating;
- breakups;
- affairs;
- marriage;
- attraction.

These may affect:
- jealousy;
- reputation;
- embarrassment;
- family pressure.

They remain claims until verified.

## 7.21.40 Adult Sexual Rumors

For adults, rumors may include sexual behavior or preferences.

These can have:
- privacy consequences;
- reputational effects;
- relationship consequences.

The simulation should preserve whether the rumor is:
- true;
- false;
- exaggerated;
- unverified.

Private adult sexual state does not become public fact merely because it is rumored.

## 7.21.41 Criminal Rumors

Criminal rumors may produce:
- suspicion;
- fear;
- surveillance;
- avoidance.

Formal legal consequences require appropriate institutional action.

## 7.21.42 Anonymous Sources

Anonymous information generally carries lower source accountability.

Its credibility depends on:
- evidence;
- track record;
- specificity;
- corroboration.

## 7.21.43 Deliberate Rumor Creation

Actors may intentionally seed rumors.

Possible goals:
- reputation attack;
- distraction;
- intimidation;
- cover story;
- propaganda;
- market manipulation;
- political influence.

This may involve:
- Deception;
- Performance;
- Diplomacy;
- social-network access.

## 7.21.44 Counter-Rumor Campaigns

Characters may respond through:
- evidence;
- trusted spokespeople;
- direct testimony;
- alternative narrative;
- public demonstration.

Counter-rumors can create competing belief networks rather than immediately replacing the original.

## 7.21.45 Rumor Saturation

When everyone in a local network has already heard a rumor, repeated transmission adds little reach.

Further spread requires:
- new network bridge;
- new location;
- institutional amplification.

## 7.21.46 Information Decay

Rumors may fade when:
- no one repeats them;
- they become irrelevant;
- newer events replace them;
- confidence decreases.

Important or scandalous rumors may persist longer.

## 7.21.47 Archival Persistence

Even faded rumors may remain in:
- letters;
- reports;
- journals;
- archives.

They can later resurface.

## 7.21.48 Time-to-Spread

The simulation may estimate spread qualitatively:
- immediate;
- hours;
- days;
- weeks;
- months.

Exact timing should only be tracked when consequential.

## 7.21.49 Off-Screen Propagation

Important rumors may continue spreading while the player is elsewhere.

The engine should process:
- relevant networks;
- elapsed time;
- transmission opportunities.

Not every NPC requires individual simulation.

## 7.21.50 Compressed Propagation

For large networks, propagation may be summarized.

Example:
- "By the next morning, the rumor is common among market merchants."

The engine should preserve:
- origin;
- affected network;
- confidence;
- major distortions;

without simulating every conversation.

## 7.21.51 Tier-Based Detail

### Major NPC
Track specific source, belief, and transmission choices.

### Supporting NPC
Track important rumors heard and repeated.

### Persistent Minor NPC
Track only notable local rumor exposure.

### Background NPC
Use population-level exposure where appropriate.

## 7.21.52 Rumor Ledger

For important rumors, the engine may maintain:

**Core Claim:** Rex threatened a chunin.
**Origin:** eyewitness misinterpretation.
**Truth:** Rex argued but made no threat.
**Current Networks:** academy, east-market merchants.
**Confidence:** mixed.
**Major Mutation:** "threatened" -> "attacked" in one branch.
**Institutional Awareness:** none.
**Reputation Effect:** minor notoriety among academy students.

## 7.21.53 Player-Facing Rumor Information

The player should encounter rumors through:
- overheard conversation;
- NPC warnings;
- changing reactions;
- gossip;
- reports;
- public notices.

The player should not automatically know:
- origin;
- accuracy;
- reach;
- mutation.

Those require investigation.

## 7.21.54 Rumor Investigation

Characters may investigate:
- source;
- truth;
- spread;
- who benefits;
- mutation history.

Useful tools may include:
- Gather Information;
- Insight;
- Interrogation;
- records;
- surveillance.

## 7.21.55 Anti-Omniscience Rule

NPCs should never know a rumor merely because it exists.

Each character must have:
- a plausible exposure path;
or:
- belong to a network where compressed propagation reasonably reached them.

## 7.21.56 Design Standard

A rumor mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- makes information instantly global;
- assumes every listener repeats everything;
- makes repeated retelling automatically prove truth;
- ignores social and geographic networks;
- makes rumors only exaggerate and never simplify or soften;
- removes source history when it matters;
- makes correction instantly erase false belief;
- treats secret leakage as universal disclosure;
- requires simulating every individual conversation;
- turns rumor directly into reputation without adoption by an audience.

## Governing Rule

**Information should move through believable social channels, change according to the people carrying it, and affect only characters and audiences that plausibly receive and believe it.**
