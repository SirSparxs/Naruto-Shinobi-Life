# 7.13 — Social Intent & Interaction Categories

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.13 defines the intended social outcome behind an interaction.

The governing principle is:

**Before choosing a social skill or making a roll, determine what the actor is actually trying to change.**

The same words can serve different intents, and the same intent can be expressed through different social skills.

## 7.13.1 Intent Before Skill

Social intent answers:

**What outcome does the actor want from the target?**

Skill answers:

**How are they trying to achieve it?**

Example:
Intent:
- convince a guard to allow entry.

Possible methods:
- Persuasion;
- Deception;
- Intimidation;
- Negotiation;
- use of formal Authority.

Do not choose the skill before identifying the goal.

## 7.13.2 Social Target

An interaction may target:
- one person;
- several individuals;
- a small group;
- an audience;
- an institution through a representative.

Group persuasion should not automatically mean every member reacts identically.

## 7.13.3 Requested Outcome

The intended outcome should be stated concretely.

Weak:
- "I persuade him."

Better:
- "I want him to lend me the key for ten minutes."

This allows the engine to evaluate:
- cost;
- risk;
- feasibility;
- relationship;
- resistance;
- consequences.

## 7.13.4 Outcome Scope

Social outcomes may attempt to change:
- immediate behavior;
- willingness;
- belief;
- emotional state;
- disclosure;
- agreement;
- commitment;
- relationship direction;
- future intention.

Changing a long-term belief should generally require more than obtaining one immediate action.

# Core Interaction Categories

## 7.13.5 Request

Ask someone to do something voluntarily.

Examples:
- lend an item;
- accompany someone;
- provide information;
- help with a task.

A request may require no roll if already reasonable and acceptable.

## 7.13.6 Persuade

Attempt to change willingness, preference, interpretation, or belief through reasons, evidence, framing, or appeal.

Persuasion does not automatically imply manipulation.

It may involve:
- logic;
- values;
- emotional appeal;
- evidence;
- consequences;
- shared goals.

## 7.13.7 Negotiate

Seek a mutually acceptable agreement where both sides have interests.

Negotiation may involve:
- terms;
- concessions;
- tradeoffs;
- deadlines;
- obligations;
- guarantees.

Success may produce compromise rather than total victory.

## 7.13.8 Bargain

A narrower exchange-focused form of negotiation.

Examples:
- price;
- service;
- information exchange;
- favor for favor;
- reduced penalty for cooperation.

## 7.13.9 Reassure

Attempt to reduce fear, anxiety, doubt, shame, or insecurity.

Reassurance may rely on:
- trust;
- evidence;
- calm presence;
- relationship history;
- empathy.

It should not automatically remove justified concern.

## 7.13.10 Comfort

Provide emotional support rather than primarily changing belief.

Possible goals:
- help someone feel less alone;
- help regulate grief;
- provide safety;
- offer affection.

Comfort and reassurance may overlap but are not identical.

## 7.13.11 Befriend

Attempt to create or deepen a friendly relationship.

This is usually a long-term process rather than one decisive check.

Possible actions:
- spend time together;
- show interest;
- provide support;
- find shared interests;
- build familiarity.

Friendship cannot normally be produced by one successful roll.

## 7.13.12 Impress

Attempt to increase:
- Respect;
- admiration;
- curiosity;
- attraction;
- perceived competence.

Methods may include:
- performance;
- achievement;
- style;
- knowledge;
- bravery;
- social confidence.

What impresses one NPC may annoy another.

## 7.13.13 Recruit

Attempt to gain someone's participation in:
- team;
- mission;
- organization;
- cause;
- faction;
- project.

Recruitment may combine persuasion, negotiation, reputation, and incentives.

## 7.13.14 Teach / Mentor

Attempt to transfer:
- knowledge;
- technique;
- judgment;
- habits;
- professional practice.

Ruleset 1 governs learning/progression.
Ruleset 7 governs trust, communication, willingness, and mentor/student relationship.

## 7.13.15 Advise

Offer a recommended course of action.

Advice may be accepted or ignored depending on:
- Trust;
- Respect;
- expertise;
- stakes;
- relationship;
- target goals.

## 7.13.16 Command

Issue an instruction backed by recognized authority.

Command differs from persuasion because compliance may arise from:
- duty;
- hierarchy;
- institutional consequence.

The target still retains agency.

## 7.13.17 Deceive

Attempt to create or preserve a false belief.

Forms include:
- direct lie;
- fabricated story;
- false identity;
- staged evidence.

Deception resolves belief, not objective truth.

## 7.13.18 Conceal

Attempt to prevent someone from learning true information.

Methods include:
- omission;
- changing topic;
- hiding evidence;
- controlled disclosure;
- emotional masking.

Concealment does not always require a direct lie.

## 7.13.19 Mislead

Attempt to encourage an incorrect interpretation without necessarily stating a falsehood.

Examples:
- selective truth;
- implication;
- ambiguous wording;
- strategic omission.

Misleading may be harder to detect than a direct lie.

## 7.13.20 Deflect

Redirect attention away from:
- topic;
- accusation;
- emotion;
- person;
- evidence.

Deflection may succeed temporarily without changing belief.

## 7.13.21 Intimidate

Attempt to influence behavior by creating or emphasizing fear of consequences.

The intended result may be:
- compliance;
- retreat;
- silence;
- disclosure;
- deterrence.

Intimidation may increase Fear, Resentment, or Hostility even when immediately successful.

## 7.13.22 Coerce

Attempt to obtain behavior through serious pressure that meaningfully limits free choice.

Methods may include:
- threats;
- blackmail;
- detention;
- severe leverage;
- institutional punishment.

Coerced compliance should be distinguished from genuine agreement.

## 7.13.23 Interrogate

Systematically attempt to obtain information from a resistant, guarded, uncertain, or potentially deceptive target.

Interrogation may involve:
- questioning;
- evidence presentation;
- contradiction testing;
- rapport;
- pressure;
- verification.

It should rarely resolve as one simple check.

## 7.13.24 Gather Information

Seek information socially without necessarily confronting a target.

Examples:
- asking around;
- casual conversation;
- interviewing witnesses;
- social observation;
- networking.

The information obtained is limited by what sources actually know.

## 7.13.25 Provoke

Attempt to trigger a reaction.

Possible goals:
- anger;
- attack;
- public mistake;
- confession;
- loss of composure.

Provocation may intentionally worsen the relationship.

## 7.13.26 De-escalate

Attempt to reduce:
- anger;
- fear;
- conflict;
- violence;
- public tension.

De-escalation may involve:
- reassurance;
- compromise;
- authority;
- empathy;
- redirecting attention.

## 7.13.27 Apologize

Acknowledge wrongdoing or harm.

Possible goals:
- express remorse;
- reduce Resentment;
- begin repair;
- restore communication.

Apology itself does not guarantee forgiveness.

## 7.13.28 Reconcile

Attempt to rebuild a damaged relationship.

Reconciliation is broader than apology and may require:
- acknowledgment;
- restitution;
- changed behavior;
- time;
- renegotiated boundaries.

## 7.13.29 Confront

Directly challenge someone about:
- behavior;
- lie;
- betrayal;
- grievance;
- suspicion.

The goal may be:
- explanation;
- accountability;
- confession;
- boundary-setting;
- resolution.

## 7.13.30 Set Boundary

Clearly communicate a limit or condition.

Examples:
- privacy;
- contact;
- professional conduct;
- romance;
- adult sexual boundaries;
- acceptable treatment.

Boundary-setting should normally be treated as communication, not as an attempt to control the other person's internal state.

## 7.13.31 Flirt

Express or test romantic or adult sexual interest through playful, suggestive, affectionate, or exploratory social behavior.

Flirting may aim to:
- signal interest;
- test reciprocity;
- create tension;
- increase comfort;
- invite further interaction.

For younger characters, flirting remains age-appropriate and nonsexual.

For adults, flirting may include romantic or sexual intent.

## 7.13.32 Court

Intentionally pursue a romantic relationship over time.

Courtship may include:
- dates;
- gifts;
- emotional investment;
- affection;
- public or private pursuit;
- family/clan involvement.

Courtship is a process, not a one-roll action.

## 7.13.33 Confess Romantic Feelings

Explicitly reveal romantic interest.

The intended outcome may simply be:
- disclosure;

or may additionally seek:
- reciprocity;
- date;
- relationship formation.

The target may respond positively, negatively, ambiguously, or request time.

## 7.13.34 Seduce

For adults only, attempt to increase reciprocal sexual interest, sexual tension, or willingness to move toward consensual sexual intimacy through attraction, flirtation, presentation, communication, or chemistry.

Seduction may aim to:
- make interest clear;
- heighten mutual tension;
- invite an adult sexual advance;
- test or develop sexual reciprocity.

Seduction cannot:
- create consent;
- override refusal;
- bypass boundaries;
- force attraction;
- convert coercion into willingness.

Repeated seduction attempts after clear rejection should normally worsen the social context.

## 7.13.35 Initiate Adult Intimacy

For adults only, clearly attempt to move an existing interaction toward consensual sexual activity.

This intent requires:
- adult participants;
- context;
- actual willingness;
- boundaries;
- consent.

A social check should not substitute for obtaining consent.

## 7.13.36 Reject Romantic or Sexual Interest

Communicate unwillingness to pursue:
- romance;
- dating;
- sexual intimacy;
- further advances.

Rejection may be:
- gentle;
- direct;
- uncertain;
- conditional;
- hostile.

A rejection is meaningful state and should alter future context.

## 7.13.37 End Relationship

Attempt to end or redefine:
- friendship;
- romance;
- partnership;
- alliance;
- mentorship.

The other person does not need to agree for the actor to withdraw from a voluntary relationship.

Consequences still follow.

## 7.13.38 Reveal / Confide

Voluntarily disclose:
- secret;
- vulnerability;
- emotion;
- identity;
- hidden motive;
- personal history.

Confiding often affects Trust, Affection, or intimacy.

## 7.13.39 Seek Validation

Attempt to receive:
- approval;
- reassurance;
- praise;
- romantic affirmation;
- recognition.

This may be explicit or indirect.

## 7.13.40 Social Testing

An actor may indirectly test:
- loyalty;
- attraction;
- honesty;
- commitment;
- willingness.

Examples:
- hypothetical question;
- small request;
- teasing comment;
- controlled disclosure.

Testing can become manipulative if excessive.

## 7.13.41 Manipulate

Manipulation is not necessarily one separate skill.

It is an intent structure where the actor attempts to influence another while obscuring:
- true motive;
- relevant information;
- pressure being applied.

Manipulation may use:
- Persuasion;
- Deception;
- Seduction;
- Intimidation;
- guilt;
- social pressure.

The simulation should track the actual method and hidden goal.

## 7.13.42 Social Intent Can Be Layered

One interaction may contain:
- primary intent;
- secondary intent;
- hidden intent.

Example:

Visible intent:
- comfort someone.

Secondary intent:
- build closeness.

Hidden intent:
- learn whether they know a secret.

This allows nuanced behavior without requiring every sentence to become a separate action.

## 7.13.43 Intent Can Change Mid-Interaction

An actor may begin by:
- negotiating;

then switch to:
- intimidation;

or begin by:
- flirting;

then shift to:
- genuine emotional disclosure.

A significant intent change should update context and possibly the relevant skill.

## 7.13.44 Intent vs Outcome

Success should be evaluated against the actual intended outcome.

Example:
The actor wants:
- target to reveal a secret.

Target becomes angry but says nothing.

Even if the conversation was emotionally intense, the primary intent failed.

Secondary consequences may still occur.

## 7.13.45 Partial Success

Social intents may resolve partially.

Examples:
- request accepted with conditions;
- target reveals only part of the information;
- apology reduces hostility but not Trust;
- flirtation is reciprocated but no date is agreed;
- negotiation produces compromise.

Ruleset 2 owns degree-of-success resolution.

## 7.13.46 No-Roll Intents

Many intents should often resolve without a check.

Examples:
- confiding voluntarily;
- setting a boundary;
- ending a voluntary relationship;
- making a request the target already wants to accept;
- giving sincere comfort when no uncertain objective exists.

A roll is only needed when the intended outcome is genuinely uncertain and contested.

## 7.13.47 Intent Difficulty Is Outcome-Specific

Do not assign difficulty merely to labels.

"Persuade" can mean:
- persuade someone to try ramen;
or:
- persuade someone to betray their village.

Those are not remotely equivalent.

Difficulty comes from the requested outcome and context.

## 7.13.48 Group Intent

When addressing a group, define:
- shared desired outcome;
- important individual holdouts;
- authority structure;
- group norms.

One speech may influence different members differently.

## 7.13.49 Player-Facing Intent

The player's declared intent should usually be clear enough for the simulation to understand the desired outcome.

The engine should infer reasonable intent from natural language rather than constantly demanding formal labels.

Example:
"I tell her I understand why she's angry and apologize for lying."

The engine can infer:
- Apologize;
- Reassure;
- begin Reconciliation.

## 7.13.50 NPC Hidden Intent

NPC intentions may remain hidden from the player.

An NPC may appear to:
- flirt;

while actually trying to:
- distract;
- gather information;
- manipulate.

The simulation should preserve true intent separately from apparent intent.

## 7.13.51 Design Standard

An intent system should be rejected or revised if it:
- chooses a skill before determining the desired outcome;
- treats every conversation as persuasion;
- assumes one social action has only one possible motive;
- makes friendship or romance a one-roll outcome;
- equates intimidation with authority;
- treats manipulation as automatic mind control;
- lets seduction override refusal;
- treats consent as a skill check;
- requires formal intent labels from the player in ordinary play;
- ignores changing intent during a conversation;
- judges success based on drama rather than the intended outcome.

## Governing Rule

**Identify the desired social outcome first, then choose the method, context, and skill needed to pursue it; never let a skill label substitute for understanding what the actor is actually trying to accomplish.**
