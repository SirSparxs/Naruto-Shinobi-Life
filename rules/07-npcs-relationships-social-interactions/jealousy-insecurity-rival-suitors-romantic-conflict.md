# 7.30 — Jealousy, Insecurity, Rival Suitors & Romantic Conflict

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.30 defines jealousy, romantic insecurity, perceived threats to relationships, rival suitors, competition for affection, and conflict caused by uncertainty or competing romantic interests.

The governing principle is:

**Jealousy is an emotional response to perceived relational threat, not proof of love, ownership, betrayal, or actual danger.**

## 7.30.1 Scope

This section governs:
- jealousy;
- romantic insecurity;
- fear of replacement;
- rival suitors;
- competing attraction;
- suspicion of infidelity;
- attention conflicts;
- possessiveness;
- reassurance;
- jealousy-driven behavior;
- romantic triangles and multi-person tension.

It does not define:
- breakup procedure;
- reconciliation after separation;
- general consent rules;
- formal relationship agreements.

## 7.30.2 Jealousy Is Not One Relationship Dimension

Jealousy should usually be treated as an emotional state influenced by:
- Attachment;
- Romantic Interest;
- insecurity;
- expectations;
- fear of loss;
- perceived competition;
- Trust.

It may become a recurring pattern memory or relationship issue, but should not normally be stored as one permanent universal score.

## 7.30.3 Jealousy Requires a Perceived Threat

Jealousy generally arises when a character believes:
- someone important may prefer another person;
- exclusivity may be threatened;
- affection or attention may be withdrawn;
- their social position may be replaced.

The threat may be:
- real;
- exaggerated;
- misunderstood;
- completely false.

## 7.30.4 Jealousy vs Envy

Jealousy:
- fear of losing a valued relationship or position to another person.

Envy:
- wanting something another person possesses.

A character may feel both.

Example:
- jealousy toward a partner's admirer;
- envy toward the admirer’s confidence or status.

## 7.30.5 Jealousy Is Not Proof of Romantic Interest

Jealousy may also arise from:
- fear of abandonment;
- exclusivity expectation;
- status;
- possessiveness;
- insecurity;
- loss of attention.

Therefore:
**Jealous behavior should not automatically reveal love or attraction.**

## 7.30.6 Insecurity

Romantic insecurity may involve fears such as:
- "I'm not enough.";
- "They'll leave me.";
- "They prefer someone else.";
- "I'm temporary.";
- "I'm being compared.";

Insecurity may emerge from:
- prior betrayal;
- low confidence;
- ambiguous relationship status;
- inconsistent reassurance;
- perceived mismatch;
- actual suspicious behavior.

## 7.30.7 Security as Context

High relationship security may reduce jealousy.

Low security may increase sensitivity to ambiguous threats.

Security should emerge from:
- Trust;
- consistency;
- communication;
- commitment clarity;
- prior history.

## 7.30.8 Attachment and Jealousy

Strong Attachment may increase the emotional stakes of perceived loss.

It does not automatically increase unhealthy jealousy.

A secure deeply attached partner may feel less threatened than a less attached but highly insecure one.

## 7.30.9 Trust and Jealousy

High Trust may:
- reduce suspicion;
- support benign interpretations;
- make reassurance more effective.

Low Trust may:
- increase monitoring;
- increase suspicion;
- amplify ambiguous cues.

## 7.30.10 Expectations Matter

Jealousy depends partly on what the person believes the relationship agreement is.

Examples:
- exclusivity;
- acceptable flirting;
- private friendships;
- emotional intimacy with others;
- non-monogamous agreements.

A behavior cannot be evaluated correctly without the relevant expectations.

## 7.30.11 Ambiguous Relationship Status

Jealousy may be especially common when:
- the relationship is undefined;
- one person believes exclusivity exists;
- the other does not.

This should create uncertainty rather than retroactively inventing an agreement.

## 7.30.12 Trigger Types

Possible jealousy triggers include:
- partner flirting with someone else;
- rival showing interest;
- partner spending unusual time with someone;
- secrecy;
- unexplained absence;
- comparison;
- ex-partner contact;
- public attention from admirers;
- rumors;
- changed intimacy.

Triggers should be interpreted through existing context.

## 7.30.13 Rumor-Driven Jealousy

A character may become jealous because of:
- gossip;
- mistaken eyewitness report;
- manipulated information.

Ruleset 7.21 governs the rumor.
This section governs the emotional and relationship response.

## 7.30.14 False Positive Jealousy

A person may interpret innocent behavior as threatening.

Examples:
- friendly conversation;
- professional closeness;
- teammate trust;
- family affection.

The simulation should support jealousy being wrong.

## 7.30.15 Real Threats

Jealousy may also accurately detect:
- genuine competing Romantic Interest;
- hidden flirtation;
- betrayal;
- emotional withdrawal.

Correct suspicion still does not justify harmful behavior automatically.

## 7.30.16 Rival Suitor Defined

A rival suitor is someone perceived as competing for the romantic attention of the same person.

The rivalry may be:
- real;
- one-sided;
- mutual;
- imagined.

The person being pursued remains an autonomous decision-maker.

## 7.30.17 No Ownership Competition

Romantic competition should never function like:
- "Who wins the person?"

The relevant person chooses:
- whom to date;
- whom to reject;
- whether to date anyone.

Suitors compete only in the sense that both seek the same person's interest.

## 7.30.18 Rival Suitor Awareness

A rival may be:
- known;
- suspected;
- hidden;
- falsely imagined.

Awareness affects:
- strategy;
- jealousy;
- confrontation;
- reassurance.

## 7.30.19 Suitor Comparison

Characters may compare themselves to rivals in:
- attractiveness;
- rank;
- skill;
- wealth;
- status;
- history;
- compatibility;
- closeness.

This can affect confidence and behavior.

## 7.30.20 Rivalry Escalation

Romantic competition may progress from:
- awareness;
- comparison;
- tension;
- direct competition;
- hostility.

Escalation should require actual events.

## 7.30.21 Rivalry Does Not Override Friendship

Two friends may become interested in the same person.

Possible outcomes:
- respectful competition;
- mutual withdrawal;
- tension;
- resentment;
- damaged friendship.

The rivalry should interact with the existing friendship rather than replace it.

## 7.30.22 Direct Competition

Characters may try to become more appealing through:
- attention;
- dates;
- gifts;
- social confidence;
- self-improvement;
- demonstrating compatibility.

They should not receive automatic advantage merely for "trying harder."

## 7.30.23 Sabotage

A rival may engage in:
- lying;
- rumor spreading;
- interference;
- humiliation;
- manipulation.

These actions use existing systems such as:
- Deception;
- Rumors;
- Reputation;
- Social Networks.

Sabotage carries consequences if discovered.

## 7.30.24 Jealousy and Possessiveness

Jealousy is an emotion.

Possessiveness is behavior or attitude implying control over another person's relationships or choices.

A character may feel jealousy without acting possessively.

## 7.30.25 Jealousy-Driven Behaviors

Possible responses include:
- seeking reassurance;
- withdrawal;
- confrontation;
- increased affection;
- comparison;
- monitoring;
- hostility toward rival;
- ending relationship;
- doing nothing.

Behavior depends on personality, values, and self-control.

## 7.30.26 Suppressed Jealousy

A character may feel jealous but hide it because of:
- pride;
- professionalism;
- trust;
- embarrassment;
- fear of appearing insecure.

Emotional masking may affect what others notice.

## 7.30.27 Open Jealousy

A character may directly say:
- "That made me uncomfortable.";
- "I'm worried about what's happening between you two.";

Direct communication may improve clarity.

It does not guarantee reassurance will resolve the issue.

## 7.30.28 Reassurance

Reassurance may involve:
- verbal affirmation;
- clarification;
- transparency;
- affection;
- changed behavior.

Its effectiveness depends on:
- Trust;
- credibility;
- underlying cause;
- whether suspicious behavior actually exists.

## 7.30.29 Reassurance Cannot Solve Every Insecurity

If jealousy arises from deep insecurity, one reassurance may provide only temporary relief.

Repeated patterns may require:
- changed behavior;
- greater clarity;
- time;
- personal growth.

## 7.30.30 Excessive Reassurance Dependence

A character may repeatedly seek reassurance.

This may become:
- emotionally exhausting;
- a recurring conflict;
- a dependency pattern.

The system should not treat endless reassurance as the only solution.

## 7.30.31 Transparency

Partners may voluntarily increase transparency by:
- explaining relationships;
- communicating plans;
- clarifying boundaries.

Transparency can increase security.

It should not automatically become surveillance or loss of privacy.

## 7.30.32 Monitoring

Jealousy may lead to:
- checking messages;
- following someone;
- interrogating friends;
- surveillance.

These behaviors may reduce Trust and increase Resentment if unwanted.

## 7.30.33 Control

Attempting to control:
- friendships;
- clothing;
- missions;
- communication;
- movement;

because of jealousy should be treated as a separate behavioral choice with relationship consequences.

Jealousy does not excuse control.

## 7.30.34 Jealousy and Anger

Jealousy may combine with:
- anger;
- shame;
- sadness;
- fear;
- envy.

The resulting behavior depends on the emotional mixture.

## 7.30.35 Jealousy and Pride

Pride may cause a character to:
- hide jealousy;
- become competitive;
- withdraw;
- refuse reassurance.

## 7.30.36 Jealousy and Fear of Abandonment

A character with strong abandonment fears may react more intensely to:
- distance;
- ambiguity;
- delayed communication;
- rivals.

This should emerge from personality and history rather than one universal trait.

## 7.30.37 Jealousy and Past Betrayal

Previous betrayal may create:
- heightened suspicion;
- sensitivity to secrecy;
- stronger reactions to ambiguous cues.

Current partners should not automatically be treated as responsible for past betrayal, but the history still affects interpretation.

## 7.30.38 Jealousy in Non-Exclusive Relationships

Consensually non-monogamous or otherwise non-exclusive relationships can still contain jealousy.

The emotion may arise despite agreed structure.

Relevant questions include:
- Was the agreement followed?
- Was communication adequate?
- What specifically feels threatening?

## 7.30.39 Jealousy Does Not Prove Agreement Violation

A character may feel jealous even when nobody violated any rule.

Emotion and wrongdoing remain separate.

## 7.30.40 Actual Betrayal

If a partner violates an established agreement, the conflict may shift from:
- jealousy;

to:
- betrayal;
- Trust damage;
- rupture.

Ruleset 7.32 governs major betrayal and reconciliation.

## 7.30.41 Emotional Infidelity Concepts

Characters may differ in what they consider betrayal.

Some may care about:
- sexual exclusivity;
- romantic exclusivity;
- emotional intimacy;
- secrecy.

These expectations must come from actual agreements or culturally understood norms.

## 7.30.42 Rival Ex-Partners

An ex-partner may trigger insecurity because of:
- history;
- unresolved attraction;
- familiarity;
- shared memories.

Contact with an ex is not automatically threatening.

## 7.30.43 Celebrity / High-Fame Attention

A famous or attractive partner may receive frequent outside attention.

This may create recurring jealousy pressure.

The partner's behavior and relationship security matter more than the existence of admirers.

## 7.30.44 Public Flirting Toward Partner

Third parties may flirt with someone already partnered.

The partnered person's response matters.

The partner's jealousy should not automatically target only the third party.

## 7.30.45 Partner Responsibility

A partner is responsible for their own behavior and agreements.

A rival suitor does not "steal" someone.

If betrayal occurs, responsibility remains with the person who violated the agreement.

## 7.30.46 Jealousy Toward Friends

A character may become jealous of a partner's close friend.

This may arise from:
- emotional closeness;
- time allocation;
- perceived competition.

Friendship itself is not evidence of romantic betrayal.

## 7.30.47 Attention Jealousy

Jealousy may concern:
- time;
- praise;
- emotional energy;
- status;

rather than romance or sex directly.

This can occur even in secure relationships.

## 7.30.48 Jealousy and Sibling / Family Competition

Romantic jealousy may intersect with family dynamics when:
- siblings like the same person;
- family compares partners;
- clan politics favor another match.

Existing family rivalry may intensify the conflict.

## 7.30.49 Romantic Triangles

A triangle may occur when:
- two people want the same person;
- one person has feelings for two people;
- relationships overlap.

The system should preserve each directional state separately.

## 7.30.50 No Forced Triangle Resolution

The simulation should not assume a triangle must end with:
- one winner;
- one loser.

Possible outcomes include:
- rejection of both;
- friendship;
- changed relationship structure;
- continued ambiguity;
- one relationship;
- neither relationship.

## 7.30.51 Multi-Person Romantic Conflict

More complex dynamics may involve:
- several admirers;
- overlapping partnerships;
- family or political expectations.

The same autonomy rules apply.

## 7.30.52 Jealousy Escalation

Repeated unresolved jealousy may develop into:
- chronic suspicion;
- Resentment;
- controlling behavior;
- frequent conflict;
- withdrawal.

This should occur through repeated patterns rather than one emotional event.

## 7.30.53 Jealousy De-Escalation

Jealousy may reduce through:
- reassurance;
- clarified expectations;
- improved Trust;
- transparency;
- time;
- positive relationship experience;
- removal of actual threat.

## 7.30.54 Self-Regulation

Characters with strong emotional regulation may:
- recognize jealousy;
- avoid impulsive reaction;
- communicate more effectively.

Ruleset 7.8 governs emotional regulation interface.

## 7.30.55 Poor Regulation

Poor regulation may increase likelihood of:
- confrontation;
- impulsive accusations;
- hostility;
- withdrawal.

This does not remove agency.

## 7.30.56 Public Jealousy

Jealous conflict occurring publicly may produce:
- embarrassment;
- rumors;
- reputation effects;
- group tension.

Private conflict may have fewer external consequences.

## 7.30.57 Professional Context

Jealousy inside:
- squads;
- workplaces;
- command structures;

may interfere with:
- cooperation;
- leadership;
- mission performance.

The relationship state should affect social context without automatically overriding professional competence.

## 7.30.58 Jealousy and Violence

Jealousy may contribute to violent motives in some characters.

It should never automatically cause violence.

Violence requires:
- personality;
- escalation;
- opportunity;
- other motives;
- decision.

## 7.30.59 One-Sided Jealousy

A person may feel jealous toward someone they are not actually dating.

Example:
- unrequited crush sees target date someone else.

The emotion is valid.
It creates no relationship entitlement.

## 7.30.60 Rejected Suitor Jealousy

A rejected character may experience jealousy later.

Respecting the rejection means they do not treat that feeling as a claim on the other person.

Repeated pursuit remains constrained by previous rejection.

## 7.30.61 NPC-Initiated Rivalry

NPCs may independently:
- become jealous;
- confront rivals;
- seek reassurance;
- compete;
- withdraw.

They should not wait for the player to trigger these dynamics.

## 7.30.62 NPC-to-NPC Romantic Conflict

NPC relationships may generate jealousy and romantic competition off-screen.

Important consequences may later reach the player through:
- rumors;
- changed alliances;
- group tension;
- direct disclosure.

## 7.30.63 Player Character Jealousy

The engine may plausibly generate:
- jealousy;
- insecurity;
- emotional reaction;

for the player character based on context and established traits.

The player retains control over how the character responds.

## 7.30.64 Hidden Jealousy

NPC jealousy should usually remain hidden unless shown through:
- behavior;
- dialogue;
- visible emotion;
- disclosure.

Exact internal jealousy intensity should not normally be exposed.

## 7.30.65 Jealousy Memory

High-salience jealousy events may become memories.

Examples:
- public flirtation;
- betrayal scare;
- major reassurance;
- humiliating romantic competition.

These memories may influence future insecurity.

## 7.30.66 Design Standard

A jealousy mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- treats jealousy as proof of love;
- treats jealousy as proof of betrayal;
- treats partners as possessions;
- makes rival suitors compete for ownership;
- assumes jealousy always becomes hostility;
- assumes secure relationships never experience jealousy;
- blames third parties for a partner's agreement violations;
- treats non-monogamy as jealousy-free;
- turns one jealous moment into permanent relationship damage;
- removes player or NPC agency because of jealousy.

## Governing Rule

**Jealousy should arise from perceived threats to valued relationships or social position, with its meaning and consequences determined by trust, security, expectations, personality, and behavior rather than by the emotion itself.**
