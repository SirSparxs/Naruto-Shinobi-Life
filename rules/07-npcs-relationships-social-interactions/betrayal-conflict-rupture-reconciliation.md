# 7.33 — Betrayal, Conflict, Rupture & Reconciliation

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.33 defines interpersonal conflict, betrayal, grudges, relationship rupture, apology, forgiveness, restitution, trust repair, reconciliation, and irreversible damage.

The governing principle is:

**Conflict changes relationships according to what happened, what it meant to the people involved, and how they respond afterward; reconciliation is a process of repair, not a reward for saying the right words.**

## 7.33.1 Scope

This section governs:
- interpersonal disagreement;
- recurring conflict;
- betrayal;
- trust rupture;
- grudges;
- estrangement;
- apology;
- forgiveness;
- restitution;
- reconciliation;
- relationship repair;
- permanent scars.

Ruleset 7.31 owns romantic breakup structure.
Ruleset 7.32 owns promises and obligations.
Ruleset 7.11 owns general relationship change.
This section governs the broader rupture-and-repair process.

## 7.33.2 Conflict Is Not Automatically Rupture

Conflict may involve:
- disagreement;
- frustration;
- competing goals;
- criticism;
- misunderstanding;
- argument.

Many relationships can survive conflict with little lasting damage.

Rupture occurs when conflict meaningfully damages:
- Trust;
- Respect;
- Affection;
- Loyalty;
- perceived safety;
- willingness to continue the relationship.

## 7.33.3 Conflict Severity

Conflict severity should depend on:
- stakes;
- frequency;
- emotional intensity;
- public/private setting;
- relationship importance;
- prior history;
- consequences.

One sharp disagreement is not equivalent to repeated humiliation.

## 7.33.4 Conflict Source

Common sources include:
- values;
- goals;
- jealousy;
- unmet expectations;
- competition;
- resource disputes;
- secrecy;
- broken promises;
- loyalty conflict;
- disrespect;
- injury or harm.

The source matters because different problems require different repair.

## 7.33.5 Misunderstanding

Conflict may arise from:
- incomplete information;
- rumor;
- ambiguous wording;
- false assumption;
- cultural mismatch.

Discovering the truth may reduce conflict.

It does not automatically erase:
- hurt;
- embarrassment;
- consequences.

## 7.33.6 Interpretation Matters

Two people may interpret the same event differently.

Example:
- A sees criticism as honest feedback.
- B sees it as public humiliation.

Relationship change follows each person's interpretation.

## 7.33.7 Intent vs Impact

Intent matters, but so does impact.

A character may:
- cause harm accidentally;
- mean well;
- underestimate consequences.

Good intent may reduce blame.
It does not erase the actual harm.

## 7.33.8 Recurring Conflict

Repeated unresolved conflict may create pattern memory.

Examples:
- "They never listen."
- "They always choose the mission over me."
- "They embarrass me in front of others."

Patterns may become more damaging than any single incident.

## 7.33.9 Conflict Escalation

Possible escalation:
1. disagreement;
2. frustration;
3. personal accusation;
4. hostility;
5. retaliation;
6. relationship rupture.

Escalation should require causal steps.

## 7.33.10 De-Escalation

Conflict may de-escalate through:
- time;
- clarification;
- apology;
- mediation;
- compromise;
- withdrawal;
- humor;
- shared priorities.

Different characters respond to different methods.

# Betrayal

## 7.33.11 Betrayal Defined

Betrayal occurs when someone meaningfully violates an expectation of loyalty, trust, secrecy, protection, commitment, or fair dealing.

Betrayal requires an expectation that mattered.

## 7.33.12 Betrayal Is Contextual

The same act may be betrayal in one relationship and not another.

Example:
- revealing a secret promised in confidence;
- sharing information with no expectation of secrecy.

The expectation state matters.

## 7.33.13 Betrayal Types

Possible categories include:
- broken confidence;
- abandoned ally;
- broken promise;
- deception;
- infidelity;
- defection;
- sabotage;
- exposing a secret;
- choosing an enemy;
- failure to protect when expected.

## 7.33.14 Betrayal Severity

Severity may depend on:
- importance of violated expectation;
- harm caused;
- deliberateness;
- selfishness;
- repetition;
- vulnerability exploited;
- public humiliation;
- prior warnings.

## 7.33.15 Deliberate Betrayal

Knowingly violating a trusted commitment usually causes stronger damage than accidental failure.

Possible effects:
- major Trust loss;
- Resentment;
- Hostility;
- grief;
- relationship reclassification.

## 7.33.16 Accidental Betrayal

Someone may violate expectations without intending harm.

Examples:
- unknowingly reveals secret;
- fails to arrive due circumstances.

The harmed person may still feel betrayed.

Later knowledge of intent can modify interpretation.

## 7.33.17 Perceived Betrayal

A character may believe betrayal occurred when it did not.

That belief can produce real relationship damage.

Correcting the belief may help repair but may not erase all consequences.

## 7.33.18 Betrayal by Omission

Withholding information may be betrayal when disclosure was reasonably expected.

Not every undisclosed fact is betrayal.

## 7.33.19 Betrayal Under Duress

A person may betray someone because of:
- threat;
- torture;
- coercion;
- hostage pressure.

The victim may respond with:
- sympathy;
- anger;
- mixed feelings.

Duress changes blame without eliminating consequence.

## 7.33.20 Betrayal of Group

A person may betray:
- team;
- clan;
- village;
- organization.

Ruleset 7.23 governs group relationship consequences.
Formal treason/legal consequences belong elsewhere.

# Rupture

## 7.33.21 Relationship Rupture

Rupture is a major breakdown in the relationship.

Possible indicators:
- Trust collapse;
- severe Resentment;
- avoidance;
- Hostility;
- refusal of contact;
- changed relationship label.

## 7.33.22 Partial Rupture

A relationship may remain active but damaged.

Example:
- still teammates;
- no longer friends.

Or:
- still family;
- emotionally estranged.

Relationship dimensions and labels should reflect the partial change.

## 7.33.23 Full Rupture

A full rupture may involve:
- ended friendship;
- estrangement;
- open hostility;
- permanent no-contact.

Objective kinship or shared history remains.

## 7.33.24 Irreversible Rupture

Some characters may decide a relationship is permanently over.

Possible reasons:
- extreme betrayal;
- repeated abuse;
- ideological boundary;
- permanent loss.

This should be character-specific.

There is no universal "unforgivable event" table.

## 7.33.25 Relationship Scar

Even repaired relationships may retain a scar:
- lower maximum Trust;
- increased sensitivity;
- remembered pain;
- changed expectations.

Repair need not restore the original relationship exactly.

# Grudges

## 7.33.26 Grudge Defined

A grudge is persistent unresolved Resentment tied to a remembered harm.

It may motivate:
- avoidance;
- refusal to help;
- retaliation;
- hostility.

## 7.33.27 Grudge Persistence

Grudges persist more when:
- harm was severe;
- no apology occurred;
- offender denies harm;
- social identity is involved;
- repeated reminders occur.

## 7.33.28 Grudge Decay

A grudge may fade through:
- time;
- distance;
- changed priorities;
- empathy;
- apology;
- restitution.

Some grudges may persist indefinitely.

## 7.33.29 Inherited Grudges

Families, clans, or groups may transmit historical grudges.

Individuals may:
- accept;
- reject;
- reinterpret;
- intensify them.

The grudge should not automatically determine behavior.

# Apology

## 7.33.30 Apology Defined

A meaningful apology generally communicates:
- acknowledgment;
- responsibility;
- regret;
- understanding of harm;
- desire to repair.

Not every apology needs all elements, but hollow wording should matter less.

## 7.33.31 Apology Quality

A stronger apology may include:
- specific acknowledgment;
- no false excuses;
- willingness to hear impact;
- restitution;
- changed behavior.

## 7.33.32 Non-Apology

Examples:
- "I'm sorry you feel that way."
- "I'm sorry, but you made me do it."

These may fail to reduce Resentment.

## 7.33.33 Forced Apology

An apology ordered by:
- superior;
- parent;
- authority;

may satisfy a social requirement without restoring sincerity.

## 7.33.34 Public vs Private Apology

Public humiliation may require public correction.

A private apology may be insufficient if reputation damage remains public.

Conversely, some people prefer private repair.

## 7.33.35 Apology Is Not a Persuasion Shortcut

A successful social roll cannot force forgiveness.

Skill may help:
- communicate sincerely;
- explain;
- avoid making things worse.

Forgiveness remains the harmed person's decision.

# Restitution and Repair

## 7.33.36 Restitution

Restitution attempts to repair practical harm.

Examples:
- replace lost property;
- correct false rumor;
- protect harmed person;
- return stolen information;
- repair public reputation.

Restitution may be necessary when apology alone cannot address consequences.

## 7.33.37 Symbolic Repair

Some harms require symbolic actions:
- public acknowledgment;
- memorial act;
- resignation;
- accepting punishment.

The meaning depends on culture and relationship.

## 7.33.38 Changed Behavior

Long-term changed behavior is often the strongest evidence of repair.

Promises matter less after repeated failures.

## 7.33.39 Repair Time

Trust repair should often take time.

Severe betrayal should not be erased by one successful conversation.

## 7.33.40 Repair Progress

Repair may occur unevenly.

Example:
- Affection returns;
- Trust remains low;
- Resentment falls;
- Respect remains damaged.

This is expected.

# Forgiveness

## 7.33.41 Forgiveness Defined

Forgiveness means the harmed person reduces or releases some desire to punish, resent, or condemn the offender.

It does not necessarily mean:
- forgetting;
- trusting;
- reconciling;
- restoring access.

## 7.33.42 Forgiveness Can Be Partial

A person may forgive the intent but not the outcome.

Or forgive emotionally while maintaining distance.

## 7.33.43 Forgiveness Without Reconciliation

Someone may say:
- "I forgive you, but I don't want you in my life."

This is valid.

## 7.33.44 Reconciliation Without Full Forgiveness

Characters may resume cooperation before all resentment is gone.

Examples:
- teammates;
- family;
- political allies.

The relationship may remain strained.

# Reconciliation

## 7.33.45 Reconciliation Defined

Reconciliation is the voluntary restoration of some relationship after rupture.

It requires:
- mutual willingness to re-engage;
- enough safety or Trust;
- changed conditions or acceptable terms.

## 7.33.46 Reconciliation Is Relationship-Specific

Possible outcomes include:
- restored friendship;
- professional cooperation only;
- family contact without closeness;
- uneasy alliance.

Reconciliation does not always mean returning to the old relationship.

## 7.33.47 Reconciliation Preconditions

Likely factors:
- acknowledgment;
- reduced Hostility;
- credible repair;
- changed behavior;
- shared interest;
- time.

Different characters require different combinations.

## 7.33.48 No Guaranteed Reconciliation

A perfect apology and full restitution may still be insufficient.

The harmed person retains the right to refuse renewed closeness.

## 7.33.49 Conditional Reconciliation

A person may agree to reconnect under conditions.

Examples:
- limited contact;
- transparency;
- no access to secrets;
- professional-only interaction.

## 7.33.50 Rebuilding Trust

Trust may rebuild through:
- small reliable actions;
- consistency;
- transparency;
- accepted vulnerability;
- time.

Trust rebuilding should generally be slower than initial trust formation after severe betrayal.

## 7.33.51 Testing

The harmed person may cautiously test reliability.

This may involve:
- small responsibilities;
- limited information;
- controlled contact.

Testing should arise from distrust, not become a formal minigame.

## 7.33.52 Setbacks

Repair may suffer setbacks when:
- old patterns repeat;
- new lies appear;
- reminders trigger emotion.

One setback need not erase all progress.

## 7.33.53 Changed Relationship Ceiling

Some relationships may never return to previous closeness.

The new stable state may still be meaningful.

# Mediation and Third Parties

## 7.33.54 Mediation

A trusted third party may help:
- clarify misunderstandings;
- reduce escalation;
- structure conversation.

Mediation does not determine who is morally correct.

## 7.33.55 Biased Mediators

A mediator may favor one side.

This can worsen conflict if discovered.

## 7.33.56 Group Pressure to Reconcile

Family, clan, or team may pressure people to make peace.

Pressure may produce:
- superficial cooperation;
- genuine repair;
- further Resentment.

## 7.33.57 Forced Proximity

Teammates or relatives may need continued contact despite rupture.

This may:
- worsen conflict;
- create gradual repair;
- produce professional-only functioning.

# Conflict Outcomes

## 7.33.58 Outcome Spectrum

Conflict may result in:
- clarification;
- compromise;
- unresolved tension;
- Resentment;
- partial rupture;
- full rupture;
- reconciliation;
- new boundaries.

Avoid binary "relationship success/failure."

## 7.33.59 Win-Lose Conflict

A character may "win" an argument while damaging the relationship.

Social resolution should distinguish:
- immediate objective achieved;
- relationship cost.

## 7.33.60 Suppressed Conflict

Avoiding conflict may preserve short-term peace.

It may also allow unresolved issues to accumulate.

## 7.33.61 Productive Conflict

Conflict can strengthen relationships when it produces:
- clearer expectations;
- better understanding;
- fair compromise;
- increased Respect.

This should emerge from outcome, not be guaranteed.

# NPC and Player Agency

## 7.33.62 NPC-Initiated Conflict

NPCs may:
- confront;
- accuse;
- apologize;
- forgive;
- refuse reconciliation;

without player prompting.

## 7.33.63 NPC-to-NPC Rupture

NPC relationships may fracture or repair off-screen when causally relevant.

Consequences may surface through:
- network changes;
- rumor;
- group cohesion;
- changed behavior.

## 7.33.64 Player Agency

The player controls voluntary choices such as:
- apologizing;
- forgiving;
- reconciling;
- ending contact.

NPC social skill cannot force the player's forgiveness.

The player cannot force an NPC to reconcile through ordinary social success.

# Persistent State

## 7.33.65 Rupture Record

For important ruptures, store:

**Relationship**
**Trigger event**
**Perceived betrayal**
**Objective event**
**Trust impact**
**Resentment / Hostility**
**Contact status**
**Repair attempts**
**Restitution**
**Forgiveness state**
**Reconciliation state**
**Persistent scars**

## 7.33.66 Conflict Audit

For major conflict, the engine should be able to answer:

1. What happened objectively?
2. What does each person believe happened?
3. What expectation was violated?
4. How severe was the harm?
5. Was it intentional?
6. What relationship dimensions changed?
7. What repair has been attempted?
8. What still blocks reconciliation?
9. What relationship is realistically possible now?

## 7.33.67 Design Standard

A conflict/rupture mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- treats every disagreement as betrayal;
- makes apology automatically restore Trust;
- makes forgiveness equal reconciliation;
- assumes intent erases impact;
- assumes harm erases all Affection;
- makes severe betrayal recover instantly;
- forces reconciliation because of social skill;
- treats grudges as universal or permanent;
- resets the relationship after repair;
- assumes repaired relationships return to their old maximum closeness.

## Governing Rule

**Conflict should be resolved through interpretation, consequence, and repair rather than binary success states, with betrayal damaging the specific expectations that were violated and reconciliation requiring mutual willingness, credible change, and time where the harm is significant.**
