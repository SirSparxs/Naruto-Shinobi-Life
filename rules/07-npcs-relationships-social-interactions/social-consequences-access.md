# 7.37 — Social Consequences & Access

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.37 defines how relationships, reputation, obligations, trust, group standing, and prior behavior affect what people can realistically access socially.

The governing principle is:

**Social state should change what opportunities are realistically available, but relationships must not become transactional reward tracks where enough points automatically unlock people, secrets, or institutions.**

## 7.37.1 Scope

This section governs social consequences involving:
- information access;
- introductions;
- invitations;
- hospitality;
- shelter;
- mentorship;
- recommendations;
- favors;
- assistance;
- professional opportunity;
- informal institutional access;
- social exclusion;
- blacklisting;
- reduced access;
- changed treatment.

Formal legal rights, wages, mission assignment, ownership, official promotion, or institutional permissions belong to their respective systems.

Ruleset 7 supplies the social context that influences them.

## 7.37.2 Consequences Must Be Causal

A social consequence should answer:

**What changed, who knows, and why does that now affect access?**

Examples:
- trusted teammate shares sensitive information;
- respected medic recommends someone to hospital leadership;
- betrayed friend stops offering shelter;
- notorious criminal is denied casual entry to a secure district.

Do not generate rewards merely because a relationship meter crossed a threshold.

## 7.37.3 Access Is Domain-Specific

Someone may grant access in one domain while refusing another.

Example:
- trusts you professionally;
- will recommend you for a mission;
- will not invite you into their home.

Access should therefore be specific to:
- information;
- space;
- network;
- opportunity;
- assistance;
- emotional closeness.

## 7.37.4 Relationship Access

Relationships may make certain opportunities more plausible.

Possible effects:
- private conversation;
- home invitation;
- personal introduction;
- confidential advice;
- emergency shelter;
- practical assistance.

The relationship opens possibility.
It does not guarantee the outcome.

## 7.37.5 Trust-Based Access

High Trust may increase willingness to:
- share sensitive information;
- lend resources;
- delegate responsibility;
- allow private access;
- rely on promises.

Trust should affect the relevant domain.

Someone may trust another emotionally while not trusting them with classified information.

## 7.37.6 Familiarity-Based Access

Familiarity may grant:
- easier conversation;
- fewer formalities;
- knowledge of routine;
- informal contact.

Familiarity alone does not imply:
- Trust;
- secrecy;
- loyalty;
- unrestricted access.

## 7.37.7 Affection-Based Access

Affection may increase willingness to:
- spend time together;
- provide comfort;
- offer hospitality;
- make personal sacrifices.

Affection should not automatically produce professional or classified access.

## 7.37.8 Respect-Based Access

Respect may increase:
- willingness to listen;
- professional referrals;
- mentorship;
- leadership opportunities.

Respect does not require personal closeness.

## 7.37.9 Loyalty-Based Access

Loyalty may increase willingness to:
- defend;
- protect;
- preserve secrets;
- take meaningful risk.

The action must still fit the character's values and competing obligations.

## 7.37.10 Obligation-Based Access

Promises, favors, and debts may create temporary access to:
- help;
- introductions;
- information;
- resources.

Ruleset 7.32 governs the obligation itself.

The request remains subject to proportionality and autonomy.

## 7.37.11 Reputation-Based Access

Reputation may affect first access before a personal relationship exists.

Examples:
- respected medic receives professional audience;
- notorious shinobi triggers security;
- reliable mercenary receives contract offers.

Ruleset 7.20 owns reputation state.

## 7.37.12 Network-Based Access

A character may reach someone through:
- mutual contact;
- broker;
- family;
- teammate;
- professional network.

Ruleset 7.22 owns network structure.

An introduction reduces distance; it does not guarantee acceptance.

## 7.37.13 Group Standing

Standing inside a:
- team;
- clan;
- workplace;
- village organization;

may affect:
- invitations;
- information;
- participation;
- informal influence.

Ruleset 7.23 owns the group relationship.

## 7.37.14 Information Access

NPCs may reveal information based on:
- Trust;
- need-to-know;
- secrecy;
- relationship;
- professional duty;
- risk;
- obligation.

Ruleset 7.6 governs what they actually know.

A social success cannot reveal information the NPC does not possess.

## 7.37.15 Confidence in Information Sharing

An NPC may know something but refuse to share it.

Reasons include:
- secrecy;
- fear;
- loyalty;
- duty;
- privacy;
- uncertainty.

Access to the person is not access to all their knowledge.

## 7.37.16 Personal Disclosure

Close relationships may make disclosure of:
- fears;
- history;
- goals;
- insecurities;
- family matters;

more likely.

Disclosure should arise from Trust, context, personality, and timing rather than a friendship tier.

## 7.37.17 Secret Sharing

Sharing a serious secret may itself become:
- Trust evidence;
- obligation;
- vulnerability;
- future betrayal risk.

The secret remains subject to Ruleset 7.6 and 7.7.

## 7.37.18 Introductions

A social contact may introduce someone to:
- mentor;
- leader;
- merchant;
- specialist;
- criminal contact;
- clan member.

The introduction changes access because the intermediary provides credibility or context.

## 7.37.19 Introduction Quality

Introductions may vary.

Examples:
- neutral introduction;
- warm recommendation;
- strong endorsement;
- reluctant referral.

The intermediary's reputation and relationship with both parties matter.

## 7.37.20 Recommendation

A recommendation is a reputational transfer attempt.

The recipient may think:
- "If Rena trusts them, I'll give them a chance."

This should improve initial access, not guarantee success.

## 7.37.21 Mentorship Access

Potential mentors may consider:
- talent;
- motivation;
- reputation;
- relationship;
- values;
- availability;
- teaching interest.

A strong relationship may make mentorship possible but should not automatically grant rare training.

Ruleset 1 owns actual learning and progression.

## 7.37.22 Rare-Knowledge Access

Access to rare:
- techniques;
- expertise;
- clan knowledge;
- specialized instruction;

should require appropriate:
- relationship;
- permission;
- trust;
- prerequisites;
- institutional context.

Social access does not bypass progression requirements.

## 7.37.23 Hospitality

Characters may offer:
- meal;
- lodging;
- safe place;
- transportation;
- household access.

Hospitality depends on:
- relationship;
- culture;
- resources;
- urgency;
- safety.

## 7.37.24 Emergency Shelter

Someone may offer emergency shelter despite low closeness because of:
- compassion;
- obligation;
- shared group;
- crisis.

Conversely, a close friend may be unable to help due to genuine risk.

## 7.37.25 Material Help

Social relationships may lead to:
- lending equipment;
- sharing supplies;
- financial help;
- transportation.

Formal inventory and economy systems own the material transfer.

Ruleset 7 explains why the person is willing.

## 7.37.26 Emotional Support

Relationships may provide:
- reassurance;
- advice;
- company;
- caregiving;
- advocacy.

These are meaningful consequences even when they do not unlock material rewards.

## 7.37.27 Advocacy

A trusted or respected NPC may:
- defend someone's reputation;
- speak on their behalf;
- recommend leniency;
- vouch for their reliability.

The receiving institution still evaluates the advocacy.

## 7.37.28 Opportunity Access

Social state may influence access to:
- interviews;
- missions;
- commissions;
- teams;
- social gatherings;
- training opportunities.

Other systems own the final selection.

Ruleset 7 determines whether the person is even considered or introduced.

## 7.37.29 Invitations

Characters may receive invitations to:
- celebrations;
- clan events;
- private gatherings;
- meetings;
- training;
- dates.

Invitations should emerge from actual relationship or role.

## 7.37.30 Invitation Exclusion

Not being invited may result from:
- weak relationship;
- conflict;
- privacy;
- limited capacity;
- deliberate exclusion.

The player should not assume every exclusion is hostile.

## 7.37.31 Social Exclusion

Social exclusion may include:
- not being invited;
- being ignored;
- losing informal access;
- being left out of conversations;
- reduced support.

It may be:
- intentional;
- emergent;
- institutional;
- temporary.

## 7.37.32 Ostracism

Severe exclusion may become ostracism.

Possible effects:
- reduced network access;
- isolation;
- reputation damage;
- loss of support.

Formal expulsion belongs elsewhere.

## 7.37.33 Blacklisting

A network or profession may informally refuse to:
- hire;
- recommend;
- trade;
- cooperate;

with someone.

Blacklisting requires actual information spread and network adoption.

It should not occur globally by magic.

## 7.37.34 Local Blacklisting

A character may be blacklisted only within:
- one clan;
- one merchant network;
- one village;
- one profession.

Scope matters.

## 7.37.35 Blacklist Accuracy

A blacklist may result from:
- verified misconduct;
- rumor;
- political retaliation;
- prejudice;
- false accusation.

The social consequence can be real even when the underlying belief is false.

## 7.37.36 Access Loss After Betrayal

Betrayal may close access to:
- secrets;
- private spaces;
- responsibilities;
- introductions;
- emotional disclosure.

The specific access lost should match the violated Trust.

## 7.37.37 Partial Access Loss

A relationship may remain intact while some privileges disappear.

Example:
- still teammates;
- no longer trusted with private plans.

Avoid all-or-nothing access changes.

## 7.37.38 Restored Access

Access may return through:
- repair;
- renewed Trust;
- changed circumstances;
- restitution;
- time.

Restoration should match the degree of repair.

## 7.37.39 Access Does Not Equal Ownership

Being trusted with:
- home access;
- secrets;
- resources;

does not create entitlement to use them whenever desired.

Access remains contextual.

## 7.37.40 Temporary Access

Some access exists only because of:
- current mission;
- emergency;
- temporary role;
- invitation.

It expires when the context ends.

## 7.37.41 Revocable Access

Characters and institutions may revoke access when:
- Trust changes;
- security changes;
- relationship ends;
- circumstances change.

Previous permission is not permanent.

## 7.37.42 Role-Based Access

A role may provide access independent of personal relationship.

Examples:
- medic enters hospital area;
- squad member attends briefing;
- clan official attends council.

Formal systems own the role.
Ruleset 7 handles interpersonal treatment within that access.

## 7.37.43 Personal Access vs Institutional Access

A leader may personally like someone but still lack authority to grant:
- classified clearance;
- restricted archive access;
- legal privileges.

Personal relationship cannot bypass every institution.

## 7.37.44 Social Access Can Reduce Friction

Relationships may reduce:
- waiting;
- formality;
- uncertainty;
- skepticism.

This is often more realistic than granting entirely new capabilities.

## 7.37.45 Social Access Can Create Priority

An NPC may prioritize:
- close family;
- trusted teammate;
- respected mentor;

over a stranger when competing requests are equal.

Urgency and duty can still override closeness.

## 7.37.46 Favoritism Risk

Preferential treatment may create:
- resentment;
- perceived unfairness;
- reputation problems;
- group conflict.

Leaders especially must balance relationship and role.

## 7.37.47 Social Consequences of Success

A successful social interaction may produce:
- information;
- agreement;
- improved access;
- invitation;
- referral;
- assistance.

The consequence should match the stated intent and plausible outcome.

## 7.37.48 Social Consequences of Failure

Failure may produce:
- refusal;
- hesitation;
- suspicion;
- reduced access;
- embarrassment;
- relationship strain.

Failure should not automatically punish unrelated relationship dimensions.

## 7.37.49 Social Consequences of Exceptional Success

Exceptional success means the best plausible result within the situation.

It might produce:
- enthusiastic recommendation;
- more complete cooperation;
- stronger reassurance;
- additional voluntary help.

It cannot:
- bypass impossible restrictions;
- create authority;
- force secrets;
- override consent.

## 7.37.50 Social Consequences of Strong Failure

A strong failure may:
- damage credibility;
- reveal bad motive;
- close access;
- trigger report to others;
- strengthen resistance.

The consequence should follow what actually went wrong.

## 7.37.51 Access Through Reputation Alone

Fame or status may open a door.

Example:
- famous shinobi gets an audience.

It does not mean:
- private trust;
- secret access;
- friendship.

Initial access and relationship depth remain separate.

## 7.37.52 Access Through Fear

Fear may force short-term cooperation or access.

This should be classified as:
- coerced;
- unstable;
- costly.

Fear-based access can create:
- resentment;
- reporting;
- retaliation.

## 7.37.53 Access Through Deception

A false identity or lie may temporarily grant access.

If exposed:
- access may collapse;
- Trust may be damaged;
- security may increase.

Ruleset 7.17 governs the deception itself.

## 7.37.54 Access Through Leverage

Blackmail or coercion may create access.

This is not genuine social trust.

Ruleset 7.18 and 7.19 govern coercion and autonomy.

## 7.37.55 Player-Facing Access

The player should experience social state through:
- who speaks openly;
- who returns messages;
- who invites them;
- who vouches for them;
- where they are welcomed;
- where they are turned away.

Avoid exposing hidden numerical unlock conditions.

## 7.37.56 No Universal Relationship Rewards

There should be no system like:
- Friendship 20 -> gift;
- Friendship 40 -> home access;
- Friendship 60 -> secret;
- Friendship 80 -> rare jutsu.

NPCs decide what they share based on:
- actual relationship;
- personality;
- context;
- information sensitivity;
- goals.

## 7.37.57 No Gift-Farming Access

Repeated:
- compliments;
- gifts;
- small favors;

should not mechanically grind toward restricted access.

Only meaningful relationship change should matter.

## 7.37.58 Access Can Be Refused Despite High Relationship

A deeply trusted NPC may still say:
- "I can't tell you that.";
- "You're not allowed in there.";
- "I can't recommend you for this.";

because of:
- duty;
- secrecy;
- ethics;
- competence concerns.

That refusal should not automatically damage the relationship.

## 7.37.59 Access Can Be Granted Despite Low Relationship

Someone may help a stranger because of:
- compassion;
- duty;
- emergency;
- shared identity;
- payment;
- professional norm.

Relationships influence access but do not monopolize it.

## 7.37.60 Social Access and Scarcity

When opportunity is scarce:
- one mentorship slot;
- one promotion;
- limited invitation;

social relationships may affect who gets considered.

Other factors such as:
- competence;
- formal rules;
- seniority;

still matter.

## 7.37.61 Consequence Persistence

Once access changes, preserve the state until:
- context changes;
- Trust changes;
- permission changes;
- role changes.

Do not regenerate access from scratch every scene.

## 7.37.62 Access Record

For important relationships or institutions, the engine may store:

**Access Domain**
**Granted By**
**Basis**
**Scope**
**Restrictions**
**Duration**
**Revocability**
**Current Status**
**Known to Player?**

Only meaningful access states should be stored.

## 7.37.63 Social Consequence Audit

For an important access change, the engine should be able to answer:

1. What exactly became available or unavailable?
2. Who granted or denied it?
3. Why?
4. Which relationship, reputation, role, or obligation mattered?
5. Is access temporary or persistent?
6. What restrictions remain?
7. Who else knows?
8. What could realistically change it later?

## 7.37.64 Design Standard

A social-access mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- turns relationships into unlock meters;
- makes high Trust grant all secrets;
- lets friendship bypass formal security automatically;
- makes favors guarantee compliance;
- creates global blacklisting from local conflict;
- treats prior permission as permanent;
- assumes social failure closes unrelated access;
- lets gifts or compliments grind access;
- makes the player automatically prioritized;
- ignores duty, privacy, competence, or institutional limits.

## Governing Rule

**Social consequences should emerge from what people know, feel, owe, trust, and are empowered to do, changing practical access and opportunity without reducing relationships to reward ladders or bypassing independent institutional constraints.**
