# 7.19 — Social Autonomy & Influence Limits

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.19 defines the limits of ordinary social influence and protects both NPC and player agency.

The governing principle is:

**Mundane social influence can alter willingness, interpretation, emotion, perceived incentives, and behavior within a plausible range; it cannot directly rewrite identity, values, memory, knowledge, or voluntary choice.**

## 7.19.1 Influence vs Control

The system should distinguish:

### Influence
Changes how attractive, costly, credible, urgent, or emotionally compelling an option feels.

### Control
Directly determines behavior regardless of the person's own decision process.

Ordinary social skills provide influence.

Direct behavioral control belongs only to systems that explicitly create loss of agency, such as certain supernatural effects governed elsewhere.

## 7.19.2 Social Skills Cannot Override Reality

No mundane social skill can:
- make impossible claims true;
- grant knowledge the target lacks;
- eliminate physical limitations;
- erase institutional consequences;
- create resources that do not exist;
- make an impossible threat credible to someone who knows it is impossible.

Skill modifies social interpretation within reality.

## 7.19.3 Social Skills Cannot Rewrite Identity

A single interaction should not directly rewrite:
- core values;
- long-standing ideology;
- family identity;
- deeply established loyalties;
- self-concept;
- moral boundaries.

Those may change over time through:
- experiences;
- relationships;
- trauma;
- repeated contradiction;
- development.

Influence may begin that process but cannot instantly complete it.

## 7.19.4 Social Skills Cannot Create Knowledge

Persuasion, Intimidation, Interrogation, Seduction, or Leadership cannot make an NPC reveal:
- information they never possessed;
- objective truth they misunderstand;
- memories they do not have.

A social action may instead reveal:
- mistaken belief;
- speculation;
- deliberate fabrication.

## 7.19.5 Social Skills Cannot Automatically Create Emotions

A social action may influence emotion, but cannot guarantee a specific internal state.

Examples:
- intimidation may create Fear;
- comfort may reduce distress;
- flirting may create excitement;
- apology may reduce anger.

But the target's personality, relationship, context, and interpretation still matter.

## 7.19.6 Social Skills Cannot Automatically Create Relationships

One successful interaction cannot normally create:
- friendship;
- loyalty;
- deep trust;
- romance;
- lasting attachment.

Those are persistent social states that develop through history.

## 7.19.7 Social Skills Cannot Force Attraction

No ordinary social check may force:
- Romantic Interest;
- adult sexual attraction;
- adult sexual chemistry;
- affection.

A character may improve presentation or reveal appealing traits.

The target may still not be attracted.

## 7.19.8 Social Skills Cannot Create Consent

Consent is not a relationship score, skill result, or threshold reward.

No amount of:
- Persuasion;
- Seduction;
- Intimidation;
- Affection;
- Loyalty;
- Romantic Interest;
- adult arousal;

creates automatic consent.

Consent remains contextual to the immediate adult interaction.

## 7.19.9 Hard Refusals

A hard refusal means the target has clearly rejected the current outcome.

Examples:
- "No."
- "I am not interested."
- "I will not betray my team."
- "Do not ask me again."

A hard refusal normally closes the current attempt.

Further pressure without meaningful contextual change should not trigger free rerolls.

## 7.19.10 Soft Refusals

A soft refusal may include:
- uncertainty;
- "not now";
- "I need time";
- conditional objection;
- discomfort without final decision.

The system should distinguish:
- lack of clarity;
- hesitation;
- temporary timing problem;
- clear rejection.

This distinction should come from context, not wishful interpretation.

## 7.19.11 Reconsideration

A previous refusal can later change only when there is a causal reason.

Possible reasons:
- new evidence;
- changed relationship;
- different goals;
- new circumstances;
- time;
- changed feelings;
- voluntary reconsideration.

The system should not treat repeated attempts alone as sufficient cause.

## 7.19.12 Boundary Persistence

Boundaries remain in force until:
- explicitly changed;
- contextually redefined;
- voluntarily renegotiated.

A successful prior interaction does not permanently remove a boundary.

## 7.19.13 No Skill-Based Boundary Override

A boundary may affect:
- what actions are available;
- how the NPC interprets pressure;
- relationship consequences.

A high skill value does not make crossing that boundary acceptable.

## 7.19.14 Moral Red Lines

Some characters have red lines they are unwilling to cross under ordinary conditions.

Examples:
- harming children;
- betraying village secrets;
- murdering family;
- abandoning comrades.

A social skill does not automatically convert these into high-difficulty checks.

They may remain outside the current outcome space.

## 7.19.15 Red Lines Can Change Through Character Development

A red line may change after:
- ideological transformation;
- extreme desperation;
- supernatural influence;
- catastrophic loss;
- long-term corruption;
- major character development.

The change must be causal and usually persistent.

## 7.19.16 Goal Conflict Does Not Equal Availability

An NPC wanting one thing strongly does not guarantee they will violate another priority.

Example:
A shinobi may desperately want to save a sibling but still refuse a plan that requires killing innocent civilians.

The decision system weighs competing motives.

## 7.19.17 Relationship Strength Is Not Ownership

High:
- Affection;
- Loyalty;
- Trust;
- Romantic Interest;

does not give one person ownership over another's decisions.

Close relationships create influence, expectations, and consequences.

They do not remove agency.

## 7.19.18 Authority Is Bounded

Formal authority applies only within its legitimate scope.

A superior may:
- issue mission orders;
- enforce policy;
- assign duties.

They do not gain universal control over:
- beliefs;
- private relationships;
- intimate decisions;
- unrelated personal choices.

## 7.19.19 Fear-Based Compliance Is Not Willingness

If someone complies because they fear severe consequences, the engine should track:
- behavior;
- fear;
- willingness separately.

This prevents forced compliance from being misread as:
- loyalty;
- agreement;
- friendship;
- consent.

## 7.19.20 Coercion Changes Interpretation

When meaningful freedom is constrained, apparent agreement must be interpreted in the coercive context.

The system should preserve:
- what pressure existed;
- what alternatives were realistically available;
- why the target complied.

## 7.19.21 Power Imbalance Matters

Power imbalance can come from:
- rank;
- captivity;
- money;
- housing;
- employment;
- political authority;
- medical dependency;
- physical strength;
- institutional control.

A social outcome should not be interpreted as fully voluntary merely because the target verbally agreed.

## 7.19.22 Mundane Influence vs Supernatural Compulsion

Ruleset 3 owns:
- genjutsu;
- mind control;
- supernatural emotional manipulation;
- chakra-based compulsion;
- memory alteration.

Ruleset 7 owns:
- ordinary persuasion;
- fear;
- social pressure;
- relationship influence;
- belief interpretation.

These must never be mechanically conflated.

## 7.19.23 Supernatural Effects Need Explicit Mechanics

If a jutsu can:
- compel behavior;
- alter memory;
- override voluntary action;

it must explicitly say so under Ruleset 3.

No ordinary social skill should gain that capability through extreme mastery.

## 7.19.24 Social Influence Under Supernatural Effects

If a supernatural effect changes:
- emotion;
- memory;
- perception;
- judgment;

Ruleset 7 may consume the altered state.

It should not redefine how the technique works.

## 7.19.25 Player Agency

The player's voluntary decisions should not normally be overwritten by mundane NPC social resolution.

NPC social success may create:
- credible information;
- emotional pressure;
- fear;
- temptation;
- incentives;
- consequences;
- impulses.

The player then chooses the character's response.

## 7.19.26 Mechanical Pressure on the Player

Rules may still create meaningful effects on the player character.

Examples:
- Fear affects available composure;
- deception alters what information the character believes;
- intimidation establishes credible threat;
- seduction may create attraction or arousal if the character plausibly experiences it.

But these should not arbitrarily choose the player's voluntary action.

## 7.19.27 Player Character Internal State

The engine may determine some internal states when appropriate:
- surprise;
- fear;
- embarrassment;
- physical arousal;
- suspicion;
- emotional response.

These should arise from character traits, mechanics, and context.

The player retains control over voluntary behavior unless an explicit loss-of-control effect applies.

## 7.19.28 NPC Agency

NPCs receive the same fundamental protection.

They should not become puppets because:
- the player has high social stats;
- the player is protagonist;
- a roll was exceptional.

NPCs may be heavily influenced while still making decisions consistent with their own state.

## 7.19.29 No Protagonist Privilege

The player should not receive:
- easier social limits;
- automatic romance;
- guaranteed loyalty;
- special access to hidden information;
- exemption from rejection.

Player importance should emerge from causal impact.

## 7.19.30 NPC-to-NPC Autonomy

NPCs should also fail to control one another through mundane social skill.

This allows:
- genuine political conflict;
- rejected advances;
- failed commands;
- persuasion failures;
- unstable alliances.

## 7.19.31 Social Skill Mastery Expands Technique, Not Authority Over Others

Higher mastery may improve:
- timing;
- delivery;
- adaptation;
- cue reading;
- framing;
- consistency.

It does not expand the target's plausible outcome space beyond what their own state permits.

## 7.19.32 Exceptional Success

Exceptional social success may:
- achieve the best plausible version of the intended outcome;
- reduce costs;
- improve terms;
- create stronger future openness.

It cannot:
- override a hard refusal;
- erase core identity;
- produce impossible information;
- bypass adult consent;
- replace supernatural compulsion.

## 7.19.33 Critical Failure

Severe failure may:
- damage Trust;
- increase Resentment;
- expose manipulation;
- harden resistance;
- end the conversation.

It should not automatically produce cartoonishly extreme consequences unless context supports them.

## 7.19.34 Social Exploitation Loops

The system must prevent loops such as:
- compliment until friendship;
- gift until love;
- seduce until consent;
- intimidate until loyalty;
- persuade until ideology flips;
- interrogate until objective truth appears.

Each attempt changes context and faces diminishing returns or hard limits.

## 7.19.35 Dependency Exploitation

Repeatedly exploiting:
- emotional dependence;
- financial dependence;
- institutional dependence;
- fear;
- isolation;

may create compliance.

The simulation should represent the cost through:
- Trust;
- Resentment;
- Fear;
- relationship instability;
- eventual resistance.

## 7.19.36 Social Conditioning

Long-term environments may shape behavior over time.

Examples:
- cult indoctrination;
- abusive hierarchy;
- propaganda;
- institutional culture.

This should be modeled as:
- repeated influence;
- information control;
- dependency;
- social reinforcement;
- punishment/reward;
- identity development.

It is not a single social check.

## 7.19.37 Indoctrination

Major ideological transformation should require:
- time;
- repeated exposure;
- controlled information;
- social reinforcement;
- relationship pressure;
- vulnerability;
- changing identity.

Even then, outcomes may vary by character.

## 7.19.38 Deprogramming / Recovery

Characters may later revise beliefs through:
- new evidence;
- restored social connections;
- reflection;
- contradictory experience;
- trusted relationships.

Long-term influence should remain reversible when causally appropriate.

## 7.19.39 Social Autonomy Audit

For any high-stakes social resolution, the engine should be able to answer:

1. What did the target originally want?
2. What changed because of the interaction?
3. What remained unchanged?
4. Why was the final outcome plausible?
5. Was the behavior voluntary, reluctant, coerced, or compelled?
6. Did any mechanic improperly bypass a boundary or knowledge limit?

## 7.19.40 Design Standard

A social-autonomy mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- turns Persuasion into mind control;
- turns Seduction into consent control;
- treats high Affection as ownership;
- treats Fear as loyalty;
- treats rank as universal authority;
- lets extraordinary success erase hard limits;
- lets repeated attempts brute-force a refusal;
- removes player control through ordinary social skill;
- gives the player special exemption from rejection;
- conflates supernatural compulsion with mundane influence;
- ignores coercive context when interpreting compliance.

## Governing Rule

**Mundane social influence may alter how a person understands, values, fears, desires, or evaluates their options, but the final voluntary choice remains theirs unless a separate explicit mechanic genuinely removes agency.**
