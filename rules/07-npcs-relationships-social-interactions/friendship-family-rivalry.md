# 7.24 — Friendship, Family & Rivalry

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.24 defines the major non-romantic relationship patterns of friendship, family, and rivalry.

The governing principle is:

**Friendship, family, and rivalry are relationship structures shaped by history, expectations, roles, and dimensions; their labels describe the relationship but do not determine how the people inside it must feel or behave.**

## 7.24.1 Relationship Archetypes Are Descriptive

Labels such as:
- friend;
- sibling;
- parent;
- cousin;
- rival;
- mentor-like elder;
- chosen family;

should organize expectations and history.

They should not automatically set:
- Trust;
- Affection;
- Loyalty;
- Respect;
- Resentment;
- Hostility.

Two sibling relationships may have completely different internal states.

## 7.24.2 Archetype Expectations

Relationship categories may create culturally or personally expected behavior.

Examples:
- friends are expected to support one another;
- siblings may expect family loyalty;
- parents may expect obedience from young children;
- rivals may expect competition.

Breaking an expectation matters only when the characters actually hold it.

## 7.24.3 Chosen vs Involuntary Relationships

Some relationships are largely chosen:
- friendship;
- chosen family;
- friendly rivalry.

Others may exist regardless of willingness:
- biological kinship;
- clan relation;
- inherited feud.

The existence of a relationship category does not require positive emotional investment.

# Friendship

## 7.24.4 Friendship Formation

Friendship may develop through:
- repeated positive interaction;
- shared interests;
- mutual support;
- compatible values;
- shared hardship;
- humor;
- vulnerability;
- reliability;
- mutual enjoyment.

No single event should normally create deep friendship unless exceptionally significant.

## 7.24.5 Friendship Preconditions

Friendship does not require identical personalities or beliefs.

It generally requires enough of:
- mutual willingness;
- Affection;
- Familiarity;
- Trust or growing Trust;
- recurring voluntary interaction.

## 7.24.6 Friendship Depth

Friendships may qualitatively range through states such as:
- acquaintance;
- friendly acquaintance;
- casual friend;
- friend;
- close friend;
- deeply bonded friend.

These are descriptive summaries rather than mandatory numerical tiers.

## 7.24.7 Acquaintance

An acquaintance relationship usually involves:
- Familiarity;
- recognition;
- limited history;
- low obligation;
- little emotional investment.

Acquaintance can remain stable for years.

## 7.24.8 Casual Friendship

Casual friendship may involve:
- shared activities;
- moderate Affection;
- enjoyable contact;
- some Trust;
- limited vulnerability.

It need not progress further.

## 7.24.9 Close Friendship

Close friendship often involves:
- high Familiarity;
- meaningful Trust;
- strong Affection;
- emotional support;
- recurring voluntary contact;
- shared memories.

Close friends may still disagree strongly.

## 7.24.10 Deep Friendship

Deep friendship may include:
- strong Loyalty;
- high emotional significance;
- major shared history;
- willingness to accept meaningful cost;
- extensive vulnerability.

This does not imply unlimited sacrifice or unconditional approval.

## 7.24.11 Friendship Reciprocity

Friendship depth may be asymmetric.

One person may view the relationship as:
- close friendship;

while the other sees:
- casual friendship.

These mismatches can create hurt, confusion, or changed expectations.

## 7.24.12 Friendship Maintenance

Maintenance depends on character expectations.

Some friendships require:
- frequent contact;
- shared time;
- emotional support.

Others tolerate years apart.

Routine maintenance should be compressed unless something meaningful changes.

## 7.24.13 Friendship Neglect

Neglect becomes socially meaningful when expected support or contact repeatedly fails.

Possible consequences:
- Affection decreases;
- Resentment grows;
- Familiarity with current life declines;
- relationship category becomes more distant.

## 7.24.14 Friendship Conflict

Friendship can survive:
- arguments;
- competition;
- disagreement;
- periods of distance.

Conflict becomes damaging when it involves:
- betrayal;
- contempt;
- repeated disregard;
- violated trust;
- abandonment during important need.

## 7.24.15 Friendship Repair

Repair may involve:
- apology;
- explanation;
- renewed contact;
- changed behavior;
- rebuilding Trust;
- accepting altered expectations.

Not every friendship can or should return to its previous state.

## 7.24.16 Former Friends

Former friends retain:
- shared memories;
- Familiarity;
- emotional associations;
- network overlap.

A former friend may become:
- stranger-like;
- rival;
- enemy;
- distant acquaintance;
- reconciled friend.

The history remains relevant.

## 7.24.17 Chosen Family

Chosen-family relationships may emerge when friendship, Loyalty, Affection, and shared life take on family-like meaning.

Examples:
- orphaned teammates;
- lifelong comrades;
- mentor households;
- adoptive social bonds.

Chosen family can generate expectations comparable to biological family without requiring formal kinship.

# Family

## 7.24.18 Kinship Is Objective; Relationship Is Social

Biological or legal kinship is a factual state.

The emotional relationship between relatives is separate.

Siblings may be:
- inseparable;
- distant;
- estranged;
- hostile;
- unknown to one another.

## 7.24.19 Family Roles

Relevant roles may include:
- parent;
- child;
- sibling;
- grandparent;
- cousin;
- aunt/uncle;
- guardian;
- adoptive relative;
- clan elder.

Roles influence expectations through:
- age;
- culture;
- history;
- household structure.

## 7.24.20 Parent-Child Relationships

Parent-child relationships may involve:
- care;
- protection;
- authority;
- dependence;
- affection;
- expectations;
- conflict.

As the child ages, authority and dependence should evolve.

Adult children should not be treated as permanently subject to childhood parental control.

## 7.24.21 Sibling Relationships

Sibling dynamics may include:
- affection;
- rivalry;
- protectiveness;
- jealousy;
- resentment;
- role expectations;
- shared history.

Birth order may influence expectations but should not dictate personality.

## 7.24.22 Extended Family

Extended relatives may vary from:
- intimate;
- frequently involved;
- ceremonial;
- nearly unknown.

The simulation should not assume all clan or family members are close.

## 7.24.23 Family Obligation

Family norms may create perceived duties such as:
- care;
- protection;
- financial support;
- secrecy;
- inheritance;
- marriage expectations;
- loyalty.

Obligation may exist even with low Affection.

## 7.24.24 Family Loyalty

Family Loyalty may be strong because of:
- identity;
- Affection;
- duty;
- shared history;
- social expectation.

It can conflict with:
- village;
- spouse;
- friends;
- personal values.

## 7.24.25 Family Resentment

Family relationships often carry strong expectations, so violations may create persistent Resentment.

Examples:
- favoritism;
- neglect;
- comparison;
- controlling behavior;
- abandonment;
- inherited expectations.

High Resentment can coexist with strong Affection and Loyalty.

## 7.24.26 Family Secrets

Families may hold:
- hidden parentage;
- crime;
- forbidden relationships;
- clan history;
- illness;
- financial secrets;
- political agreements.

Ruleset 7.6 governs who actually knows them.

## 7.24.27 Family Reputation

A character may inherit expectations from family reputation.

Examples:
- respected lineage;
- disgraced household;
- famous parent.

This may affect first impressions without determining the individual.

## 7.24.28 Family Role Pressure

NPCs may be pressured to act as:
- heir;
- caretaker;
- protector;
- successful child;
- obedient younger sibling;
- replacement for deceased relative.

Role pressure may affect goals and self-concept.

## 7.24.29 Family Comparison

Parents, elders, or siblings may compare relatives.

This may create:
- pride;
- rivalry;
- insecurity;
- resentment;
- motivation.

The effect depends on the character.

## 7.24.30 Estrangement

Estrangement occurs when relatives substantially reduce or end voluntary contact.

It may result from:
- abuse;
- betrayal;
- ideological conflict;
- boundary violation;
- accumulated resentment.

Kinship remains factual even when the social relationship is effectively severed.

## 7.24.31 Family Reconciliation

Reconciliation may require:
- acknowledgment;
- boundaries;
- restitution;
- changed behavior;
- time.

Family status should not guarantee forgiveness.

## 7.24.32 Adoption and Guardianship

Adoptive and guardian relationships may develop:
- parental attachment;
- duty;
- authority;
- family identity.

The simulation should treat them as socially meaningful family relationships without ranking them below biological relationships.

## 7.24.33 Mentor-Like Family Bonds

Mentors, teachers, commanders, or elders may become family-like.

This can produce:
- parental affection;
- filial loyalty;
- sibling-like bonds among students.

These are emergent relationship meanings, not mandatory consequences of mentorship.

## 7.24.34 Bereavement and Family Change

Death may alter:
- family roles;
- obligations;
- inheritance expectations;
- household leadership;
- emotional support.

The family network should adapt.

# Rivalry

## 7.24.35 Rivalry Defined

Rivalry is a persistent relationship in which one or both people use the other as a meaningful point of comparison, competition, opposition, or motivation.

Rivalry is not automatically hostility.

## 7.24.36 Rivalry Can Be One-Sided

One person may view another as:
- their main rival;

while the other barely considers them competition.

The simulation should preserve asymmetry.

## 7.24.37 Rivalry Sources

Rivalry may arise from:
- similar ability;
- shared goal;
- repeated competition;
- family comparison;
- status competition;
- ideological opposition;
- romantic competition;
- historical feud.

## 7.24.38 Competitive Rivalry

Healthy competitive rivalry may include:
- high Respect;
- moderate or high Affection;
- low Hostility;
- desire to surpass.

Competition may improve motivation and skill development.

## 7.24.39 Friendly Rivalry

A friendly rival may also be:
- friend;
- teammate;
- sibling.

The rivalry adds competition without replacing affection.

## 7.24.40 Hostile Rivalry

Hostile rivalry may contain:
- Resentment;
- Hostility;
- jealousy;
- desire for humiliation or defeat.

It may still include high Respect.

## 7.24.41 Ideological Rivalry

Two people may oppose each other's:
- worldview;
- methods;
- leadership philosophy;
- political goals.

Ideological rivalry can remain nonviolent or escalate.

## 7.24.42 Rivalry Intensity

Rivalry intensity depends on:
- salience of competition;
- frequency;
- emotional investment;
- shared stakes;
- identity.

A minor training rival should not dominate an NPC's life unless the rivalry becomes personally important.

## 7.24.43 Comparison

Rivals may compare:
- rank;
- strength;
- recognition;
- accomplishments;
- relationships;
- approval from mentors.

Comparison can produce:
- motivation;
- envy;
- insecurity;
- pride.

## 7.24.44 Rivalry and Respect

Respect often stabilizes rivalry.

High Respect may encourage:
- fair competition;
- admiration;
- desire to improve.

Low Respect may shift competition toward contempt or dismissal.

## 7.24.45 Rivalry and Affection

Rivals may care deeply about one another.

This can produce:
- concern;
- protectiveness;
- grief;
- competition.

Competition does not cancel affection.

## 7.24.46 Rivalry and Hostility

Rivalry can become dangerous when:
- competition becomes identity-threatening;
- humiliation accumulates;
- values conflict;
- harm occurs.

Hostility is a separate relationship dimension.

## 7.24.47 Rivalry Escalation

Escalation may proceed through:
- competition;
- resentment;
- humiliation;
- sabotage;
- violence;
- feud.

This should require causal events rather than happen automatically.

## 7.24.48 Rivalry De-escalation

Rivalry may soften through:
- mutual respect;
- changing priorities;
- shared hardship;
- reconciliation;
- one person losing interest.

A rival relationship may become friendship or simply fade.

## 7.24.49 Surpassing a Rival

If one rival dramatically surpasses the other, possible responses include:
- admiration;
- renewed motivation;
- envy;
- resignation;
- rivalry fading.

The outcome depends on personality and history.

## 7.24.50 Losing a Rival

Death, retirement, or separation of a major rival may create:
- grief;
- loss of direction;
- relief;
- unresolved resentment;
- memorial motivation.

Rivalry may be emotionally important even without friendship.

# Cross-Archetype Relationships

## 7.24.51 Family + Rival

Siblings may simultaneously have:
- Affection;
- Loyalty;
- rivalry;
- jealousy;
- Resentment.

This is fully valid.

## 7.24.52 Friend + Rival

Friends may compete intensely while remaining supportive.

## 7.24.53 Former Friend + Rival

A friendship may deteriorate into rivalry without becoming full Hostility.

## 7.24.54 Rival + Enemy

A rivalry may coexist with active Hostility.

The rivalry label describes the competitive relationship.
Hostility describes active opposition.

## 7.24.55 Chosen Family + Team

Teammates may gradually develop family-like expectations.

This should emerge through history rather than being granted by team membership.

# Simulation Rules

## 7.24.56 Archetype Formation

A relationship archetype may emerge when:
- state;
- history;
- mutual behavior;
- expectations;

support it.

The engine should not require a formal "friendship unlock."

## 7.24.57 Archetype Transition

Relationships can change category.

Examples:
- acquaintance -> friend;
- friend -> chosen family;
- friend -> former friend;
- competitor -> rival;
- rival -> friend;
- family -> estranged family.

The underlying history remains.

## 7.24.58 Multiple Archetypes

A relationship may carry multiple descriptors.

Examples:
- sibling + rival;
- friend + teammate;
- mentor + chosen parent;
- enemy + respected rival.

Do not force one exclusive category.

## 7.24.59 Archetype Asymmetry

Each person may describe the same relationship differently.

Example:
- A sees B as best friend.
- B sees A as close teammate.

The simulation should preserve both interpretations.

## 7.24.60 Cultural Variation

Friendship, family, and rivalry expectations may vary by:
- village;
- clan;
- generation;
- household;
- profession.

Culture influences expectations without dictating outcomes.

## 7.24.61 Player-Facing Relationship Labels

Labels may be shown narratively when obvious or explicitly established.

Exact internal dimensions should remain hidden during normal play.

## 7.24.62 Design Standard

A friendship/family/rivalry mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- treats friendship as one successful check;
- assumes friendship is reciprocal;
- assumes family implies affection;
- assumes family implies obedience;
- treats adoption as socially lesser by default;
- assumes rivalry equals hostility;
- forces rivalry to be reciprocal;
- makes all siblings competitive;
- treats labels as overriding actual relationship dimensions;
- erases history when the relationship category changes.

## Governing Rule

**Friendship, family, and rivalry should emerge from shared history, expectations, attachment, competition, duty, and personal meaning, allowing the same relationship label to produce very different interpersonal realities for different characters.**
