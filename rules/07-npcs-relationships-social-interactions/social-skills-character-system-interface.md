# 7.14 — Social Skills & Character-System Interface

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.14 defines the social skills used to execute social intent.

The governing principle is:

**Social skill represents learned social technique. Attributes represent the underlying capabilities used to apply that technique.**

Ruleset 7 defines what each social skill means and what it can accomplish.

Ruleset 1 owns:
- skill progression;
- mastery;
- prerequisites;
- training;
- attribute growth.

Ruleset 2 owns:
- difficulty;
- opposed checks;
- uncertainty;
- degrees of success;
- hidden resolution.

## 7.14.1 No Charisma Attribute

The established attributes remain:
- Agility;
- Intelligence;
- Strength;
- Endurance;
- Perception;
- Willpower;
- Chakra.

Ruleset 7 should not add a universal Charisma attribute.

Social effectiveness should emerge from:
- learned social skills;
- relevant attributes;
- relationship state;
- context;
- evidence;
- reputation;
- leverage;
- actual reasonableness of the desired outcome.

This prevents one attribute from becoming the universal solution to every social situation.

## 7.14.2 Contextual Attribute Pairing

A social skill should not always use exactly one attribute.

The relevant attribute depends on how the skill is being applied.

Examples:

Persuasion through logical argument may rely heavily on:
- Intelligence.

Persuasion through reading emotional concerns may rely more on:
- Perception.

Persuasion requiring calm persistence under hostility may rely on:
- Willpower.

The skill remains Persuasion.

The supporting attribute changes with the method.

## 7.14.3 Primary Social Skill Roster

The recommended core social skills are:

1. Persuasion
2. Negotiation
3. Deception
4. Insight
5. Etiquette
6. Leadership
7. Intimidation
8. Teaching
9. Interrogation
10. Performance
11. Diplomacy
12. Seduction

These should remain distinct enough to create meaningful specialization.

Additional niche skills should only be added if repeated simulation shows a real mechanical gap.

# 7.14.4 Persuasion

Persuasion represents skill at changing another person's willingness, interpretation, preference, or position through communication.

Common methods:
- reasoning;
- framing;
- evidence;
- emotional appeal;
- shared values;
- explanation.

Likely attributes:
- Intelligence;
- Perception;
- Willpower.

Persuasion can:
- clarify;
- convince;
- motivate;
- reduce resistance;
- reframe an issue.

Persuasion cannot:
- force impossible behavior;
- override a hard boundary;
- create information;
- automatically create friendship or romance.

## 7.14.5 Negotiation

Negotiation represents skill at identifying interests, trading concessions, structuring agreements, and finding mutually acceptable terms.

Likely attributes:
- Intelligence;
- Perception;
- Willpower.

Negotiation is especially useful when:
- both sides have leverage;
- both sides have costs;
- compromise is possible.

It can influence:
- terms;
- price;
- obligations;
- concessions;
- timing;
- guarantees.

Negotiation differs from Persuasion because the goal is often not to convince the other party that you are right, but to create terms they are willing to accept.

## 7.14.6 Deception

Deception represents skill at creating, maintaining, or presenting false or misleading impressions.

It may involve:
- direct lies;
- fabricated stories;
- false identities;
- selective truth;
- emotional masking;
- maintaining consistency.

Likely attributes:
- Intelligence;
- Perception;
- Willpower.

Different applications may emphasize:
- Intelligence for constructing a plausible lie;
- Perception for adapting to the target's reactions;
- Willpower for maintaining composure.

Deception does not determine whether the lie is objectively plausible.
Context still matters.

## 7.14.7 Insight

Insight represents skill at interpreting social and emotional information.

It may help identify:
- discomfort;
- hesitation;
- hidden tension;
- motive conflicts;
- emotional shifts;
- inconsistencies;
- likely social priorities.

Likely attributes:
- Perception;
- Intelligence.

Insight is not:
- mind reading;
- automatic lie detection;
- access to exact relationship values;
- access to hidden memories.

Insight generates better inference from observable evidence.

## 7.14.8 Etiquette

Etiquette represents learned understanding of social conventions, protocol, ceremony, rank, and culturally appropriate conduct.

Possible domains:
- village protocol;
- clan customs;
- noble courts;
- military formality;
- diplomatic ceremony;
- professional environments.

Likely attributes:
- Intelligence;
- Perception.

Etiquette can:
- prevent accidental offense;
- improve first impressions;
- signal respect;
- navigate formal hierarchy.

Etiquette should allow specialization by culture or institution.

## 7.14.9 Leadership

Leadership represents skill at directing, motivating, coordinating, reassuring, and maintaining confidence in groups.

Likely attributes:
- Willpower;
- Intelligence;
- Perception.

Leadership may influence:
- morale;
- cohesion;
- willingness to follow;
- confidence in plans;
- response to crisis.

Leadership does not create formal authority.

A low-ranking shinobi may be an excellent leader.
A high-ranking shinobi may be a poor one.

## 7.14.10 Intimidation

Intimidation represents skill at making threats or displays of danger credible, psychologically effective, and difficult to dismiss.

Likely attributes depend strongly on method.

Possible attributes:
- Strength for physical menace;
- Willpower for controlled dominance;
- Intelligence for strategically targeted threats;
- Perception for identifying vulnerabilities.

Intimidation may increase:
- Fear;
- compliance;
- hesitation;
- retreat.

It may also increase:
- Resentment;
- Hostility;
- desire for revenge.

Intimidation cannot make an impossible threat credible if the target knows it cannot be carried out.

## 7.14.11 Teaching

Teaching represents skill at explaining, demonstrating, correcting, motivating, and adapting instruction to a learner.

Likely attributes:
- Intelligence;
- Perception;
- Willpower.

Teaching can improve:
- learning efficiency;
- comprehension;
- motivation;
- retention.

Ruleset 1 owns actual skill growth and training results.

Teaching should not duplicate progression mechanics.

## 7.14.12 Interrogation

Interrogation represents skill at systematically obtaining and evaluating information from guarded, resistant, deceptive, or uncertain sources.

Likely attributes:
- Perception;
- Intelligence;
- Willpower.

Interrogation may involve:
- rapport;
- structured questioning;
- contradiction testing;
- evidence presentation;
- strategic pressure;
- information verification.

Interrogation is not automatically intimidation.

A skilled interrogator may rarely threaten anyone.

It also cannot extract information the target does not possess.

## 7.14.13 Performance

Performance represents skill at deliberately controlling presentation for an audience.

Possible uses:
- acting;
- public speaking;
- storytelling;
- disguise support;
- entertainment;
- ceremonial performance;
- emotional presentation.

Likely attributes:
- Agility where physical expression matters;
- Perception for reading an audience;
- Willpower for composure;
- Intelligence for planned presentation.

Performance may support other social skills but should not replace them universally.

## 7.14.14 Diplomacy

Diplomacy represents skill at managing relationships between groups, representatives, institutions, clans, or political actors.

Likely attributes:
- Intelligence;
- Perception;
- Willpower.

Diplomacy includes:
- formal negotiation;
- face-saving;
- symbolic concessions;
- managing competing interests;
- preventing escalation;
- preserving long-term relationships.

Diplomacy overlaps with Negotiation but operates more strongly in institutional and multi-party contexts.

## 7.14.15 Seduction

Seduction is an adult-only social skill representing learned ability to create, communicate, and develop sexual or romantically charged interpersonal tension where genuine potential for reciprocity exists.

Seduction may involve:
- flirtation;
- presentation;
- reading reciprocal interest;
- pacing;
- confidence;
- suggestive communication;
- adapting to another adult's preferences and reactions;
- creating an intimate social atmosphere.

Likely attributes:
- Perception;
- Willpower;
- Agility where physical presentation or controlled body language matters;
- Intelligence where strategic communication or preference-reading matters.

Seduction can improve:
- clarity of sexual or romantic signaling;
- mutual tension;
- comfort with escalation;
- ability to recognize reciprocal interest;
- effectiveness of flirtation.

Seduction cannot:
- create attraction from nothing;
- create consent;
- override refusal;
- bypass boundaries;
- erase incompatibility;
- negate power imbalance;
- substitute for explicit willingness.

A highly skilled seducer can still be rejected.

## 7.14.16 Seduction vs Persuasion

Seduction is not simply Persuasion applied to sex.

Persuasion primarily attempts to change:
- beliefs;
- willingness;
- preferences;
- decisions.

Seduction primarily manages:
- attraction signaling;
- sexual or romantic tension;
- reciprocal interest;
- interpersonal pacing.

If someone has clearly declined intimacy, further attempts should not become a Persuasion problem.

The refusal remains part of the context.

## 7.14.17 Seduction vs Performance

Performance controls presentation to an audience.

Seduction applies presentation specifically toward reciprocal romantic or sexual tension with another adult.

Performance may support Seduction.

It does not replace it.

## 7.14.18 Seduction vs Insight

Insight detects social and emotional cues.

Seduction uses relevant cues to shape an intimate interaction.

A character may be:
- excellent at recognizing attraction;
- poor at expressing their own.

Or the reverse.

## 7.14.19 Skill Overlap

Social skills may support one another without collapsing together.

Examples:
- Insight can support Negotiation.
- Etiquette can support Diplomacy.
- Performance can support Deception.
- Insight can support Seduction.
- Leadership can support Persuasion with a group.
- Deception may support Manipulation.

The primary skill should be chosen from the action's actual method.

Supporting skills may provide context or secondary advantage where Ruleset 2 permits.

## 7.14.20 No Universal Social Skill

No single skill should solve:
- negotiation;
- deception;
- leadership;
- interrogation;
- romance;
- intimidation;
- diplomacy.

This is a deliberate anti-optimization rule.

A master Persuasion specialist may still be:
- a terrible liar;
- poor at formal etiquette;
- awkward at seduction;
- ineffective at interrogation.

## 7.14.21 Natural Talent vs Learned Skill

Attributes may create natural advantages.

Examples:
- high Perception helps read others;
- high Intelligence helps construct arguments;
- high Willpower helps maintain composure.

But attributes should not replace actual training.

An intelligent person is not automatically a skilled negotiator.

A perceptive person is not automatically a skilled seducer.

## 7.14.22 Skill Mastery

Ruleset 1 should determine mastery structure.

Ruleset 7 defines what greater mastery generally means.

Higher mastery may improve:
- consistency;
- adaptability;
- ability to handle difficult contexts;
- recognition of subtle cues;
- recovery from mistakes;
- efficiency;
- ability to manage multiple social factors.

Mastery should not expand the skill beyond its conceptual limits.

Master Seduction still cannot override consent.

Master Persuasion still cannot make someone reveal knowledge they do not have.

## 7.14.23 Specializations

Social skills may develop specializations where useful.

Examples:

Persuasion:
- logical argument;
- emotional appeal;
- motivational speech.

Negotiation:
- commercial;
- hostage;
- political.

Etiquette:
- Konoha protocol;
- clan ceremony;
- noble court.

Interrogation:
- rapport;
- contradiction analysis;
- pressure.

Seduction:
- playful flirtation;
- confident directness;
- subtle tension;
- romantic charm.

Specialization should improve depth without creating dozens of separate skills.

## 7.14.24 Familiarity With Target

Knowing someone well may improve application of a social skill because the actor understands:
- values;
- habits;
- vulnerabilities;
- preferences;
- communication style.

Familiarity is contextual advantage.

It is not a replacement for skill.

## 7.14.25 Expertise About Context

Knowledge relevant to the subject may matter.

Examples:
- legal knowledge during negotiation;
- clan knowledge during diplomacy;
- medical knowledge while reassuring a patient.

The social skill controls communication.
Subject expertise controls whether the content is credible and useful.

## 7.14.26 Social Skill Without Good Content

Excellent delivery cannot permanently compensate for:
- obviously false evidence;
- impossible promises;
- nonsensical arguments;
- unbelievable threats.

Ruleset 7.12 context and Ruleset 7.6 knowledge remain relevant.

## 7.14.27 Good Content Without Social Skill

A socially awkward character may still succeed if:
- evidence is overwhelming;
- request is reasonable;
- target already agrees;
- relationship is strong.

Social skill should matter without replacing reality.

## 7.14.28 Skill Opposition

Different interactions may pit:
- Deception vs Insight;
- Intimidation vs Willpower or relevant resistance;
- Negotiation vs Negotiation;
- Seduction vs social awareness, boundaries, or decision resistance where uncertainty exists;
- Interrogation vs Deception, Willpower, or training.

Ruleset 2 determines opposed-resolution mechanics.

Ruleset 7 determines what the opposition represents.

## 7.14.29 Passive Social Competence

Not every use of a social skill requires deliberate action.

Examples:
- Insight may passively notice an unusual reaction;
- Etiquette may prevent an accidental breach;
- Leadership may stabilize routine group behavior.

Ruleset 2 may use hidden checks where uncertainty matters.

## 7.14.30 Social Skill Training

Possible training sources include:
- mentors;
- missions;
- diplomacy;
- sales/trade;
- command experience;
- intelligence work;
- acting;
- formal education;
- repeated social practice.

Ruleset 1 determines actual growth.

Practice should be meaningful enough to create learning.

Spam should not produce efficient advancement.

## 7.14.31 Failure Can Teach

A failed social attempt may still create learning if:
- the character receives useful feedback;
- recognizes the mistake;
- reflects;
- trains afterward.

Failure alone should not guarantee skill growth.

## 7.14.32 NPC Skill Visibility

Characters generally should not know exact social skill levels.

They may infer:
- "excellent negotiator";
- "poor liar";
- "charismatic leader";
- "socially inexperienced."

Reputation and experience may inform those judgments.

## 7.14.33 Skill Use Should Match Declared Behavior

The engine should infer skill from what the character actually does.

Example:
"I calmly explain the evidence and show why his theory cannot be true."

Primary method:
- Persuasion.

Possible supporting factors:
- Intelligence;
- evidence.

Not:
- whichever social skill has the highest value.

## 7.14.34 Player Choice vs Automatic Optimization

The system should not silently switch to the player's best social skill if their described method uses another.

If the player threatens someone, that is Intimidation even if their Persuasion is much higher.

This preserves meaningful builds and choices.

## 7.14.35 Hybrid Actions

Some actions genuinely combine methods.

Example:
A diplomatic negotiation involving deception.

The engine may:
- resolve the primary objective with Negotiation;
- separately resolve the lie with Deception.

Avoid combining every skill into one giant check.

## 7.14.36 Social Skills and Reputation

Reputation can alter context.

A famous diplomat may receive more initial credibility.

A known liar may face greater skepticism.

Reputation modifies opportunity and interpretation.

It does not directly increase skill.

## 7.14.37 Social Skills and Relationships

Relationships can:
- improve access;
- reduce resistance;
- increase vulnerability;
- create expectations.

A close friend may be easier to reassure but harder to deceive because they know the character well.

Relationships should not simply provide universal bonuses.

## 7.14.38 Skill Ceiling From Possibility

No skill level permits an outcome outside the plausible range.

Examples:
- Persuasion cannot make a loyal ANBU casually betray the village without causal basis.
- Interrogation cannot reveal unknown information.
- Intimidation cannot make an impossible threat believable.
- Seduction cannot turn clear rejection into consent.

Resolution determines quality within possibility.

## 7.14.39 Design Standard

A social-skill mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- introduces a universal Charisma attribute;
- lets one social skill solve every social problem;
- permanently binds a social skill to one attribute regardless of method;
- duplicates Ruleset 1 progression;
- duplicates Ruleset 2 resolution;
- turns Insight into lie detection;
- turns Leadership into authority;
- turns Intimidation into automatic obedience;
- turns Seduction into consent control;
- lets high skill compensate for impossible content;
- automatically selects the player's highest skill regardless of described behavior;
- requires unnecessary skill proliferation.

## Governing Rule

**Use the skill that matches the actor's actual social method, pair it with the attribute most relevant to how that method is being applied, and never allow skill mastery to exceed the real social possibilities of the situation.**
