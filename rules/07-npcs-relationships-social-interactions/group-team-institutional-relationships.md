# 7.23 — Group, Team & Institutional Relationships

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.23 defines the social relationship between individuals and larger groups such as teams, clans, workplaces, villages, organizations, and institutions.

The governing principle is:

**A person's relationship with a group is not reducible to their relationships with individual members, and group membership does not imply uniform loyalty, trust, belonging, or approval.**

## 7.23.1 Scope and Ownership

Ruleset 7 owns:
- subjective belonging;
- identification with a group;
- loyalty and attachment toward a group;
- trust in group leadership or norms;
- resentment and alienation;
- team cohesion;
- informal social standing;
- interpersonal effects of group membership.

Future institutional, political, legal, mission, or faction systems should own:
- formal membership rules;
- rank;
- office;
- legal authority;
- promotion;
- expulsion procedures;
- official discipline;
- governance;
- faction resources.

Ruleset 7 may consume those formal states without duplicating them.

## 7.23.2 Individual-to-Group Relationship

A persistent individual may have a directional relationship toward a group.

Examples:
- Rex -> Team 8;
- Karn -> Tabita Clan;
- Rena -> Kusagakure;
- medic -> hospital;
- missing-nin -> former village.

This relationship answers:
- Do I feel I belong here?
- Do I trust this group?
- Do I identify with it?
- Am I loyal to it?
- Do I resent it?
- Do I feel obligated to it?

## 7.23.3 Group-to-Individual Relationship

A group may also possess a socially meaningful stance toward an individual.

This can represent:
- acceptance;
- confidence;
- suspicion;
- esteem;
- informal influence;
- social exclusion.

This should usually be an aggregate or institutional summary rather than pretending the group has one literal mind.

Where disagreement matters, track important subgroups or individuals separately.

## 7.23.4 Group Relationship Is Not Member Average

Do not average personal relationships to determine group attachment.

A character may:
- dislike most squadmates but strongly believe in the squad;
- love clan relatives but resent the clan institution;
- distrust village leadership while remaining patriotic;
- dislike a commander while remaining loyal to the mission.

Group identity is its own social object.

## 7.23.5 Core Individual-to-Group Dimensions

Useful group-facing dimensions may include:

- Belonging;
- Identification;
- Group Trust;
- Group Respect / Legitimacy;
- Loyalty / Commitment;
- Obligation / Duty;
- Attachment / Affection;
- Alienation;
- Resentment;
- Fear.

Only relevant dimensions need active tracking.

## 7.23.6 Belonging

Belonging answers:

**How strongly does the person feel socially accepted and "part of" the group?**

High Belonging may produce:
- comfort;
- willingness to participate;
- emotional investment;
- identity reinforcement.

Low Belonging may produce:
- isolation;
- guarded behavior;
- withdrawal;
- desire to leave.

Formal membership does not guarantee Belonging.

## 7.23.7 Identification

Identification answers:

**How strongly does the person include the group in their sense of self?**

Examples:
- "I am a Leaf shinobi."
- "I am Tabita."
- "This squad is my family."

Identification may remain strong even after:
- conflict;
- expulsion;
- distance;
- leadership change.

## 7.23.8 Group Trust

Group Trust answers:

**How much does the person trust the organization, its members in general, its procedures, or its leadership to act reliably or fairly?**

This may have domains:
- leadership;
- confidentiality;
- protection;
- competence;
- justice.

A shinobi may trust village military competence while distrusting its political leadership.

## 7.23.9 Group Respect / Legitimacy

This represents how much the person regards the group's:
- authority;
- traditions;
- expertise;
- institutions;
- norms;

as legitimate or worthy of respect.

Low legitimacy can produce resistance even when formal authority remains.

## 7.23.10 Group Loyalty / Commitment

Group Loyalty answers:

**How willing is the person to preserve, support, defend, or remain aligned with the group?**

It may arise from:
- identity;
- duty;
- affection;
- ideology;
- shared history;
- obligation.

Loyalty does not imply agreement with every leader or policy.

## 7.23.11 Group Obligation / Duty

A character may feel they owe a group:
- service;
- obedience;
- protection;
- secrecy;
- repayment.

Duty may remain high even when affection or trust is low.

## 7.23.12 Group Attachment

Attachment represents emotional fondness toward:
- the team;
- clan;
- village;
- workplace;
- community.

It may involve:
- nostalgia;
- pride;
- grief after separation;
- protectiveness.

## 7.23.13 Alienation

Alienation represents felt distance from the group.

Sources may include:
- exclusion;
- cultural mismatch;
- discrimination;
- secrecy;
- leadership conflict;
- prolonged absence.

Alienation does not automatically imply hostility.

## 7.23.14 Group Resentment

Resentment may arise from:
- unfair treatment;
- favoritism;
- institutional betrayal;
- exploitation;
- neglect;
- punishment.

A character may remain loyal while deeply resenting the institution.

## 7.23.15 Group Fear

A person may fear:
- punishment;
- surveillance;
- retaliation;
- exclusion;
- leadership;
- institutional power.

Fear-based membership should remain distinct from loyalty.

# Team-Level Social State

## 7.23.16 Team Cohesion

Cohesion represents how effectively members function as a social unit.

Cohesion may emerge from:
- Trust;
- Familiarity;
- shared goals;
- communication;
- leadership;
- successful shared experiences;
- clear roles.

Low cohesion may produce:
- poor coordination;
- factionalism;
- withholding;
- blame.

## 7.23.17 Cohesion Is Not Friendship

A highly professional team may have strong cohesion despite low personal Affection.

Likewise, close friends may form a poor operational team if:
- roles are unclear;
- discipline is weak;
- conflict is unmanaged.

## 7.23.18 Team Trust

Teams may develop shared expectations such as:
- "Everyone covers each other."
- "No one abandons the mission."
- "Secrets stay inside the squad."

Violation by one member can affect the entire team.

## 7.23.19 Shared Identity

Teams may develop:
- name;
- traditions;
- jokes;
- rituals;
- symbols;
- shared reputation;
- stories.

These can strengthen group Identification and Belonging.

## 7.23.20 Group Norms

Groups establish expectations about:
- honesty;
- discipline;
- risk;
- secrecy;
- conflict;
- emotional expression;
- hierarchy;
- romance between members.

Norms may be:
- formal;
- informal;
- explicit;
- assumed.

## 7.23.21 Norm Enforcement

Norms may be reinforced through:
- praise;
- criticism;
- inclusion;
- exclusion;
- leadership;
- peer pressure.

Formal punishment belongs to the appropriate institutional system.

## 7.23.22 Role Clarity

Teams function better when members understand:
- responsibilities;
- authority;
- expectations;
- expertise.

Role ambiguity can generate:
- conflict;
- duplicated effort;
- resentment;
- insecurity.

## 7.23.23 Informal Roles

Groups may develop informal roles such as:
- mediator;
- organizer;
- emotional anchor;
- skeptic;
- comic relief;
- unofficial leader.

These should emerge from behavior rather than fixed archetypes.

## 7.23.24 Leadership and Cohesion

Leadership may influence:
- clarity;
- morale;
- trust;
- conflict management;
- group identity.

Formal leader and social leader may be different people.

## 7.23.25 Informal Leadership

A lower-ranked member may become the person others actually:
- trust;
- seek advice from;
- follow socially.

This can create tension with formal command.

## 7.23.26 Group Morale

Morale is a temporary collective state reflecting confidence and emotional readiness.

It may be affected by:
- success;
- losses;
- leadership;
- exhaustion;
- fear;
- internal conflict.

Morale is not the same as Cohesion.

A cohesive team can have low morale after a disaster.

## 7.23.27 Collective Emotion

Groups may temporarily share:
- fear;
- grief;
- excitement;
- anger;
- hope.

Ruleset 7.8 governs individual emotion.
This section allows compressed group-level summaries where appropriate.

## 7.23.28 Internal Conflict

Group conflict may emerge from:
- personality differences;
- competing goals;
- leadership;
- resource disputes;
- romance;
- favoritism;
- ideology;
- past betrayal.

Conflict does not automatically destroy cohesion.

## 7.23.29 Healthy Disagreement

High-cohesion groups may argue openly while preserving:
- trust;
- respect;
- commitment.

Suppression of disagreement is not automatically high cohesion.

## 7.23.30 Cliques

Subgroups may form inside teams or institutions.

Cliques can create:
- support;
- identity;
- information silos;
- exclusion;
- factionalism.

These should emerge from actual relationships.

## 7.23.31 New Member Integration

Joining an established group may involve:
- unfamiliarity;
- testing;
- mentorship;
- suspicion;
- social comparison.

Integration should usually develop over time.

## 7.23.32 Veteran vs Newcomer Dynamics

Veterans may possess:
- stronger identity;
- more informal influence;
- shared history.

Newcomers may bring:
- outside perspective;
- uncertainty;
- new skills;
- disruption.

Neither status is inherently superior.

## 7.23.33 Turnover

Members leaving, dying, transferring, or joining can change:
- cohesion;
- morale;
- norms;
- leadership;
- identity.

Groups should adapt rather than remain socially frozen.

## 7.23.34 Group Rupture

Major events may fracture a group.

Examples:
- betrayal;
- catastrophic mission failure;
- leadership scandal;
- internal violence;
- ideological split.

Possible outcomes:
- reconciliation;
- faction split;
- resignation;
- dissolution;
- lasting scar.

## 7.23.35 Institutional Betrayal

A person may feel betrayed by an organization rather than an individual.

Examples:
- village abandons them;
- clan conceals abuse;
- hospital sacrifices patient welfare;
- command knowingly misuses a squad.

This may sharply reduce:
- Group Trust;
- Legitimacy;
- Loyalty;
- Belonging.

## 7.23.36 Institutional Repair

Repair may require:
- acknowledgment;
- restitution;
- reform;
- leadership change;
- demonstrated new behavior;
- time.

One official apology should not automatically restore institutional trust.

## 7.23.37 Divided Loyalty

A character may be loyal to multiple groups whose interests conflict.

Examples:
- clan vs village;
- family vs squad;
- hospital ethics vs military orders;
- village vs romantic partner's village.

Ruleset 7.5 should resolve the resulting decision.

## 7.23.38 Loyalty Hierarchy Is Contextual

Do not assign one universal permanent order such as:
- village > clan > family.

Different situations may activate different priorities.

## 7.23.39 Group Membership and Secrets

Groups may control access to:
- mission information;
- clan techniques;
- personal histories;
- institutional records.

Membership creates opportunity for access, not automatic knowledge.

Ruleset 7.6 governs actual information state.

## 7.23.40 Group Reputation

A group's reputation can influence how outsiders treat members.

Examples:
- prestigious clan;
- feared ANBU unit;
- corrupt organization.

Ruleset 7.20 owns reputation.
This section handles association effects on members.

## 7.23.41 Member Behavior and Group Image

A member's public behavior may affect the group's reputation if observers see them as representative.

The strength depends on:
- member visibility;
- rank;
- role;
- event severity.

## 7.23.42 Group Image and Member Treatment

People may judge an individual because of:
- village;
- clan;
- squad;
- organization.

This can create:
- prestige;
- suspicion;
- prejudice;
- Fear.

Direct experience may override association.

## 7.23.43 Group-to-Individual Standing

A group may maintain an informal or official view of a member.

Possible components:
- trusted;
- respected;
- controversial;
- socially central;
- isolated;
- suspected.

Formal rank or disciplinary status belongs elsewhere.

## 7.23.44 Group Consensus Is Rarely Total

A group should not usually hold one perfectly uniform opinion.

Instead:
- majority view;
- minority view;
- leadership view;
- subgroup views;

may differ.

Use a single aggregate only when the detail does not matter.

## 7.23.45 Collective Decisions

Formal voting, governance, command procedure, or faction policy belongs to future institutional systems.

Ruleset 7 may supply:
- member preferences;
- loyalties;
- social influence;
- relationships;
- group pressure.

## 7.23.46 Group Conflict Spillover

Conflict between two members may spread if:
- others take sides;
- shared norms are violated;
- the dispute affects operations;
- rumors spread.

Not every personal argument should divide the group.

## 7.23.47 Romantic Relationships Inside Groups

Romance between group members may affect:
- privacy;
- perceived favoritism;
- jealousy;
- cohesion;
- professionalism;
- network ties.

The relationship itself remains governed by later romance sections.

## 7.23.48 Breakups Inside Groups

A breakup may create:
- awkwardness;
- divided friends;
- professionalism challenges;
- changed routines.

It does not automatically make the team dysfunctional.

## 7.23.49 Exclusion and Belonging

Repeated exclusion may lower Belonging and increase Alienation.

Inclusion may strengthen:
- identity;
- attachment;
- loyalty.

The effect depends on whether the character values membership.

## 7.23.50 Leaving a Group

Leaving may be:
- voluntary;
- reluctant;
- forced;
- temporary.

It can produce:
- relief;
- grief;
- resentment;
- identity crisis;
- continued loyalty.

Formal resignation or expulsion mechanics belong elsewhere.

## 7.23.51 Former-Member Relationships

A former member may retain:
- loyalty;
- secrets;
- friends;
- resentment;
- identity.

Leaving an institution does not erase social history.

## 7.23.52 Group Relationship Change

Individual-to-group state may change through:
- fair treatment;
- shared success;
- recognition;
- neglect;
- betrayal;
- discrimination;
- reform;
- leadership change;
- group protection.

Ruleset 7.11 principles apply.

## 7.23.53 Group-Level Memory

Teams and institutions may develop shared narratives:
- founding event;
- famous mission;
- betrayal;
- tragedy;
- victory.

Individual members may remember or interpret these differently.

## 7.23.54 Off-Screen Group Simulation

Important groups may evolve while the player is absent:
- member conflict;
- cohesion change;
- leadership influence;
- recruitment;
- social splits.

Only consequential developments require explicit processing.

## 7.23.55 Group Simulation Depth

### Major Team / Group
Track:
- cohesion;
- morale;
- norms;
- important subgroups;
- leadership;
- member integration;
- major tensions.

### Supporting Group
Track:
- broad cohesion;
- leader;
- dominant norms;
- major current conflict.

### Background Group
Use role, culture, and current context only.

## 7.23.56 Player-Facing Group State

Exact cohesion or institutional relationship values should normally remain hidden.

Players infer them through:
- meetings;
- behavior;
- invitations;
- exclusion;
- gossip;
- cooperation;
- conflict.

## 7.23.57 Group Audit

For an important team or institution, the engine should be able to answer:

1. Who feels they belong?
2. Who actually trusts the group?
3. Who holds informal influence?
4. What norms define membership?
5. What current conflicts threaten cohesion?
6. Where are loyalties divided?
7. How does the group see the player or relevant NPC?
8. What changed recently?

## 7.23.58 Design Standard

A group-relationship mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- treats membership as automatic loyalty;
- averages all member relationships into one score;
- assumes all members share one opinion;
- equates cohesion with friendship;
- equates morale with cohesion;
- makes rank equal social influence;
- ignores informal leadership;
- treats clan or village identity as unchangeable;
- duplicates formal institutional mechanics;
- requires full simulation of every member in large organizations.

## Governing Rule

**Groups should function as social systems made from overlapping individual relationships, shared identity, norms, leadership, and history, allowing people to belong, distrust, love, resent, obey, challenge, or leave the same institution for different reasons.**
