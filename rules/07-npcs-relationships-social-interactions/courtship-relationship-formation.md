# 7.27 — Courtship & Relationship Formation

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.27 defines how mutual attraction or romantic interest develops into dating, courtship, explicit relationship agreements, commitment, and established partnership.

The governing principle is:

**A romantic relationship forms through mutual recognition and agreement, not merely because attraction is high or enough successful social interactions have occurred.**

## 7.27.1 Scope

This section governs:
- dating;
- courtship;
- mutually acknowledged romantic exploration;
- relationship labels;
- exclusivity discussions;
- relationship structure;
- compatibility discovery;
- early commitment;
- mismatched expectations;
- transition into established partnership.

Later sections govern:
- established romance and intimacy;
- consent and boundaries;
- jealousy and romantic conflict;
- breakups and reconciliation.

## 7.27.2 Mutual Interest Is Not Yet a Relationship

Two people may both possess:
- Romantic Interest;
- Attraction;
- Affection;
- chemistry;

without being in a relationship.

A relationship requires some form of:
- mutual recognition;
- shared expectation;
- agreement;
- established pattern.

## 7.27.3 Relationship Formation Is Socially Defined

Different characters and cultures may recognize relationship formation differently.

Examples:
- explicit conversation;
- repeated dates;
- family or clan acknowledgment;
- public declaration;
- exclusive behavior;
- culturally recognized courtship ritual.

The engine should not assume one modern dating script applies universally.

## 7.27.4 Courtship Defined

Courtship is the process through which two people with potential romantic interest explore:
- mutual interest;
- compatibility;
- trust;
- expectations;
- commitment.

Courtship may be:
- informal;
- formal;
- brief;
- extended;
- private;
- socially supervised.

## 7.27.5 Dating

A date is an interaction mutually understood as carrying romantic exploratory intent.

Possible activities:
- meal;
- walk;
- festival;
- training together;
- private conversation;
- shared hobby;
- cultural event.

The activity itself does not make it a date.

Shared interpretation does.

## 7.27.6 Ambiguous Dating

Two people may disagree about whether an interaction was a date.

Example:
- A believes it was clearly romantic.
- B thought they were simply spending time together.

This may create:
- embarrassment;
- confusion;
- disappointment;
- clarification.

## 7.27.7 Asking for a Date

Asking someone on a date usually does not require a skill check merely to express the request.

The uncertainty lies in:
- whether the other person wants to accept;
- how the request is received;
- what social consequences follow.

## 7.27.8 Date Acceptance

Acceptance may depend on:
- Romantic Interest;
- curiosity;
- Affection;
- attraction;
- availability;
- existing commitments;
- social context;
- perceived compatibility.

Someone may accept a date without already having strong Romantic Interest.

## 7.27.9 Dating as Exploration

Early dating should reveal information.

Characters may learn:
- communication style;
- habits;
- values;
- humor;
- ambition;
- conflict style;
- lifestyle expectations;
- family expectations;
- adult compatibility where relevant.

Dating is not merely a way to increase romance meters.

## 7.27.10 Positive Dates

A positive date may improve:
- Affection;
- Familiarity;
- Romantic Interest;
- Trust;
- chemistry.

The exact changes depend on what actually occurred.

## 7.27.11 Neutral Dates

A date may be pleasant but not transformative.

Possible result:
- "I enjoyed that, but I'm still unsure."

Neutral outcomes should be common.

## 7.27.12 Poor Dates

A poor date may reveal:
- awkwardness;
- incompatible values;
- mismatched expectations;
- lack of chemistry;
- poor communication.

A poor date does not always eliminate interest.

## 7.27.13 Compatibility Discovery

Courtship should gradually reveal compatibility in areas such as:
- emotional needs;
- values;
- communication;
- lifestyle;
- career;
- village allegiance;
- family expectations;
- privacy;
- independence;
- relationship structure.

For adults, it may also reveal:
- sexual preferences;
- intimacy expectations;
- exclusivity expectations.

## 7.27.14 Compatibility Can Be Unknown

Two people may begin a relationship before understanding major incompatibilities.

This is realistic and creates later conflict.

The engine should not require full compatibility knowledge before relationship formation.

## 7.27.15 Compatibility Is Weighted Individually

The same incompatibility may be:
- trivial to one person;
- relationship-ending to another.

Example:
Different career priorities may be manageable for one couple but impossible for another.

## 7.27.16 Courtship Pace

Characters may prefer:
- rapid commitment;
- slow progression;
- prolonged ambiguity;
- formal courtship;
- casual dating.

Pace should depend on:
- personality;
- culture;
- experience;
- age;
- confidence;
- relationship history;
- current life circumstances.

## 7.27.17 Pace Mismatch

One person may want:
- exclusivity quickly;

while the other wants:
- more time.

This should create a real negotiation rather than automatically selecting the faster or slower pace.

## 7.27.18 Romantic Initiative

Either party may:
- plan dates;
- initiate conversation;
- define the relationship;
- confess feelings;
- suggest exclusivity.

NPCs should initiate based on their own motives and personality.

## 7.27.19 Reciprocity Over Time

Healthy courtship usually involves some degree of reciprocal investment.

Possible signs:
- both initiate contact;
- both make time;
- both disclose;
- both accommodate.

One-sided effort may indicate:
- lower interest;
- uncertainty;
- personality difference;
- contextual barriers.

It should not be interpreted automatically.

## 7.27.20 Courtship Effort

Effort may include:
- time;
- planning;
- emotional vulnerability;
- practical help;
- gifts;
- social risk.

Effort gains meaning from context.

Expensive gifts should not mechanically purchase romance.

## 7.27.21 Gifts

Gifts may communicate:
- affection;
- courtship intent;
- apology;
- status;
- obligation.

Effect depends on:
- appropriateness;
- culture;
- relationship stage;
- recipient preferences.

Repeated gifts should not function as romance grinding.

## 7.27.22 Family and Clan Expectations

Courtship may be affected by:
- family approval;
- clan politics;
- social status;
- village rivalry;
- inheritance expectations;
- cultural tradition.

These pressures affect context but do not erase individual agency.

## 7.27.23 Social Approval

Friends, family, teammates, or clan members may:
- approve;
- disapprove;
- interfere;
- encourage;
- gossip.

Their reactions can influence:
- stress;
- secrecy;
- confidence;
- relationship difficulty.

## 7.27.24 Secrecy

A developing relationship may be:
- public;
- private;
- secret.

Reasons for secrecy may include:
- professionalism;
- clan conflict;
- rank;
- politics;
- fear of gossip;
- existing obligations.

Secrecy may create stress even when mutually agreed.

## 7.27.25 Public Recognition

Becoming publicly recognized as a couple may alter:
- reputation;
- social networks;
- family relationships;
- group dynamics.

Public recognition is separate from private relationship status.

## 7.27.26 Relationship Labels

Possible labels may include:
- dating;
- courting;
- partner;
- boyfriend/girlfriend or cultural equivalent;
- engaged;
- spouse.

Labels should represent shared expectations rather than arbitrary progression tiers.

## 7.27.27 Defining the Relationship

A "define the relationship" conversation may clarify:
- whether the relationship is romantic;
- exclusivity;
- seriousness;
- expectations;
- future intent.

This conversation generally does not require a roll simply to occur.

Each person's decision remains their own.

## 7.27.28 Relationship Structure

Characters may prefer:
- monogamy;
- consensual non-monogamy;
- casual dating;
- exclusive courtship;
- long-term partnership.

Compatibility in structure matters independently from attraction.

## 7.27.29 Exclusivity

Exclusivity should never be assumed solely because:
- dates occurred;
- intimacy occurred;
- attraction is mutual.

It should come from:
- cultural default known to both;
- explicit conversation;
- clearly shared expectation.

## 7.27.30 Ambiguous Exclusivity

If two people hold different assumptions:
- one believes they are exclusive;
- the other believes they are not;

future conflict may occur even without intentional betrayal.

The engine should preserve each person's expectation state.

## 7.27.31 Commitment

Commitment represents intention to maintain and prioritize the relationship over time.

It may involve:
- exclusivity;
- shared planning;
- emotional investment;
- sacrifice;
- public recognition.

Commitment is distinct from Affection or attraction.

## 7.27.32 Commitment Can Be Asymmetric

One partner may be:
- deeply committed;

while the other remains:
- uncertain;
- exploratory;
- casual.

This mismatch should remain possible until clarified.

## 7.27.33 Relationship Formation Threshold

There should be no universal numerical threshold such as:
- Romantic Interest 70;
- Trust 50;
- Affection 60.

Instead, formation becomes plausible when:
- both want some form of relationship;
- expectations are sufficiently compatible;
- barriers do not currently prevent it.

The relationship begins when the people mutually establish it.

## 7.27.34 High Attraction Is Not Enough

Two people may have:
- very high attraction;
- strong chemistry;

but decide not to date because of:
- values;
- duty;
- incompatibility;
- existing relationships;
- political consequences.

## 7.27.35 Low Initial Attraction Can Still Develop

Two people may begin dating because of:
- curiosity;
- compatibility;
- emotional connection;

with attraction developing later.

This should remain possible.

## 7.27.36 Courtship Failure

Courtship may end because:
- interest fades;
- incompatibility emerges;
- expectations differ;
- timing is poor;
- family or duty pressure is too high.

Not every failed courtship should become a dramatic breakup.

## 7.27.37 Graceful Courtship Ending

Characters may mutually conclude:
- "We're better as friends."
- "This isn't working."

Possible outcomes:
- preserved friendship;
- temporary awkwardness;
- disappointment;
- relief.

## 7.27.38 One-Sided Courtship Ending

One person may wish to continue while the other stops.

The refusal ends the current romantic progression.

Persistent feelings may remain.

## 7.27.39 Relationship Formation Event

When a relationship becomes explicit, store:
- participants;
- relationship label;
- date / time;
- structure;
- exclusivity state;
- known expectations;
- public/private status.

This provides continuity.

## 7.27.40 Early Relationship State

At formation, the relationship may still contain:
- uncertainty;
- insecurity;
- undiscovered incompatibility;
- mismatched intensity.

"Official" does not mean stable.

## 7.27.41 Early Expectations

New relationships may develop expectations about:
- communication;
- time together;
- affection;
- privacy;
- exclusivity;
- support;
- future plans.

These expectations should become persistent social context.

## 7.27.42 Expectation Negotiation

Partners may need to negotiate:
- frequency of contact;
- independence;
- public affection;
- mission priorities;
- family involvement;
- secrecy;
- exclusivity.

Negotiation can succeed without either person getting everything they want.

## 7.27.43 Unspoken Expectations

Some expectations may remain unstated.

This can create future misunderstandings.

The engine should distinguish:
- expectation exists;
- expectation communicated;
- expectation mutually agreed.

## 7.27.44 Misaligned Relationship Labels

One person may think:
- "serious relationship."

The other:
- "casual dating."

Until clarified, both beliefs may coexist.

This creates realistic conflict.

## 7.27.45 Courtship and Trust

Trust can make relationship formation easier by reducing uncertainty.

But romantic relationships can begin with incomplete Trust.

Trust continues developing afterward.

## 7.27.46 Courtship and Affection

Affection may grow through dating.

Strong Affection can also exist before romance through friendship.

Romantic formation should not erase the prior relationship history.

## 7.27.47 Courtship and Sexual Chemistry

For adults, sexual chemistry may contribute to relationship formation.

But high sexual chemistry cannot resolve:
- values conflict;
- structure mismatch;
- lack of commitment.

## 7.27.48 Courtship and Adult Intimacy

For adults, intimacy may occur:
- before relationship formation;
- during courtship;
- after commitment;
- never.

Relationship status does not automatically authorize intimacy.

Ruleset 7.28 governs established intimacy and 7.29+ consent/boundary interactions where applicable.

## 7.27.49 Relationship Formation After Friendship

Friends may transition into romance.

This can alter:
- expectations;
- vulnerability;
- social networks;
- fear of losing friendship.

Their previous friendship remains part of relationship history.

## 7.27.50 Relationship Formation After Rivalry

Rivals may develop romance through:
- respect;
- chemistry;
- shared history;
- changing perception.

Rivalry may remain part of the relationship.

## 7.27.51 Cross-Group Relationships

Romance across:
- clans;
- villages;
- ranks;
- factions;

may create:
- political pressure;
- secrecy;
- loyalty conflict;
- reputation effects.

The relationship itself still depends on mutual choice.

## 7.27.52 NPC-Initiated Courtship

NPCs should independently:
- ask others on dates;
- define relationships;
- seek exclusivity;
- end courtship.

This should emerge from their goals and emotional state.

## 7.27.53 NPC-to-NPC Relationship Formation

NPCs may form relationships off-screen.

Important relationships should be simulated when they affect:
- social networks;
- plot;
- living arrangements;
- politics;
- player relationships.

## 7.27.54 Player Agency

The player controls whether their character:
- accepts dates;
- defines a relationship;
- agrees to exclusivity;
- commits.

The engine may present:
- attraction;
- emotions;
- NPC offers;
- contextual pressure.

Mundane social mechanics should not force relationship commitment.

## 7.27.55 Hidden NPC Intent

The player may not initially know whether an NPC wants:
- casual dating;
- serious commitment;
- validation;
- adult sexual interaction only;
- eventual marriage.

These intentions may emerge through communication and behavior.

## 7.27.56 Courtship Memory

Important milestones may become memories:
- first date;
- first confession;
- first public appearance;
- becoming exclusive;
- defining the relationship.

These may later influence attachment and nostalgia.

## 7.27.57 No Relationship XP Bar

Relationship formation should never be:
- complete enough dates;
- reach enough Romance points;
- give enough gifts.

Progress occurs through actual:
- mutual interest;
- compatibility;
- trust;
- communication;
- decisions.

## 7.27.58 Design Standard

A courtship mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- treats mutual attraction as automatic relationship formation;
- assumes every date advances romance;
- makes gifts purchase affection;
- assumes exclusivity without shared expectation;
- uses one numerical threshold for relationship formation;
- forces relationships through successful social rolls;
- assumes official relationships are automatically stable;
- treats intimacy as proof of commitment;
- makes family approval override individual choice;
- turns NPCs into passive romance targets waiting for the player.

## Governing Rule

**Courtship should function as a mutual discovery and negotiation process in which attraction opens the possibility of romance, compatibility and experience shape whether it works, and a relationship exists only when both people meaningfully recognize and participate in it.**
