# 7.12 — Social Context & Situational Modifiers

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.12 defines the situational factors that shape a social interaction before Ruleset 2 resolves uncertainty.

The governing principle is:

**Social actions should be evaluated in context. The same words, request, threat, confession, apology, or offer may produce very different outcomes depending on setting, audience, authority, timing, evidence, stakes, and power.**

## 7.12.1 Context Is an Input, Not a Separate Roll

Social context should not create a second resolution engine.

Ruleset 7 determines:
- what contextual factors exist;
- how they help or hinder;
- which relationship dimensions matter;
- whether the action is plausible.

Ruleset 2 determines the actual uncertain outcome where a roll is needed.

## 7.12.2 Privacy

An interaction may be:
- fully private;
- semi-private;
- publicly observable;
- formally witnessed;
- secretly monitored.

Privacy can affect:
- honesty;
- embarrassment;
- willingness to confess;
- willingness to flirt;
- willingness to apologize;
- willingness to reveal secrets;
- fear of reputational consequences.

A character may say something privately they would never say in front of their team or clan.

## 7.12.3 Audience

Observers matter because the NPC may care about:
- reputation;
- authority;
- embarrassment;
- loyalty;
- cultural expectations;
- appearing weak;
- appearing competent.

Relevant audiences may include:
- teammates;
- subordinates;
- superiors;
- family;
- clan members;
- civilians;
- romantic partners;
- political rivals.

The same request may be easier privately and harder publicly.

## 7.12.4 Audience Alignment

The audience may:
- support the speaker;
- support the target;
- be neutral;
- be hostile to both.

This can affect pressure and willingness.

Example:
A chunin may be more willing to challenge a commander privately than in front of the entire squad.

## 7.12.5 Rank & Formal Hierarchy

Rank and office may create:
- authority;
- deference;
- fear of punishment;
- expectation of obedience;
- legitimacy.

Relevant structures include:
- academy hierarchy;
- genin/chunin/jonin command;
- ANBU command;
- clan hierarchy;
- medical authority;
- village administration.

Formal authority modifies decisions.
It does not erase agency.

## 7.12.6 Legitimate vs Illegitimate Authority

Authority should be judged by whether the target sees it as legitimate.

An order from:
- a recognized commander;
- a clan elder;
- a parent;
- an occupying enemy;
- a criminal boss;

may carry very different weight.

Perceived legitimacy matters more than title alone.

## 7.12.7 Status

Social status may come from:
- rank;
- clan;
- wealth;
- fame;
- age;
- office;
- reputation;
- expertise;
- family.

Status can influence:
- who gets heard;
- who is deferred to;
- who is expected to speak first;
- who can make certain requests without offense.

Status does not guarantee agreement.

## 7.12.8 Age & Seniority

Age and seniority may matter differently across cultures and institutions.

They may affect:
- expected respect;
- mentoring norms;
- family authority;
- perceived credibility.

Age should not replace actual competence.

## 7.12.9 Clan Context

Clan culture may influence:
- etiquette;
- courtship;
- loyalty expectations;
- secrecy;
- inheritance;
- hierarchy;
- acceptable conflict.

An action acceptable in one clan may be offensive in another.

## 7.12.10 Village & Cultural Norms

Each village or region may have different norms around:
- directness;
- hierarchy;
- family;
- honor;
- public emotion;
- courtship;
- hospitality;
- negotiation;
- apology;
- conflict.

Culture should shape interpretation without making every member identical.

## 7.12.11 Professional Context

Social behavior may differ in:
- mission briefings;
- hospitals;
- shops;
- classrooms;
- military meetings;
- clan councils;
- criminal networks.

Professional roles create expectations.

Example:
A medic may reject flirtation during emergency treatment even if attraction exists.

## 7.12.12 Timing

Timing affects receptiveness.

Examples:
- asking for a favor during crisis;
- confessing feelings before a dangerous mission;
- negotiating immediately after victory;
- apologizing while the target is still furious.

Good timing can reduce resistance.
Bad timing can increase it.

## 7.12.13 Urgency

Urgency changes:
- patience;
- verification;
- willingness to negotiate;
- willingness to accept risk.

High urgency may simplify decisions but increase mistakes.

## 7.12.14 Emotional Context

Current emotional state modifies the interaction.

Relevant states may include:
- anger;
- fear;
- grief;
- embarrassment;
- confidence;
- jealousy;
- relief;
- adult arousal.

Emotion should influence willingness and interpretation without dictating outcome.

## 7.12.15 Physical & Medical Context

Ruleset 4 may supply:
- pain;
- exhaustion;
- injury;
- illness;
- intoxication.

These may affect:
- patience;
- concentration;
- vulnerability;
- fear;
- urgency.

Ruleset 7 consumes the condition rather than redefining it.

## 7.12.16 Location

Location can alter behavior.

Examples:
- private home;
- clan compound;
- battlefield;
- hospital;
- prison;
- public market;
- office;
- training ground.

Locations may carry:
- safety;
- symbolism;
- authority;
- surveillance;
- social expectations.

## 7.12.17 Territory & Home Advantage

People may behave differently in spaces they control.

Home territory may increase:
- confidence;
- support;
- access to allies;
- perceived authority.

Foreign or hostile territory may increase caution.

## 7.12.18 Evidence

Evidence can dramatically change persuasion or accusation.

Evidence may be:
- direct;
- circumstantial;
- testimonial;
- documentary;
- physical;
- sensory;
- forged.

Evidence affects:
- plausibility;
- confidence;
- burden of proof;
- willingness to reconsider.

Ruleset 7.6 governs knowledge quality.

## 7.12.19 Leverage

Leverage is something that changes the target's incentives.

Examples:
- information;
- money;
- favors;
- protection;
- access;
- political support;
- threat of exposure;
- bargaining advantage.

Leverage increases influence because it changes the decision environment.

It does not create mind control.

## 7.12.20 Positive vs Negative Leverage

### Positive Leverage
Offers benefit.

Examples:
- payment;
- promotion;
- assistance;
- protection;
- introduction.

### Negative Leverage
Threatens cost.

Examples:
- exposure;
- punishment;
- loss of status;
- credible harm.

Negative leverage may also increase:
- Fear;
- Resentment;
- Hostility.

## 7.12.21 Credibility of Leverage

Leverage only matters if the target believes it.

A threat from someone incapable of carrying it out has little weight.

A promise from a known liar may be discounted.

## 7.12.22 Stakes

High-stakes requests are harder.

Examples:
- trivial favor;
- money;
- reputation;
- career;
- clan loyalty;
- life;
- betrayal of village.

The more costly the request, the more resistance should matter.

## 7.12.23 Cost to Target

A request should consider actual and perceived cost.

Costs may include:
- time;
- money;
- danger;
- embarrassment;
- reputation;
- relationships;
- moral compromise;
- legal risk.

A small request should not require the same persuasion burden as a life-changing sacrifice.

## 7.12.24 Benefit to Target

Requests are easier when they serve the NPC's own goals.

If the requested action already benefits the target, persuasion may be unnecessary.

## 7.12.25 Shared Goals

Common goals reduce resistance.

Examples:
- mutual enemy;
- shared mission;
- family safety;
- political objective.

Shared goals can create cooperation even without affection.

## 7.12.26 Conflicting Goals

If the request conflicts with a high-priority goal, difficulty increases.

Ruleset 7.4 supplies goal priorities.

## 7.12.27 Relationship Context

Relevant dimensions may modify willingness:
- Trust;
- Respect;
- Affection;
- Loyalty;
- Fear;
- Obligation;
- Resentment;
- Hostility;
- Romantic Interest;
- adult sexual chemistry where relevant.

No single dimension should control the interaction.

## 7.12.28 History

Prior interactions affect context.

Examples:
- previous lie;
- kept promise;
- prior rejection;
- repeated support;
- earlier coercion.

The same words carry different weight depending on history.

## 7.12.29 Reputation Context

The target may know the speaker only by reputation.

Relevant reputation may include:
- honesty;
- competence;
- cruelty;
- reliability;
- danger;
- status.

Reputation affects initial interpretation, not truth.

## 7.12.30 Witness Risk

The possibility of being seen may change behavior.

Examples:
- accepting a bribe;
- revealing a secret;
- flirting;
- breaking rank;
- apologizing publicly.

Perceived witness risk matters even if no one is actually watching.

## 7.12.31 Secrecy Risk

Some actions become harder if discovery would be costly.

Examples:
- forbidden romance;
- leaking intelligence;
- betrayal;
- secret negotiation.

The target may require:
- privacy;
- assurance;
- safeguards;
- deniability.

## 7.12.32 Power Imbalance

Power imbalance may arise from:
- rank;
- physical strength;
- wealth;
- employment;
- command;
- captivity;
- medical dependence;
- social status;
- age;
- institutional control.

Power imbalance changes the meaning of social behavior.

## 7.12.33 Power Does Not Equal Genuine Agreement

A subordinate may comply because:
- fear;
- duty;
- economic dependence;
- lack of alternatives.

That is different from:
- persuasion;
- trust;
- enthusiasm;
- genuine consent.

The simulation should distinguish compliance from willingness.

## 7.12.34 Captivity & Coercive Context

Captivity, restraint, interrogation, or threat may reduce meaningful freedom.

Social outcomes in these contexts should distinguish:
- voluntary cooperation;
- pressured compliance;
- coerced behavior;
- false confession;
- strategic deception.

## 7.12.35 Professional & Authority Boundaries

Some relationships have built-in constraints.

Examples:
- commander/subordinate;
- teacher/student;
- medic/patient;
- jailer/prisoner;
- employer/employee.

These contexts can affect:
- disclosure;
- romance;
- consent;
- retaliation;
- reputation;
- institutional consequences.

## 7.12.36 Romantic Context

Romantic interactions depend on:
- privacy;
- existing relationship;
- prior signals;
- timing;
- culture;
- current emotion;
- boundaries;
- power imbalance.

A romantic confession may be welcomed privately but humiliating publicly.

## 7.12.37 Adult Sexual Context

For adults, sexual interactions may consider:
- privacy;
- relationship expectations;
- attraction;
- adult sexual chemistry;
- current arousal;
- boundaries;
- safety;
- power imbalance;
- explicit willingness.

High chemistry may improve receptiveness but never substitutes for consent.

## 7.12.38 Public Pressure & Consent

Public pressure should not be treated as genuine willingness.

Examples:
- public proposal;
- public romantic confession;
- group pressure;
- superior pressuring subordinate.

The target may comply socially while privately unwilling.

The system should preserve the distinction.

## 7.12.39 Gifts

Gift meaning depends on:
- culture;
- relationship;
- timing;
- value;
- symbolism;
- expectation of repayment.

A gift may be:
- generous;
- romantic;
- manipulative;
- insulting;
- inappropriate.

## 7.12.40 Bribes

A bribe is not just a gift.

It usually attempts to exchange benefit for behavior that may violate:
- duty;
- policy;
- law;
- expectation.

Bribery context should consider:
- amount;
- risk;
- target need;
- moral boundaries;
- detection risk.

## 7.12.41 Threats

A threat should be evaluated by:
- capability;
- intent;
- immediacy;
- target vulnerability;
- alternatives;
- audience;
- consequences.

An unbelievable threat should have little effect.

## 7.12.42 Promises

Promises gain weight from:
- credibility;
- history;
- specificity;
- ability to fulfill;
- enforcement.

A promise from someone trustworthy matters more.

## 7.12.43 Social Momentum

The immediate conversation may develop momentum.

Examples:
- repeated agreement;
- escalating hostility;
- growing openness;
- increasing embarrassment.

Momentum should modify current interaction but decay afterward.

## 7.12.44 Interruptions

Interruptions may change:
- privacy;
- emotional tone;
- willingness;
- topic;
- audience.

A confession interrupted by teammates may not resume automatically.

## 7.12.45 Context Shifts

If context changes substantially, recalculate the interaction.

Examples:
- private -> public;
- calm -> crisis;
- friendly -> threatening;
- evidence revealed;
- superior enters room.

Do not continue using stale modifiers.

## 7.12.46 Context Stacking

Multiple favorable or unfavorable factors may combine.

However, stacking should not become unlimited arithmetic.

The simulation should identify:
- dominant factors;
- reinforcing factors;
- conflicting factors.

Avoid dozens of tiny modifiers.

## 7.12.47 Dominant Context

Some factors may overwhelm others.

Examples:
- immediate mortal danger;
- severe coercion;
- overwhelming evidence;
- catastrophic betrayal.

Dominant context should be rare but meaningful.

## 7.12.48 No Automatic Roll

Context may make an outcome obvious.

Examples:
- trusted friend asks for trivial favor;
- subordinate receives routine lawful order;
- hostile prisoner is asked to betray village secrets with no leverage.

Ruleset 2 need not roll when uncertainty is negligible.

## 7.12.49 Player-Facing Context

Players should be able to perceive much of the external context:
- audience;
- location;
- rank;
- known evidence;
- obvious stakes.

Hidden context may include:
- secret leverage;
- unknown observers;
- hidden relationship;
- concealed authority.

## 7.12.50 Context Snapshot

For a meaningful interaction, the engine may internally summarize:

**Setting:** Private office  
**Audience:** None known  
**Authority:** Speaker outranks target  
**Relationship:** High Respect, moderate Trust  
**Target Emotion:** Irritated  
**Request Cost:** High  
**Shared Goal:** Moderate  
**Evidence:** Strong  
**Leverage:** Low  
**Time Pressure:** High

This snapshot can feed Ruleset 2 without exposing exact hidden state.

## 7.12.51 Design Standard

A context mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- ignores audience;
- treats rank as mind control;
- treats status as automatic credibility;
- ignores culture;
- treats leverage as guaranteed success;
- ignores power imbalance;
- treats coerced compliance as genuine agreement;
- ignores timing;
- uses dozens of trivial numeric modifiers;
- fails to update when the context changes;
- makes sexual chemistry substitute for adult consent;
- requires a roll when outcome is already obvious.

## Governing Rule

**Before resolving a social action, evaluate the circumstances surrounding it, because social meaning depends not only on what is said or done, but on who is present, what is at stake, who holds power, what evidence exists, and what the target stands to gain or lose.**
