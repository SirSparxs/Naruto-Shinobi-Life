# 7.34 — Leadership, Loyalty & Followership

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.34 defines leadership, followership, personal loyalty, legitimacy, dissent, obedience, informal authority, command confidence, desertion, and breakdown of social command.

The governing principle is:

**People follow leaders for different reasons, and formal authority, personal loyalty, fear, respect, trust, and legitimacy should remain distinct sources of influence.**

## 7.34.1 Scope

This section governs:
- leadership relationships;
- informal leadership;
- personal loyalty;
- followership;
- command confidence;
- legitimacy;
- dissent;
- refusal;
- desertion;
- mutiny-like social breakdown;
- loyalty conflicts.

Formal military rank, legal authority, promotion, punishment, and institutional chain of command belong to future organizational or military systems.

Ruleset 7 governs how people socially respond to those structures.

## 7.34.2 Formal Authority vs Social Authority

Formal authority comes from:
- rank;
- office;
- assignment;
- law;
- institutional structure.

Social authority comes from:
- Trust;
- Respect;
- competence;
- confidence;
- shared history;
- reputation;
- perceived legitimacy.

A leader may possess one without the other.

## 7.34.3 Formal Rank Does Not Guarantee Followership

A formally appointed leader may face:
- reluctant compliance;
- quiet resistance;
- low Trust;
- low morale;
- covert disobedience.

Likewise, someone with no official rank may become the person others actually look to.

## 7.34.4 Informal Leadership

Informal leaders emerge when others consistently:
- seek their advice;
- follow their judgment;
- rely on them in uncertainty;
- trust their decisions;
- defer socially.

This may arise from:
- expertise;
- courage;
- emotional steadiness;
- charisma-like social presence;
- seniority;
- reliability.

No Charisma attribute is required.

## 7.34.5 Leadership Without a Charisma Stat

Leadership effectiveness should emerge from:
- Leadership skill;
- Willpower;
- Intelligence;
- Perception;
- communication;
- reputation;
- relationship history;
- competence;
- context.

Different leaders may succeed through different styles.

## 7.34.6 Leadership Styles

Possible leadership styles include:
- directive;
- collaborative;
- mentoring;
- inspirational;
- procedural;
- protective;
- strategic;
- authoritarian.

Style should affect:
- who responds well;
- what situations favor it;
- what risks it creates.

No one style should be universally superior.

## 7.34.7 Legitimacy

Legitimacy answers:

**Do followers believe this person has a rightful or acceptable basis to lead?**

Sources may include:
- appointment;
- tradition;
- demonstrated competence;
- earned trust;
- election or selection;
- clan norms;
- battlefield necessity.

Legitimacy is socially perceived, not merely formal.

## 7.34.8 Competence Confidence

Followers may evaluate whether a leader:
- understands the situation;
- makes sound decisions;
- protects the group;
- learns from mistakes.

Competence confidence is distinct from affection.

A leader may be disliked but trusted professionally.

## 7.34.9 Moral Confidence

Followers may ask:
- "Will this leader use us fairly?"
- "Will they sacrifice us needlessly?"
- "Do they respect our values?"

A highly competent leader may still lose followership through perceived moral illegitimacy.

## 7.34.10 Personal Loyalty

Personal Loyalty is attachment to the leader as a person.

It may arise from:
- shared history;
- rescue;
- mentorship;
- admiration;
- friendship;
- gratitude;
- family ties.

Personal loyalty can persist after formal authority ends.

## 7.34.11 Institutional Loyalty

A character may be loyal to:
- village;
- clan;
- squad;
- office;
- mission;
- doctrine.

This can conflict with loyalty to the current leader.

## 7.34.12 Loyalty Is Directional

A may be deeply loyal to B.

B may view A as merely a subordinate.

The simulation should preserve asymmetry.

## 7.34.13 Loyalty Does Not Equal Obedience

A loyal follower may:
- disagree;
- criticize;
- refuse a harmful order;
- challenge the leader.

Blind obedience is not required for strong Loyalty.

## 7.34.14 Obedience Sources

A character may obey because of:
- legitimacy;
- duty;
- Trust;
- Loyalty;
- fear;
- habit;
- professional discipline;
- lack of alternatives.

The reason should matter.

## 7.34.15 Fear-Based Obedience

Fear may create:
- rapid compliance;
- silence;
- concealment;
- avoidance.

It may also create:
- Resentment;
- sabotage;
- desertion;
- revenge;
- collapse when the threat disappears.

Fear is not the same as loyalty.

## 7.34.16 Respect-Based Followership

Respect-based followers may cooperate because they believe:
- leader is capable;
- leader earned authority;
- decisions are usually sound.

This tends to be more stable than pure fear.

## 7.34.17 Trust-Based Followership

Trust-based followers may accept uncertainty because they believe the leader:
- acts in good faith;
- protects the group;
- communicates honestly.

A serious betrayal can sharply damage this form of followership.

## 7.34.18 Inspirational Followership

Some leaders create strong motivation through:
- vision;
- identity;
- hope;
- symbolic leadership.

This may increase willingness to accept hardship.

It should not override self-preservation or core values automatically.

## 7.34.19 Transactional Followership

Some followers cooperate because of:
- pay;
- protection;
- status;
- opportunity;
- access.

If the benefit disappears, loyalty may be limited.

## 7.34.20 Habitual Followership

Long-standing teams may obey quickly because:
- roles are familiar;
- routines are established;
- leader has a proven track record.

Habit can reduce decision friction without eliminating agency.

## 7.34.21 Command Confidence

Command Confidence is a contextual group belief that leadership can successfully guide the current situation.

It may rise through:
- successful decisions;
- clear communication;
- competence;
- calm under pressure.

It may fall through:
- indecision;
- contradiction;
- avoidable losses;
- deception;
- repeated failure.

## 7.34.22 Morale vs Command Confidence

Morale:
- how emotionally ready and hopeful the group feels.

Command Confidence:
- how much the group believes leadership can guide them.

A demoralized group may still trust its commander.
A confident group may lose faith in leadership.

## 7.34.23 Crisis Leadership

Under crisis, followers may value:
- decisiveness;
- clarity;
- competence;
- emotional steadiness.

A style effective in crisis may be poor for long-term normal leadership.

## 7.34.24 Leadership Under Uncertainty

A leader does not need perfect information.

Followers may judge:
- whether uncertainty was acknowledged;
- whether reasoning was sound;
- whether adaptation occurred.

Pretending certainty and being exposed can damage Trust.

## 7.34.25 Mistakes

Leaders can make mistakes without losing legitimacy.

Impact depends on:
- severity;
- frequency;
- honesty;
- accountability;
- whether lessons are learned.

## 7.34.26 Accountability

Admitting mistakes may increase Respect for some followers.

Blaming subordinates for obvious leadership failure may increase Resentment.

## 7.34.27 Favoritism

Perceived favoritism may damage:
- fairness;
- legitimacy;
- cohesion;
- Trust.

Actual favoritism and merely perceived favoritism should remain separate.

## 7.34.28 Consistency

Followers often prefer predictable standards.

Inconsistent:
- discipline;
- rewards;
- expectations;
- risk tolerance;

may reduce confidence and legitimacy.

## 7.34.29 Fairness

Fair treatment can strengthen:
- Respect;
- institutional Trust;
- willingness to accept unpleasant decisions.

Fairness does not require identical treatment when circumstances differ.

## 7.34.30 Protectiveness

Leaders may gain Loyalty by protecting subordinates.

But overprotection can:
- suppress growth;
- undermine autonomy;
- create resentment.

## 7.34.31 Sacrifice by Leader

A leader who accepts personal risk or sacrifice may gain:
- Respect;
- Loyalty;
- symbolic importance.

This should depend on whether the act appears:
- necessary;
- sincere;
- competent.

Reckless self-sacrifice may instead reduce confidence.

## 7.34.32 Sacrificing Followers

Orders that place followers at risk may be accepted when:
- mission stakes justify it;
- leader shares risk;
- decision is credible;
- alternatives are poor.

Repeated seemingly needless sacrifice can destroy legitimacy.

## 7.34.33 Dissent

Dissent is disagreement with:
- order;
- policy;
- judgment;
- leader.

Dissent is not automatically disloyalty.

## 7.34.34 Constructive Dissent

Healthy groups may allow:
- objections;
- alternative plans;
- warnings;
- criticism.

Leaders who tolerate competent dissent may gain better information and stronger long-term Trust.

## 7.34.35 Suppressed Dissent

Fear or authoritarian culture may suppress disagreement.

This can create:
- apparent unity;
- hidden opposition;
- poorer decisions;
- sudden later breakdown.

Silence should not be interpreted automatically as agreement.

## 7.34.36 Public Challenge

Challenging leadership publicly may affect:
- authority;
- face;
- group cohesion;
- reputation.

The consequences depend on culture and urgency.

## 7.34.37 Private Challenge

A trusted subordinate may challenge privately while preserving public unity.

This may strengthen the relationship when handled well.

## 7.34.38 Refusal

A follower may refuse an order because of:
- moral boundary;
- illegitimacy;
- unacceptable risk;
- conflicting loyalty;
- disbelief in authority;
- personal interest.

Ruleset 7.18 governs command resolution.
This section governs the leadership relationship consequences.

## 7.34.39 Refusal Does Not Automatically End Loyalty

A subordinate may refuse one order while remaining deeply loyal overall.

The leader's reaction may determine whether the conflict escalates.

## 7.34.40 Conflicting Loyalties

A follower may be pulled between:
- leader;
- village;
- clan;
- family;
- squad;
- personal ethics.

No universal priority applies.

## 7.34.41 Loyalty Crisis

A loyalty crisis occurs when important commitments demand incompatible actions.

Possible outcomes:
- obedience;
- refusal;
- neutrality;
- defection;
- attempted compromise;
- self-sacrifice.

## 7.34.42 Defection

Defection is leaving one side or allegiance for another.

Motives may include:
- ideological change;
- betrayal;
- survival;
- family;
- reward;
- coercion.

Defection does not automatically mean previous loyalty was fake.

## 7.34.43 Desertion

Desertion is abandonment of expected duty or group participation.

Possible causes:
- fear;
- exhaustion;
- low legitimacy;
- hopelessness;
- moral opposition;
- self-preservation.

Formal legal consequences belong elsewhere.

## 7.34.44 Mutiny-Like Breakdown

A command structure may socially collapse when enough members lose willingness to obey.

Possible precursors:
- severe legitimacy loss;
- repeated disastrous orders;
- shared Resentment;
- alternative leader;
- communication among dissenters.

This should be emergent, not triggered by one hidden meter.

## 7.34.45 Alternative Leader

During leadership failure, followers may begin looking to:
- respected veteran;
- informal leader;
- specialist;
- dissenter.

This can create dual authority.

## 7.34.46 Split Followership

Different members may support different leaders.

The group may fragment into:
- factions;
- temporary camps;
- competing chains of influence.

Ruleset 7.23 and 7.22 govern group and network effects.

## 7.34.47 Succession

When a leader:
- dies;
- resigns;
- disappears;
- is removed;

followers may transfer loyalty unevenly.

Formal succession does not guarantee social legitimacy.

## 7.34.48 Legacy Loyalty

Followers may remain loyal to:
- deceased leader;
- former commander;
- old regime.

This may complicate acceptance of new authority.

## 7.34.49 Mentor-Leader Overlap

A leader may also be:
- mentor;
- parent-like figure;
- friend.

These overlapping roles can strengthen Loyalty while making betrayal more damaging.

## 7.34.50 Charismatic Presence Without Charisma Attribute

Some characters may naturally command attention through combinations of:
- Willpower;
- confidence;
- appearance;
- speaking skill;
- reputation;
- emotional intensity.

This should be treated as emergent social presence, not a universal Charisma stat.

## 7.34.51 Leadership Skill

Leadership skill improves:
- clarity;
- coordination;
- morale support;
- delegation;
- conflict management;
- motivating communication.

It cannot create Loyalty from nothing or make obviously illegitimate orders universally accepted.

## 7.34.52 Delegation

Effective leaders may delegate based on:
- competence;
- Trust;
- role.

Good delegation can improve:
- efficiency;
- subordinate development;
- ownership.

Poor delegation may produce overload or confusion.

## 7.34.53 Micromanagement

Excessive control may reduce:
- autonomy;
- morale;
- initiative;
- Trust.

Some high-risk situations may temporarily justify tighter control.

## 7.34.54 Empowerment

Giving followers meaningful responsibility may increase:
- competence;
- investment;
- confidence;
- Loyalty.

This depends on whether responsibility feels earned and supported.

## 7.34.55 Recognition

Public or private recognition may strengthen:
- Respect;
- Affection;
- motivation;
- belonging.

Insincere or unfair recognition may have little effect or backfire.

## 7.34.56 Discipline

Social discipline may affect:
- Respect;
- Fear;
- legitimacy.

Formal punishment mechanics belong elsewhere.

Fair, proportionate discipline may preserve legitimacy.
Arbitrary punishment may destroy it.

## 7.34.57 Loyalty to Bad Leaders

A follower may remain loyal despite:
- incompetence;
- cruelty;
- corruption.

Possible reasons:
- history;
- fear;
- identity;
- dependency;
- ideology;
- personal debt.

The simulation should not assume people instantly abandon leaders who behave badly.

## 7.34.58 Disillusionment

Loyal followers may gradually lose faith through:
- repeated contradiction;
- betrayal;
- hypocrisy;
- incompetence;
- abuse.

Disillusionment should often be a process, not one switch.

## 7.34.59 Rebuilding Leadership Trust

A damaged leader may repair standing through:
- accountability;
- changed behavior;
- successful decisions;
- fairness;
- transparency;
- time.

Some followers may never return.

## 7.34.60 Leadership Reputation

Leaders may develop domain reputations such as:
- inspiring;
- ruthless;
- reliable;
- reckless;
- fair;
- cowardly;
- brilliant tactician.

Ruleset 7.20 owns the reputation itself.

## 7.34.61 Followership Profiles

Different NPCs may prefer different leaders.

Examples:
- one values decisiveness;
- one values fairness;
- one values competence;
- one values warmth;
- one values ideological alignment.

There is no universally ideal leader.

## 7.34.62 Player Leadership

If the player holds authority, NPCs should independently:
- trust;
- doubt;
- challenge;
- obey;
- disobey;
- defect;

based on actual history and circumstances.

Player status should not guarantee Loyalty.

## 7.34.63 NPC Leadership

NPC leaders should influence:
- the player;
- other NPCs;
- groups;

through the same rules.

## 7.34.64 Hidden Leadership State

Exact:
- Loyalty;
- confidence;
- legitimacy;

should usually remain hidden.

The player infers them through:
- behavior;
- dissent;
- initiative;
- obedience;
- morale;
- conversations.

## 7.34.65 Leadership Audit

For an important leader, the engine should be able to answer:

1. Why do followers obey?
2. Do they trust the leader personally?
3. Do they believe the leader is competent?
4. Do they view the authority as legitimate?
5. Who holds informal influence?
6. What current dissent exists?
7. Where are loyalties divided?
8. What could realistically cause defection or command breakdown?

## 7.34.66 Design Standard

A leadership mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- equates rank with loyalty;
- equates obedience with loyalty;
- treats fear as respect;
- assumes silence means agreement;
- makes Leadership skill mind control;
- creates one universal leadership style;
- ignores informal leaders;
- makes defection require previous fake loyalty;
- assumes dissent is betrayal;
- gives the player automatic command authority over NPC choices.

## Governing Rule

**Leadership should emerge from a combination of formal authority, legitimacy, competence, trust, loyalty, fear, and shared purpose, with followers retaining independent judgment and the possibility of dissent, refusal, defection, or continued loyalty under difficult conditions.**
