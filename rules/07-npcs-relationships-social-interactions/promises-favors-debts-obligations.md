# 7.32 — Promises, Favors, Debts & Obligations

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.32 defines promises, favors, debts of gratitude, reciprocal obligations, social commitments, repayment expectations, and the consequences of honoring or violating one's word.

The governing principle is:

**Social obligations should arise from actual commitments, accepted benefits, shared norms, and relationship history rather than functioning as a universal currency that can compel behavior.**

## 7.32.1 Scope

This section governs:
- promises;
- vows and informal commitments;
- favors;
- debts of gratitude;
- reciprocal expectations;
- repayment;
- calling in favors;
- refusing obligations;
- broken promises;
- social consequences of unreliability;
- obligations created by rescue, sacrifice, mentorship, or patronage.

Formal contracts, legal debt, wages, taxation, and enforceable institutional obligations belong to future legal/economic systems.

## 7.32.2 Promise Defined

A promise is a communicated commitment that another person may reasonably rely on.

Examples:
- "I'll keep this secret."
- "I'll come back."
- "I'll help you."
- "I won't tell anyone."
- "I'll protect your family."

A promise should create stronger expectations than a casual statement of intent.

## 7.32.3 Intention Is Not Always a Promise

Statements such as:
- "I'll try.";
- "I probably can.";
- "I want to help.";

should not automatically be treated as promises.

The engine should preserve:
- what was actually said;
- how strongly it was phrased;
- whether the listener reasonably interpreted it as a commitment.

## 7.32.4 Explicit vs Implicit Commitments

Commitments may be:
- explicit;
- culturally implied;
- role-based;
- relationship-based.

Examples:
- squadmates may expect mutual rescue;
- a mentor may be expected to protect a student;
- a clan heir may be expected to represent the family.

Implicit obligations should exist only when socially plausible.

## 7.32.5 Promise Strength

Promises may vary in seriousness.

Possible levels:
- casual assurance;
- meaningful commitment;
- serious promise;
- solemn oath.

Strength depends on:
- wording;
- context;
- witnesses;
- personal values;
- cultural importance;
- stakes.

## 7.32.6 Witnessed Promises

A promise made publicly may carry additional consequences because:
- more people know;
- reputation is at stake;
- social pressure increases.

Private promises may carry equal emotional weight while having fewer public consequences.

## 7.32.7 Promise Memory

Meaningful promises should be remembered explicitly.

Important record fields may include:
- promisor;
- recipient;
- content;
- time;
- witnesses;
- seriousness;
- status;
- conditions.

Ruleset 7.7 owns memory persistence.

## 7.32.8 Conditional Promises

Promises may depend on conditions.

Example:
- "If you get me safely out of here, I'll help your family."

The obligation activates only when the agreed condition occurs.

## 7.32.9 Ambiguous Conditions

If terms are unclear, participants may later disagree about whether the promise was fulfilled.

This can create:
- resentment;
- conflict;
- negotiation;
- accusations of bad faith.

## 7.32.10 Impossible Promises

A character may promise something they later cannot do.

The social consequence depends on:
- whether impossibility was foreseeable;
- whether they tried;
- whether they communicated honestly;
- whether they accepted responsibility.

Failure and betrayal should not be treated as identical.

## 7.32.11 Good-Faith Failure

Someone may sincerely try and still fail.

Possible consequences:
- disappointment;
- reduced confidence;
- little or no Trust loss;
- sympathy.

Context determines interpretation.

## 7.32.12 Negligent Failure

If failure results from carelessness or avoidable inaction, consequences may include:
- Trust loss;
- Resentment;
- reputation damage;
- reduced future reliance.

## 7.32.13 Deliberate Promise-Breaking

Knowingly violating a promise may create:
- major Trust loss;
- betrayal memory;
- Resentment;
- reputation damage;
- relationship rupture.

Severity depends on:
- importance;
- harm caused;
- motive;
- history;
- whether the promise concerned a vulnerability or secret.

## 7.32.14 Conflicting Promises

A character may become unable to honor all commitments simultaneously.

Examples:
- promise to protect a friend;
- duty to village;
- promise to keep a secret;
- order to disclose information.

Ruleset 7.5 should resolve the decision.

Breaking one commitment may still have consequences even if another obligation was stronger.

## 7.32.15 Promise Priority Is Not Automatic

There should be no universal hierarchy such as:
- oath > family > friendship.

Priority depends on:
- character values;
- cultural norms;
- relationship strength;
- stakes;
- wording;
- consequences.

## 7.32.16 Oaths

Oaths are especially serious promises tied to:
- identity;
- honor;
- religion;
- clan;
- profession;
- personal code.

Breaking an oath may create stronger:
- guilt;
- shame;
- reputation effects;
- self-concept conflict.

Formal supernatural oaths, if any, belong to relevant chakra/jutsu systems.

## 7.32.17 Favor Defined

A favor is help provided with some expectation of goodwill or future reciprocity.

A favor may be:
- freely given;
- implicitly reciprocal;
- explicitly exchanged;
- strategically offered.

## 7.32.18 Not Every Kindness Creates Debt

Ordinary kindness should not automatically generate an obligation.

Examples:
- buying lunch;
- offering directions;
- helping with a minor task.

Whether an obligation forms depends on:
- magnitude;
- context;
- expectation;
- relationship;
- culture.

## 7.32.19 Accepted Favor

A favor is more likely to create obligation when the recipient knowingly accepts meaningful help.

The recipient may still reject the implied debt if no reciprocal expectation was agreed.

## 7.32.20 Unsolicited Favor

Someone cannot automatically create enforceable social debt by doing something unwanted and later demanding repayment.

Example:
- "I did this for you, so now you owe me."

The recipient's interpretation and prior understanding matter.

## 7.32.21 Debt of Gratitude

A debt of gratitude is a social or emotional sense of owing someone because they provided significant help, protection, sacrifice, or opportunity.

It may arise from:
- rescue;
- shelter;
- mentorship;
- major sacrifice;
- career opportunity;
- protecting family;
- life-saving aid.

## 7.32.22 Gratitude vs Obligation

Gratitude is emotional appreciation.

Obligation is perceived duty to repay or reciprocate.

A character may feel:
- grateful but not obligated;
- obligated without affection;
- both;
- neither.

## 7.32.23 Magnitude

Obligation magnitude should depend on:
- cost to benefactor;
- benefit to recipient;
- risk;
- rarity;
- intent;
- cultural expectations.

Saving someone's life may create more obligation than lending them money once.

## 7.32.24 Sacrifice

A favor involving genuine sacrifice may create especially strong:
- gratitude;
- Loyalty;
- obligation.

But sacrifice does not create ownership over the recipient.

## 7.32.25 Favors and Relationship Dimensions

Favors may influence:
- Trust;
- Affection;
- Loyalty;
- Obligation;
- Respect.

Which dimensions change depends on motive and interpretation.

## 7.32.26 Hidden Motives

A favor may be:
- sincere;
- strategic;
- manipulative;
- self-serving.

If hidden motives are discovered, relationship interpretation may change retroactively.

## 7.32.27 Calling In a Favor

A character may later ask:
- "Remember what I did for you?"

The request should be evaluated based on:
- actual obligation;
- requested cost;
- relationship;
- risk;
- fairness;
- prior understanding.

A favor opens social leverage.
It does not guarantee compliance.

## 7.32.28 Proportionality

Repayment expectations should be roughly proportional unless the character's culture or values say otherwise.

A minor favor should not plausibly justify:
- major betrayal;
- life-threatening risk;
- permanent servitude.

## 7.32.29 Overreaching Requests

If someone uses a small favor to demand something extreme, the target may:
- refuse;
- feel manipulated;
- lose Respect;
- become Resentful.

The original favor may even lose positive social meaning.

## 7.32.30 Refusing a Favor Request

A character may refuse repayment.

Consequences depend on:
- legitimacy of the request;
- strength of obligation;
- reason for refusal;
- cultural expectations.

Refusal is not automatically betrayal.

## 7.32.31 Repayment

A favor may be repaid through:
- equivalent help;
- protection;
- information;
- introduction;
- resources;
- service;
- symbolic repayment.

The original parties may disagree about whether repayment was sufficient.

## 7.32.32 Obligation Closure

Once an obligation is reasonably satisfied, it should normally close.

Do not allow one favor to become infinite leverage.

Some relationships may continue to carry generalized gratitude even after specific debt is repaid.

## 7.32.33 Partial Repayment

A large obligation may be reduced gradually.

Internal state may track:
- outstanding;
- partially repaid;
- satisfied;
- disputed.

Avoid turning this into a universal point economy.

## 7.32.34 Reciprocal Exchange

Relationships often involve repeated mutual help without explicit accounting.

Examples:
- close friends;
- family;
- teammates.

The simulation should not convert every act into a ledger.

Only socially meaningful imbalance should be tracked.

## 7.32.35 Scorekeeping Personalities

Some characters may explicitly keep track of:
- who owes whom;
- favors;
- gifts;
- sacrifices.

Others may dislike transactional thinking.

This is personality-dependent.

## 7.32.36 Cultural Obligation

Some communities may place strong emphasis on:
- hospitality;
- debt repayment;
- family duty;
- honor;
- patronage.

Cultural norms may increase social pressure without removing individual agency.

## 7.32.37 Clan Obligation

Clan membership may produce expectations such as:
- service;
- secrecy;
- mutual defense;
- resource sharing.

Formal clan requirements belong to future institutional systems.

Ruleset 7 owns the subjective social obligation.

## 7.32.38 Mentor Debt

Students may feel indebted to mentors for:
- training;
- protection;
- advocacy;
- sacrifice.

This can create:
- Loyalty;
- gratitude;
- duty.

It should not automatically create lifelong obedience.

## 7.32.39 Rescue Debt

Being saved from death or catastrophic harm may create powerful gratitude.

Possible responses:
- intense Loyalty;
- desire to repay;
- discomfort;
- resentment at dependency;
- rejection of the debt concept.

Different characters may interpret rescue differently.

## 7.32.40 Life Debt

Some cultures or individuals may interpret rescue as a "life debt."

This should be a belief or norm, not a universal law.

It may motivate extraordinary repayment without creating literal ownership.

## 7.32.41 Patronage

A powerful person may provide:
- employment;
- resources;
- protection;
- introductions;
- training.

This may create social obligation.

Patronage may become exploitative if the benefactor uses dependency to control the recipient.

## 7.32.42 Obligation and Power Imbalance

Obligation can become coercive when:
- recipient depends on benefactor;
- refusal risks housing, safety, employment, or protection;
- debt is deliberately inflated.

Ruleset 7.19 governs autonomy and coercive implications.

## 7.32.43 Emotional Blackmail

A character may invoke:
- past sacrifice;
- family duty;
- guilt;
- loyalty;

to pressure someone.

Example:
- "After everything I've done for you..."

This may be persuasive without making the request legitimate.

## 7.32.44 Favors and Manipulation

A manipulative character may deliberately create indebtedness by:
- helping strategically;
- making favors public;
- withholding aid unless obligation is accepted.

The target's awareness and interpretation matter.

## 7.32.45 Debts Between Enemies

Opponents may still owe one another.

Examples:
- enemy saves enemy's life;
- prisoner spared;
- information traded.

Obligation can coexist with:
- Hostility;
- low Trust;
- rivalry.

## 7.32.46 Debts Between Rivals

Rivals may strongly value fair repayment.

This can create:
- Respect;
- temporary cooperation;
- competitive reciprocity.

## 7.32.47 Promises Under Duress

A promise extracted through:
- threat;
- torture;
- captivity;
- blackmail;

should be treated differently from a freely given commitment.

Its moral or social binding force depends on culture and character values.

## 7.32.48 False Promise

A character may make a promise they never intend to keep.

This is Deception.

If discovered, consequences may be more severe than ordinary failure.

## 7.32.49 Secret Promise

A private promise may be known only to the participants.

Breaking it may create no public reputation effect unless exposed.

The interpersonal consequence can still be severe.

## 7.32.50 Public Oath

A public oath may create strong:
- reputation;
- honor;
- institutional expectations.

Breaking it can affect far more than the direct recipient.

## 7.32.51 Promise to the Dead

A character may feel obligated by promises made to someone who has died.

Possible examples:
- protect their child;
- complete their mission;
- preserve their secret.

The obligation persists because of the survivor's values and memory.

## 7.32.52 Inherited Obligation

Family or clan members may feel bound by:
- ancestral debt;
- old alliance;
- inherited promise.

This should be socially constructed.

A descendant may accept or reject that obligation.

## 7.32.53 Obligation Transfer

A favor owed to one person should not automatically transfer to another.

Transfer may occur if:
- explicitly agreed;
- culturally recognized;
- institutionalized.

Otherwise:
- "Your father owed me" does not automatically mean "you owe me."

## 7.32.54 Third-Party Favors

Someone may ask a favor on behalf of:
- family;
- teammate;
- client.

The target evaluates the actual relationship and obligation involved.

## 7.32.55 Favors as Social Access

A favor may produce:
- introduction;
- meeting;
- recommendation;
- temporary access.

Ruleset 7.22 governs the network consequences.

## 7.32.56 Favors and Reputation

A reputation for:
- keeping promises;
- repaying debts;
- helping allies;

may increase social confidence.

A reputation for:
- exploiting favors;
- breaking promises;
- never repaying;

may create caution.

Ruleset 7.20 owns the persistent reputation.

## 7.32.57 Reliability Reputation

Repeated promise outcomes may generate a domain-specific reputation such as:
- dependable;
- unreliable;
- oath-bound;
- transactional;
- generous.

This should emerge from patterns rather than one minor incident.

## 7.32.58 Broken Word and Trust

Breaking one's word should primarily damage Trust when:
- the other person relied on it;
- the promise was meaningful;
- the failure was avoidable or deceptive.

Not all missed commitments deserve equal Trust loss.

## 7.32.59 Forgiveness

The recipient may forgive a broken promise.

Forgiveness does not necessarily restore:
- Trust;
- reliance;
- willingness to accept future promises.

## 7.32.60 Repairing Broken Commitments

Repair may require:
- admission;
- explanation;
- apology;
- restitution;
- completing delayed obligation;
- changed behavior.

The best repair depends on the harm.

## 7.32.61 Repeated Broken Promises

Repeated failures should create pattern memory.

Future promises may become less credible even when sincerely intended.

## 7.32.62 Overpromising

A character may damage reliability by repeatedly promising more than they can realistically deliver.

Intentions may be good while reputation still declines.

## 7.32.63 Refusing to Promise

A cautious character may say:
- "I can't promise that."

This may preserve Trust better than giving a false assurance.

## 7.32.64 Promise Credibility

An NPC evaluates a promise using:
- prior reliability;
- feasibility;
- motive;
- relationship;
- current circumstances;
- reputation.

Words alone do not guarantee belief.

## 7.32.65 Social Skill and Promises

Persuasion may improve how a promise or offer is communicated.

It cannot make an impossible commitment credible when evidence strongly contradicts it.

Deception may create false credibility temporarily.

## 7.32.66 Negotiated Exchange

Two characters may explicitly trade:
- information for protection;
- help for access;
- service for service.

This is a social bargain.

Ruleset 7.16 governs negotiation.

## 7.32.67 Favor Chains

A favor may create a chain:
- A helps B;
- B later helps C on A's behalf;
- C later becomes connected to A.

These can expand social networks.

The chain should remain causal rather than turning into fungible currency.

## 7.32.68 Obligation Networks

Powerful NPCs may accumulate many people who:
- owe favors;
- depend on them;
- expect reciprocal support.

This can create informal influence without formal authority.

## 7.32.69 Calling in Multiple Debts

Someone may coordinate several obligations to:
- gather support;
- secure information;
- gain introductions.

Each person still evaluates their own obligation independently.

## 7.32.70 NPC-to-NPC Obligations

NPCs should:
- make promises;
- owe favors;
- repay debts;
- break commitments;

without the player being involved.

These obligations may later affect alliances and decisions.

## 7.32.71 Player-Facing Information

The player should know obligations their character:
- explicitly accepted;
- clearly remembers;
- was told about.

Hidden obligations between NPCs should remain hidden until discovered.

## 7.32.72 Obligation Record

For important obligations, the engine may store:

**Type:** promise / favor / gratitude debt / oath
**Owed by:** character
**Owed to:** character or group
**Content:** what is expected
**Origin:** event
**Magnitude:** minor / meaningful / major / defining
**Status:** active / disputed / partially repaid / satisfied / broken
**Conditions:** if any
**Witnesses:** if relevant
**Publicity:** private / known / public

## 7.32.73 Obligation Audit

For an important obligation, the engine should be able to answer:

1. What created the obligation?
2. Did both sides understand it similarly?
3. How costly was the original favor or promise?
4. What repayment is reasonably expected?
5. Has any part already been repaid?
6. Is the request proportional?
7. Is pressure becoming coercive?
8. What would happen socially if the obligation is refused or broken?

## 7.32.74 Design Standard

A promise/favor mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- turns all kindness into debt;
- treats favors as spendable universal currency;
- makes obligation override autonomy;
- lets minor favors justify extreme demands;
- assumes gratitude equals obedience;
- erases debt automatically without considering interpretation;
- treats failed promises and deliberate betrayal identically;
- makes public promises known globally;
- assumes inherited debt is automatically binding;
- tracks every tiny reciprocal act as a ledger entry.

## Governing Rule

**Promises and obligations should matter because people remember, rely on, and interpret them, with their force determined by commitment, proportionality, culture, relationship, and history rather than by an abstract debt currency.**
