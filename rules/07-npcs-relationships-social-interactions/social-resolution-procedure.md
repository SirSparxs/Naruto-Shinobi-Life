# 7.15 — Social Resolution Procedure

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.15 defines the standard procedure for resolving uncertain social actions.

The governing principle is:

**Social resolution determines how effectively an actor influences a plausible social situation; it does not replace NPC agency, knowledge limits, boundaries, or reality.**

## 7.15.1 Resolution Overview

The default sequence is:

1. Identify actor intent.
2. Identify target and desired outcome.
3. Determine whether the outcome is possible.
4. Determine baseline target willingness.
5. Determine whether a roll is needed.
6. Build the social context.
7. Select the primary social skill.
8. Select the relevant supporting attribute.
9. Determine resistance / opposition.
10. Resolve through Ruleset 2.
11. Interpret degree of outcome.
12. Determine immediate NPC response.
13. Apply social consequences.
14. Update memory, beliefs, relationships, goals, and reputation where relevant.
15. Change the situation so identical retries are not free.

Not every interaction requires every step.

Routine interactions may compress several stages.

## 7.15.2 Step 1 — Identify Intent

Determine what the actor is actually trying to accomplish.

Examples:
- obtain information;
- secure cooperation;
- change a belief;
- negotiate terms;
- intimidate;
- apologize;
- flirt;
- seduce;
- de-escalate.

Intent is defined by Ruleset 7.13.

## 7.15.3 Step 2 — Define the Desired Outcome

The outcome should be concrete.

Weak:
- "Persuade her."

Strong:
- "Convince her to postpone reporting the incident until tomorrow morning."

The desired outcome determines:
- stakes;
- cost;
- feasibility;
- resistance;
- relevant skill.

## 7.15.4 Step 3 — Possibility Check

Before any roll, determine whether the requested outcome is:

- automatic / already desired;
- readily possible;
- possible but costly;
- highly resistant;
- currently impossible.

### Automatic / Already Desired
The target already wants to do it.

No roll is normally needed.

### Readily Possible
The target is open and the request is reasonable.

A roll may be unnecessary unless quality matters.

### Possible but Costly
The target may agree, but there are meaningful costs, risks, or conflicts.

This is a common roll case.

### Highly Resistant
The target strongly opposes the outcome but may plausibly move under sufficient evidence, leverage, relationship, or circumstance.

### Currently Impossible
The action falls outside plausible willingness or capability.

Examples:
- revealing information the target does not know;
- ordinary persuasion compelling suicide;
- seduction converting explicit refusal into consent;
- intimidation relying on a threat the target knows cannot occur;
- one conversation erasing a defining betrayal.

No roll should be used to brute-force an impossible outcome.

## 7.15.5 Possibility Can Change

"Impossible" usually means impossible under current conditions, not metaphysically impossible forever.

Future changes may include:
- new evidence;
- changed relationship;
- desperation;
- different leverage;
- changed goals;
- different authority;
- character development.

The simulation should record why the outcome is unavailable.

## 7.15.6 Step 4 — Baseline Willingness

Determine what the target would likely do before the actor's current social attempt.

Useful qualitative states:

- eager;
- willing;
- leaning willing;
- undecided;
- reluctant;
- strongly resistant;
- firmly opposed.

Baseline willingness draws from:
- goals;
- values;
- relationships;
- beliefs;
- obligations;
- current emotion;
- consequences.

This is not the final outcome.

It is the starting position.

## 7.15.7 Baseline Willingness Is Outcome-Specific

An NPC can be:
- eager to help with a mission;
- unwilling to lend money;
- firmly opposed to revealing a secret;

all toward the same person.

Do not use one global disposition as willingness.

## 7.15.8 Step 5 — Determine Whether a Roll Is Needed

A roll is needed only when:
- outcome is uncertain;
- meaningful resistance exists;
- degree of success matters;
- failure would matter.

Do not roll for:
- obvious acceptance;
- obvious refusal;
- routine social behavior;
- voluntary self-expression.

## 7.15.9 Step 6 — Build Social Context

Use Ruleset 7.12 to identify the factors that materially affect the attempt.

Examples:
- privacy;
- audience;
- rank;
- legitimacy;
- culture;
- emotional state;
- timing;
- evidence;
- leverage;
- request cost;
- shared goals;
- power imbalance;
- history.

The engine should identify dominant factors rather than stacking dozens of tiny modifiers.

## 7.15.10 Step 7 — Select Primary Skill

Choose the skill matching the actor's actual method.

Examples:
- logical appeal -> Persuasion;
- bargaining terms -> Negotiation;
- false story -> Deception;
- threat -> Intimidation;
- structured information extraction -> Interrogation;
- adult flirtatious escalation -> Seduction.

Do not automatically choose the actor's best social skill.

## 7.15.11 Step 8 — Select Supporting Attribute

Choose the attribute that best represents how the skill is being applied.

Examples:
- logical Persuasion -> Intelligence;
- emotionally adaptive Persuasion -> Perception;
- controlled Intimidation -> Willpower;
- physical Intimidation -> Strength;
- cue-reading Seduction -> Perception;
- composed Seduction -> Willpower.

Ruleset 1 owns the attributes and skill progression.

## 7.15.12 Step 9 — Determine Resistance

Resistance may come from:
- target Willpower;
- opposing social skill;
- values;
- relationship state;
- hard boundaries;
- goal conflict;
- suspicion;
- authority structure;
- consequences;
- knowledge.

The target does not always need an explicit opposing skill.

Sometimes resistance is primarily circumstantial.

## 7.15.13 Opposed Resolution

Use an opposed resolution when another actor is actively countering the attempt.

Examples:
- Deception vs Insight;
- Negotiation vs Negotiation;
- Interrogation vs Deception / resistance;
- debate between two advocates.

Ruleset 2 owns the mechanics.

## 7.15.14 Static / Contextual Difficulty

Use contextual difficulty when the main resistance comes from the situation rather than active counter-performance.

Examples:
- convincing a friend to take a moderate risk;
- reassuring a frightened civilian;
- asking for a meaningful favor.

## 7.15.15 Hidden Resolution

Some checks should be hidden.

Examples:
- whether a lie was believed;
- whether the target noticed manipulation;
- whether flirtation was genuinely reciprocated;
- whether a witness was persuaded or merely pretending.

Player-facing narration should reveal only what the character can observe.

## 7.15.16 Step 10 — Ruleset 2 Resolution

Ruleset 2 determines:
- success or failure;
- degree of success;
- opposed outcome;
- hidden result;
- uncertainty.

Ruleset 7 then interprets what that means socially.

## 7.15.17 Degree-of-Outcome Interpretation

A social result should rarely be limited to binary yes/no.

Possible interpretations include:

### Strong Failure
- target becomes more resistant;
- attempt is exposed;
- relationship damage;
- unintended consequence.

### Failure
- desired outcome is not achieved;
- target may refuse, delay, or disengage.

### Partial Success
- limited concession;
- incomplete information;
- agreement with conditions;
- temporary cooperation.

### Success
- desired plausible outcome is achieved.

### Strong / Exceptional Success
- outcome achieved cleanly;
- better terms;
- reduced cost;
- improved future context;
- deeper disclosure;

but still only within plausible limits.

## 7.15.18 Exceptional Success Does Not Break Limits

Exceptional success cannot:
- manufacture knowledge;
- erase identity;
- override consent;
- create impossible loyalty;
- bypass supernatural restrictions;
- ignore obvious consequences.

It improves quality within the valid outcome space.

## 7.15.19 Target Response Is Still a Decision

After resolution, the NPC decision system determines the actual behavioral response.

A successful persuasion may change:
- perceived benefit;
- perceived risk;
- willingness;
- interpretation.

Then the NPC chooses.

For many interactions, the result and decision are effectively simultaneous.

For high-stakes interactions, preserving this distinction helps maintain agency.

## 7.15.20 Compliance vs Agreement

The engine should distinguish:

- genuine agreement;
- reluctant agreement;
- compliance from duty;
- compliance from fear;
- coerced compliance;
- strategic cooperation;
- false cooperation.

All may look like "yes" externally.

Their future consequences differ dramatically.

## 7.15.21 Information Outcomes

When the intended outcome involves information, resolve separately:
- whether target is willing to disclose;
- what target actually knows;
- what they believe;
- whether they lie;
- how complete the disclosure is.

A social success cannot improve the truth quality of information the target misunderstands.

## 7.15.22 Persuasion and Belief

Changing belief should depend heavily on:
- evidence;
- source credibility;
- prior confidence;
- worldview;
- expertise.

A strong Persuasion result may improve openness to reconsideration.

It should not make obviously false claims true.

## 7.15.23 Relationship Outcomes

A social result may also affect:
- Trust;
- Respect;
- Affection;
- Loyalty;
- Fear;
- Obligation;
- Resentment;
- Hostility;
- Romantic Interest;
- adult Sexual Chemistry.

Only relevant dimensions should change.

Use Ruleset 7.11.

## 7.15.24 Emotional Outcomes

Interactions may create or change:
- anger;
- fear;
- relief;
- embarrassment;
- jealousy;
- confidence;
- hope;
- adult arousal where applicable.

Use Ruleset 7.8.

## 7.15.25 Memory Outcomes

A sufficiently meaningful interaction should create memory.

High-stakes:
- confession;
- betrayal;
- threat;
- rejection;
- major apology;
- romantic turning point;
- coercion;

should usually persist.

Use Ruleset 7.7.

## 7.15.26 Goal Outcomes

Social interactions may:
- create goals;
- remove goals;
- reprioritize goals;
- alter plans.

Example:
A failed threat may create:
- "report this person";
- "seek protection";
- "take revenge."

Use Ruleset 7.4.

## 7.15.27 Reputation Outcomes

If observers or later reports exist, a social action may affect reputation.

Examples:
- public humiliation;
- inspiring speech;
- exposed lie;
- abusive threat;
- successful negotiation.

Reputation effects require plausible information spread.

## 7.15.28 Retry Rule

A failed social attempt normally changes context.

Possible effects:
- target becomes suspicious;
- patience decreases;
- resistance increases;
- conversation ends;
- new evidence is required;
- different approach becomes necessary.

Identical immediate retries should usually be invalid or strongly disadvantaged.

## 7.15.29 New Evidence Creates New Attempt

A new attempt becomes meaningfully different when something changes, such as:
- new evidence;
- new leverage;
- new relationship state;
- different location;
- passage of time;
- different argument;
- different target goal;
- changed authority.

This is not reroll spam.

It is a changed situation.

## 7.15.30 Conversation-Level Resolution

Do not roll every sentence.

One roll may resolve:
- a persuasion sequence;
- a negotiation phase;
- a deception;
- a romantic advance;
- an interrogation segment.

Additional rolls should occur only when:
- intent changes;
- stakes change;
- new information appears;
- new opposition emerges;
- another meaningful turning point occurs.

## 7.15.31 Extended Social Actions

Some interactions require multiple stages.

Examples:
- diplomacy;
- interrogation;
- complex negotiation;
- courtship;
- long-term persuasion;
- reconciliation.

Each stage should change state and context.

Do not reduce long-term processes to one decisive roll.

## 7.15.32 Social Progress Clocks

For especially complex social objectives, the engine may use hidden progress toward:
- agreement;
- trust rebuilding;
- information extraction;
- reconciliation.

Progress should be tied to concrete developments, not arbitrary point accumulation.

Do not use progress clocks for ordinary conversation.

## 7.15.33 Group Resolution

When influencing a group:
- identify shared group pressure;
- identify leaders;
- identify important holdouts;
- allow individual reactions.

A successful speech may shift overall group mood without converting every member.

## 7.15.34 Cascading Social Effects

One interaction may trigger later consequences.

Example:
- subordinate reluctantly obeys;
- later tells teammate;
- rumor spreads;
- command reputation changes.

Immediate resolution should not attempt to resolve the entire future chain at once.

Instead create the appropriate state and let later systems propagate it.

## 7.15.35 Natural-Language Engine Behavior

The player should be able to speak normally.

Example:
"I show her the report, explain that the mission details don't match Dagar's story, and ask her to hold off arresting Karn until we investigate."

The engine can infer:
- Intent: persuade / delay action;
- Skill: Persuasion;
- Attribute: likely Intelligence;
- Evidence: strong;
- Stakes: high;
- Target cost: delay investigation;
- Desired outcome: postpone arrest.

The player does not need to specify mechanics manually.

## 7.15.36 NPC Social Resolution

NPCs use the same procedure when acting on:
- player;
- other NPCs.

The player should not receive special immunity from:
- deception;
- persuasion;
- intimidation;
- seduction;
- social pressure.

However, social influence should never remove meaningful player agency.

When an NPC influences the player character, mechanics may:
- alter perceived credibility;
- provide emotional pressure;
- create character impulses;
- change known information;
- impose consequences;

but should not arbitrarily choose the player's voluntary decision unless another ruleset explicitly governs loss of control.

## 7.15.37 Player Agency Protection

For player-controlled voluntary choices, NPC social success should generally provide:
- compelling information;
- emotional state;
- incentives;
- pressures;
- consequences;

rather than dictating:
- "You agree."

Exceptions require explicit mechanics such as supernatural compulsion owned elsewhere.

## 7.15.38 Social Resolution Record

For major interactions, internal state may store:

**Intent:** Delay arrest  
**Method:** Persuasion  
**Skill:** Persuasion  
**Attribute:** Intelligence  
**Baseline Willingness:** Reluctant  
**Context:** Strong evidence, high urgency  
**Resolution:** Success  
**Outcome:** Target delays arrest for six hours  
**Relationship:** Respect + minor, Trust unchanged  
**Memory:** Notable  
**New Goal:** Verify report

This is useful for continuity and debugging.

## 7.15.39 Design Standard

A social-resolution mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- rolls before defining the desired outcome;
- uses rolls for obvious outcomes;
- lets exceptional success bypass impossibility;
- ignores baseline willingness;
- chooses the actor's strongest skill instead of actual method;
- treats all success as enthusiastic agreement;
- allows repeated identical retries;
- resolves long-term relationships with one check;
- makes information more accurate than the target's knowledge;
- removes player agency through mundane NPC social skill;
- updates every relationship dimension after every interaction;
- duplicates Ruleset 2 probability mechanics.

## Governing Rule

**Resolve social actions by first establishing what is possible and what the target already wants, then use context, skill, attributes, and Ruleset 2 only for the genuinely uncertain portion of the interaction, with consequences that persist afterward.**
