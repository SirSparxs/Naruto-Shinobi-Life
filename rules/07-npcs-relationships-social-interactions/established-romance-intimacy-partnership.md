# 7.28 — Established Romance, Intimacy & Partnership

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.28 defines how established romantic relationships function over time after formation.

The governing principle is:

**An established relationship is not a completed state; it is an evolving partnership shaped by attachment, expectations, intimacy, maintenance, conflict, compatibility, shared history, and continued choice.**

## 7.28.1 Scope

This section governs:
- established romantic partnerships;
- attachment and love;
- relationship maintenance;
- affection styles;
- emotional intimacy;
- adult physical and sexual intimacy;
- shared routines;
- partnership roles;
- relationship satisfaction;
- commitment change;
- long-distance relationships;
- relationship development after formation.

Later sections govern:
- boundaries and consent in greater depth;
- jealousy, insecurity, and rival romantic conflict;
- breakups, separation, and reconciliation.

## 7.28.2 Relationship Status Does Not Freeze State

Once a relationship forms, its dimensions continue changing.

Partners may gain or lose:
- Trust;
- Affection;
- Respect;
- Loyalty;
- Romantic Interest;
- Adult Sexual Chemistry;
- Resentment;
- Familiarity.

A relationship label does not lock these values.

## 7.28.3 Love Is Emergent

Love should not exist as one universal hidden score.

It may emerge from combinations of:
- deep Affection;
- Attachment;
- Trust;
- Loyalty;
- Romantic Interest;
- shared history;
- emotional importance;
- commitment.

Different characters may experience and express love differently.

## 7.28.4 Love Can Exist Without Compatibility

Two people may sincerely love each other while remaining poorly compatible.

This may occur because of:
- conflicting values;
- incompatible goals;
- different lifestyles;
- mismatched intimacy needs;
- different communication styles.

Love should matter greatly without becoming a universal solution.

## 7.28.5 Love Can Persist Through Conflict

Relationship conflict does not automatically erase love.

Characters may experience:
- Affection;
- anger;
- Resentment;
- Loyalty;
- grief;

at the same time.

Contradictory emotional states should remain valid.

## 7.28.6 Attachment

Attachment represents the emotional importance of the partner and the relationship.

High Attachment may create:
- comfort in proximity;
- distress during separation;
- protectiveness;
- strong memory salience;
- grief after loss.

Attachment does not imply healthy dependency.

## 7.28.7 Security

A relationship may feel:
- secure;
- uncertain;
- unstable;
- threatened.

Security emerges from:
- reliability;
- communication;
- Trust;
- expectation consistency;
- prior conflict.

Security should not be one permanent trait.

## 7.28.8 Relationship Satisfaction

Satisfaction describes how well the relationship currently meets each partner's relevant needs and expectations.

It should be directional.

A may be very satisfied while B is increasingly unhappy.

Satisfaction may be influenced by:
- emotional connection;
- support;
- affection;
- communication;
- intimacy;
- autonomy;
- shared time;
- conflict;
- compatibility.

## 7.28.9 Satisfaction Is Not Love

A person may:
- love their partner;
- remain deeply attached;
- still be unhappy in the relationship.

This distinction is essential for believable breakups.

## 7.28.10 Partnership Expectations

Established partners may develop expectations around:
- communication;
- availability;
- honesty;
- exclusivity;
- support;
- affection;
- privacy;
- independence;
- shared decisions;
- mission risk;
- family involvement.

These expectations may be:
- explicit;
- implicit;
- mutually agreed;
- mismatched.

## 7.28.11 Relationship Maintenance

Relationships may be maintained through:
- time together;
- communication;
- affection;
- support;
- reliability;
- shared activities;
- repair after conflict;
- respecting autonomy.

Maintenance should not become repetitive chores.

## 7.28.12 Maintenance Needs Vary

Some partners need:
- frequent contact;
- frequent reassurance;
- shared routines.

Others need:
- substantial independence;
- less frequent contact;
- private space.

There is no universal ideal frequency.

## 7.28.13 Neglect

Neglect becomes meaningful when important relationship expectations repeatedly go unmet.

Possible effects:
- reduced satisfaction;
- reduced Affection;
- increased Resentment;
- insecurity;
- emotional distance.

One missed interaction should not automatically count as neglect.

## 7.28.14 Affection Styles

Characters may commonly express affection through:
- verbal reassurance;
- physical affection;
- gifts;
- acts of service;
- shared time;
- protection;
- humor;
- practical support.

These are behavioral patterns, not rigid personality categories.

## 7.28.15 Affection Mismatch

One partner may express affection differently from how the other recognizes it.

Example:
- A shows care through practical help.
- B wants verbal reassurance.

Both may care deeply while B still feels emotionally neglected.

## 7.28.16 Emotional Intimacy

Emotional intimacy involves:
- vulnerability;
- disclosure;
- emotional safety;
- being understood;
- mutual support;
- sharing fears, hopes, or private thoughts.

It often develops through Trust and repeated experience.

## 7.28.17 Emotional Intimacy Can Be Asymmetric

One partner may disclose extensively while the other remains guarded.

This may reflect:
- personality;
- trauma;
- Trust;
- habit;
- cultural expectation.

Asymmetry may or may not be a problem.

## 7.28.18 Privacy Within Partnership

Being partners does not eliminate personal privacy.

Characters may retain:
- private thoughts;
- private friendships;
- professional secrets;
- personal space.

Expectations around privacy should be negotiated or culturally shaped.

## 7.28.19 Secrets Within Relationships

A partner may keep secrets because of:
- duty;
- fear;
- shame;
- professional obligation;
- deception.

The social meaning depends on:
- what was hidden;
- why;
- expected disclosure;
- consequences.

## 7.28.20 Shared Routines

Established couples may develop:
- meals;
- schedules;
- training habits;
- sleeping arrangements;
- traditions;
- communication routines;
- recurring dates.

Routines can increase:
- Familiarity;
- security;
- Attachment.

They may also become stale for some personalities.

## 7.28.21 Shared History

Milestones accumulate into relationship history.

Examples:
- first mission together;
- first home;
- major injury;
- reunion after absence;
- anniversary;
- family event.

These memories can become emotional anchors.

## 7.28.22 Partnership Roles

Partners may informally divide responsibilities involving:
- finances;
- household;
- emotional support;
- planning;
- social obligations;
- caregiving;
- mission logistics.

Roles should emerge or be negotiated.

## 7.28.23 Role Imbalance

Unequal responsibility can create:
- fatigue;
- resentment;
- dependence;
- gratitude.

Whether imbalance is problematic depends on:
- consent;
- expectations;
- duration;
- perceived fairness.

## 7.28.24 Mutual Support

Partners may support:
- training;
- career;
- recovery;
- family obligations;
- emotional wellbeing.

Support can increase Trust and Affection when it matches actual needs.

## 7.28.25 Overprotection

Protectiveness can become controlling when it overrides another person's autonomy.

Examples:
- preventing missions;
- monitoring;
- making decisions for partner;
- restricting contacts.

Intent to protect does not automatically make behavior healthy or welcome.

## 7.28.26 Independence

Healthy partnership may include substantial independent:
- goals;
- friendships;
- missions;
- hobbies;
- identity.

High independence does not imply low commitment.

## 7.28.27 Dependency

Partners may become practically or emotionally dependent.

Dependency can be:
- ordinary and temporary;
- mutually supportive;
- unhealthy;
- coercively exploited.

Ruleset 7.19 governs autonomy implications.

## 7.28.28 Adult Physical Intimacy

For adults 18+ only, established relationships may include physical intimacy such as:
- kissing;
- cuddling;
- sleeping together;
- sexual activity.

The specific form depends on:
- attraction;
- adult Sexual Chemistry;
- preference;
- boundaries;
- current willingness;
- relationship context.

Relationship status never automatically authorizes any intimate act.

## 7.28.29 Adult Sexual Relationship State

Where simulation-relevant, adult partners may develop persistent expectations involving:
- frequency;
- initiation;
- preferred activities;
- preferred roles or dynamics;
- experimentation;
- privacy;
- exclusivity;
- contraception or reproductive planning where relevant.

These should remain private unless revealed in-world.

## 7.28.30 Sexual Compatibility

Adult sexual compatibility may involve:
- preferred frequency;
- activities;
- roles;
- initiation style;
- novelty;
- comfort;
- communication;
- boundaries.

Compatibility can change through communication and experience.

## 7.28.31 Desire Discrepancy

Adult partners may have different levels of:
- libido;
- current desire;
- preferred frequency.

This should not automatically imply:
- rejection of partner;
- loss of love;
- infidelity.

It may require communication and compromise.

## 7.28.32 Current Desire vs Persistent Attraction

An adult may remain strongly attracted to a partner while not currently wanting sexual activity.

Current desire is temporary.

Attraction and chemistry are more persistent.

## 7.28.33 Initiation

Either adult partner may initiate intimacy.

The other may:
- reciprocate;
- decline;
- suggest something different;
- ask to postpone.

A declined initiation should not automatically damage the relationship.

## 7.28.34 Consent Remains Event-Specific

Prior:
- dating;
- marriage;
- sexual history;
- current arousal;
- past consent;

does not create automatic consent now.

Ruleset 7.29 or the dedicated boundaries/consent section should govern detailed consent handling.

## 7.28.35 Intimacy and Trust

Physical or sexual intimacy may:
- increase Trust;
- increase Attachment;
- increase Affection;
- reveal compatibility;
- reveal incompatibility.

No universal positive effect should be assumed.

## 7.28.36 Intimacy and Vulnerability

Intimacy may create emotional vulnerability.

Possible outcomes:
- closeness;
- reassurance;
- embarrassment;
- insecurity;
- regret;
- stronger attachment.

Context determines which.

## 7.28.37 Intimacy Does Not Repair Everything

Adult physical intimacy should not automatically:
- resolve conflict;
- erase betrayal;
- restore Trust;
- remove Resentment.

It may temporarily create closeness while deeper problems remain.

## 7.28.38 Adult Intimacy Without Romance

Adult sexual relationships may exist without:
- Romantic Interest;
- long-term commitment.

This section primarily governs established romance, but the broader adult-intimacy mechanics should support consensual non-romantic arrangements where later rules require them.

## 7.28.39 Relationship Structure

Established adult partnerships may follow:
- monogamy;
- consensual non-monogamy;
- other mutually agreed structures.

The important mechanical state is:
- what each person believes the agreement is;
- what has actually been agreed;
- whether actions comply with it.

## 7.28.40 Exclusivity

Exclusivity should be persistent once mutually established until:
- renegotiated;
- ended;
- violated.

A violation can create betrayal even if attraction to others was never prohibited internally.

## 7.28.41 Commitment Change

Commitment can:
- deepen;
- stabilize;
- weaken;
- become uncertain.

Possible causes:
- shared life;
- support;
- betrayal;
- changing goals;
- incompatibility;
- distance;
- external pressure.

Commitment should not automatically increase with time.

## 7.28.42 Deepening Partnership

Partnership may deepen through:
- shared plans;
- increased Trust;
- mutual sacrifice;
- living together;
- family integration;
- surviving hardship;
- raising children where applicable;
- long-term reliability.

No single milestone universally defines seriousness.

## 7.28.43 Cohabitation

Partners may live together.

Cohabitation can reveal:
- habits;
- privacy needs;
- responsibility division;
- financial expectations;
- lifestyle compatibility.

Moving in should create new context rather than automatically increasing relationship quality.

## 7.28.44 Engagement and Marriage

Engagement or marriage may represent:
- personal commitment;
- legal status;
- clan alliance;
- religious or cultural ceremony;
- political arrangement.

Future legal/institutional rules may own formal consequences.

Ruleset 7 owns:
- emotional meaning;
- expectations;
- relationship state.

## 7.28.45 Marriage Does Not Guarantee Stability

Marriage may coexist with:
- high love and high satisfaction;
- low satisfaction;
- resentment;
- poor compatibility;
- separation.

Status should never replace relationship state.

## 7.28.46 Family Integration

Partners may form relationships with:
- parents;
- siblings;
- clan members;
- children;
- chosen family.

These are separate relationship objects.

Partner approval does not guarantee family approval.

## 7.28.47 Shared Social Network

Established relationships often create overlapping networks.

This can:
- increase support;
- complicate privacy;
- create mutual friends;
- increase breakup consequences.

Ruleset 7.22 governs the network layer.

## 7.28.48 Public Couple Identity

Some couples develop a public shared identity.

This may affect:
- reputation;
- invitations;
- politics;
- clan expectations;
- social assumptions.

The public image may differ from private reality.

## 7.28.49 Private Relationship Reality

A couple publicly seen as ideal may privately be:
- distant;
- conflicted;
- unhappy.

A couple viewed as unstable may privately be secure.

Public image does not determine actual relationship quality.

## 7.28.50 Long-Distance Relationships

Distance may occur because of:
- missions;
- village assignments;
- travel;
- war;
- imprisonment;
- exile.

Distance may reduce:
- contact frequency;
- physical intimacy;
- shared routine.

It may increase:
- longing;
- insecurity;
- independence;
- communication importance.

## 7.28.51 Distance Does Not Automatically Cause Decay

Some relationships tolerate long separation well.

Others deteriorate rapidly.

Important factors include:
- Trust;
- expectations;
- communication;
- Attachment;
- personality;
- reason for absence.

## 7.28.52 Reunion

Reunion may produce:
- relief;
- excitement;
- awkwardness;
- changed expectations.

Long separation may mean both partners changed.

Familiarity with past self does not equal familiarity with current self.

## 7.28.53 Mission Risk

Shinobi relationships may regularly confront:
- injury;
- death risk;
- secrecy;
- sudden deployment.

Partners may differ in how much risk they tolerate.

Repeated dangerous choices can create:
- worry;
- resentment;
- admiration;
- acceptance.

## 7.28.54 Professional Conflict

Partners may have:
- competing missions;
- rank differences;
- command relationships;
- confidential information;
- conflicts of interest.

Romance should not automatically override professional obligations.

## 7.28.55 Support During Injury or Illness

A partner may provide:
- caregiving;
- practical help;
- emotional support.

Ruleset 4 owns the medical state.
Ruleset 7 owns the relationship effects of caregiving.

## 7.28.56 Caregiver Strain

Long-term caregiving can create:
- exhaustion;
- guilt;
- resentment;
- deeper Attachment;
- gratitude.

The simulation should not assume caregiving always strengthens a relationship.

## 7.28.57 Shared Goals

Couples may develop shared goals such as:
- home;
- family;
- career;
- travel;
- clan responsibility;
- retirement.

Shared goals can strengthen partnership but may later diverge.

## 7.28.58 Diverging Goals

Partners may gradually want different futures.

Examples:
- one wants children;
- one does not;
- one wants leadership;
- one wants retirement;
- one wants to leave the village.

This may create negotiation or incompatibility.

## 7.28.59 Relationship Check-Ins

Partners may periodically discuss:
- satisfaction;
- concerns;
- expectations;
- plans.

These should occur naturally based on personality and events.

They should not be mandatory scheduled mechanics.

## 7.28.60 Conflict Is Normal

Established relationships may experience:
- disagreement;
- frustration;
- jealousy;
- disappointment;
- misunderstanding.

Conflict itself should not indicate relationship failure.

How conflict is handled matters more.

## 7.28.61 Repair

Healthy repair may include:
- acknowledgment;
- apology;
- listening;
- compromise;
- changed behavior;
- restitution;
- reassurance.

Ruleset 7.32 will govern rupture and reconciliation more broadly.

## 7.28.62 Repeated Unresolved Conflict

Repeated unresolved issues may create:
- pattern memories;
- anticipatory tension;
- Resentment;
- reduced satisfaction.

The relationship can degrade without one dramatic betrayal.

## 7.28.63 Relationship Plateau

Some relationships reach stable periods with little dramatic change.

This is valid.

A simulation should not manufacture conflict merely to keep romance interesting.

## 7.28.64 Growth Without Crisis

Relationships can deepen through:
- ordinary reliability;
- shared routines;
- humor;
- support;
- accumulated positive memory.

Not all progression requires dramatic events.

## 7.28.65 Relationship Transition

Established romance may evolve into:
- marriage;
- lifelong partnership;
- companionate relationship;
- long-distance partnership;
- separation;
- friendship after romance.

Transition should follow actual decisions and history.

## 7.28.66 Romantic Interest Over Time

Romantic Interest may:
- remain high;
- stabilize;
- decline;
- rekindle.

A long-term relationship can persist during temporary lower romantic intensity if commitment and affection remain strong.

## 7.28.67 Affection Without Romantic Excitement

Long-term partners may shift from intense early romance toward:
- companionship;
- deep Attachment;
- trust;
- routine Affection.

This should not automatically be treated as relationship decline.

## 7.28.68 Novelty and Excitement

Some characters value novelty strongly.

Others prefer predictability.

Changes in excitement should depend on individual needs rather than universal "relationship boredom."

## 7.28.69 Betrayal Risk

Established intimacy creates more expectations and vulnerability.

Violations involving:
- exclusivity;
- secrets;
- abandonment;
- manipulation;

may therefore carry larger social consequences.

Later sections govern specific rupture.

## 7.28.70 Relationship Satisfaction Audit

For important partnerships, the engine may internally answer:

1. What does each partner currently value in the relationship?
2. Which needs are being met?
3. Which expectations are unmet?
4. Where are major compatibility conflicts?
5. How secure does each person feel?
6. How committed is each person?
7. What recent events strengthened or weakened the bond?
8. Are problems temporary or becoming patterns?

## 7.28.71 Persistent Relationship Record

For established relationships, the engine may preserve:

**Relationship label**
**Formation date**
**Structure / exclusivity**
**Public status**
**Key expectations**
**Shared milestones**
**Major unresolved conflicts**
**Shared routines**
**Current commitment state**
**Important compatibility issues**

Do not duplicate relationship dimensions already stored elsewhere.

## 7.28.72 NPC-to-NPC Partnership

NPC couples should:
- spend time together;
- support each other;
- argue;
- deepen commitment;
- drift apart;

off-screen when causally relevant.

The player does not need to witness every development.

## 7.28.73 Player Agency

The player controls voluntary decisions such as:
- staying in the relationship;
- making commitments;
- initiating intimacy;
- moving in;
- proposing marriage.

The engine may generate:
- feelings;
- attraction;
- partner requests;
- consequences;
- relationship pressure.

Ordinary social mechanics should not force the player's voluntary commitment.

## 7.28.74 Design Standard

A partnership mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- treats relationship formation as the end of development;
- assumes love equals compatibility;
- assumes marriage equals satisfaction;
- turns relationship maintenance into repetitive chores;
- makes adult intimacy automatically improve Trust or Affection;
- assumes sexual frequency measures relationship quality;
- treats rejection of intimacy as rejection of the partner;
- assumes distance automatically causes decay;
- treats conflict as automatic relationship failure;
- creates drama simply because the relationship is stable;
- makes relationship status override consent.

## Governing Rule

**Established romance should remain an evolving relationship in which affection, attachment, intimacy, autonomy, compatibility, commitment, and shared life continue changing through ordinary experience as well as major events.**
