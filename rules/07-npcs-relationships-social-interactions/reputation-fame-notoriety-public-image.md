# 7.20 — Reputation, Fame, Notoriety & Public Image

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.20 defines how people, communities, organizations, and regions perceive a character beyond direct personal relationships.

The governing principle is:

**Reputation is distributed social belief, not an objective summary of who someone truly is.**

A character may have several different reputations at the same time, each held by different audiences and based on different information.

## 7.20.1 Reputation vs Relationship

Relationship state is personal.

Reputation is socially distributed.

An NPC may:
- personally trust someone with a terrible reputation;
- distrust someone widely admired;
- know nothing about someone famous elsewhere.

Personal experience may reinforce or override reputation.

## 7.20.2 Reputation vs Fame

These should remain distinct.

### Reputation
What people believe about a person's qualities, behavior, history, or character.

### Fame
How widely recognized or discussed the person is.

A character can be:
- highly famous with a mixed reputation;
- infamous and widely recognized;
- respected but known only locally;
- unknown despite being extremely capable.

## 7.20.3 Notoriety

Notoriety represents recognition associated with danger, scandal, crime, fear, or controversy.

Notoriety is not simply "negative fame."

A notorious shinobi may be:
- feared;
- admired;
- respected;
- hated;
- mythologized;

depending on the observer.

## 7.20.4 Public Image

Public image is the socially visible narrative attached to a character.

It may include:
- titles;
- nicknames;
- known achievements;
- accusations;
- symbolic identity;
- public behavior;
- rumors;
- institutional messaging.

Public image may differ substantially from private reality.

## 7.20.5 Reputation Is Domain-Specific

Avoid one universal Reputation score.

Useful domains may include:
- combat capability;
- reliability;
- honesty;
- loyalty;
- cruelty;
- leadership;
- medical expertise;
- intelligence work;
- diplomacy;
- criminality;
- generosity;
- romantic behavior;
- professional competence.

Only relevant domains should be tracked.

## 7.20.6 Audience-Specific Reputation

Different audiences may hold different views.

Examples:

**Kusagakure civilians**
- sees someone as local hero.

**Village intelligence**
- sees same person as security risk.

**Criminal network**
- sees them as dangerous but reliable.

**Enemy shinobi**
- sees them as notorious opponent.

There is no requirement for these reputations to converge.

## 7.20.7 Geographic Scope

Reputation should have scope.

Possible scopes:
- household;
- neighborhood;
- academy;
- clan;
- team;
- village;
- country;
- regional;
- international.

A local incident should not automatically produce international fame.

## 7.20.8 Social-Network Scope

Information may also spread through specific networks:
- merchants;
- medics;
- shinobi;
- nobles;
- clan members;
- criminal organizations;
- intelligence communities.

Someone may be famous among seal specialists while unknown to civilians.

## 7.20.9 Recognition

Fame does not mean every person recognizes the character immediately.

Recognition depends on:
- fame;
- audience;
- location;
- visual familiarity;
- aliases;
- disguises;
- age of information;
- available portraits or descriptions.

A name may be famous even when the face is not.

## 7.20.10 Name Recognition vs Face Recognition

Track separately where relevant:
- recognizes name;
- recognizes face;
- knows title;
- knows reputation;
- knows specific history.

This is especially useful for wanted shinobi, aliases, and public figures.

## 7.20.11 Aliases and Titles

A character may accumulate:
- legal name;
- nickname;
- codename;
- shinobi title;
- criminal alias;
- false identity.

Reputation may attach to the alias rather than the real person.

The two reputations should merge only when the identity connection becomes known.

## 7.20.12 False Identity Reputation

A long-running false identity may develop its own reputation.

Example:
An infiltrator's cover identity may become known as:
- helpful merchant;
- reliable courier;
- quiet traveler.

Exposure can connect that social history back to the real identity.

## 7.20.13 Reputation Sources

Reputation may arise from:
- direct witnesses;
- repeated personal accounts;
- mission reports;
- official announcements;
- public records;
- propaganda;
- rumors;
- criminal notices;
- visible achievements;
- trials;
- public ceremonies;
- media or equivalent communication channels.

The source influences credibility.

## 7.20.14 Reputation Requires Information Spread

An event should affect reputation only if relevant people learn about it.

A secret heroic act may create no public reputation at all.

A fabricated story may create major reputation despite being false.

This preserves the distinction between truth and social belief.

## 7.20.15 Reputation Accuracy

A reputation may be:
- accurate;
- mostly accurate;
- exaggerated;
- incomplete;
- outdated;
- false.

The simulation should preserve the objective events separately.

## 7.20.16 Reputation Confidence

Groups may hold a reputation with varying confidence.

Example:
- "I've heard she's dangerous."
vs.
- "Everyone in ANBU knows she's dangerous."

Confidence depends on source quality and repetition.

## 7.20.17 Reputation Momentum

Repeated similar reports reinforce a reputation.

Examples:
- multiple successful rescues -> dependable hero;
- repeated broken agreements -> unreliable;
- several brutal victories -> dangerous opponent.

One isolated event should usually have less effect than a pattern unless the event is extraordinary.

## 7.20.18 Reputation Inertia

Established reputation should resist trivial contradiction.

A person known for honesty for fifteen years should not become universally known as a liar because of one minor disputed story.

Major scandals can still cause rapid change.

## 7.20.19 Reputation Shock

Certain events may cause sudden major public-image change.

Examples:
- public betrayal;
- major victory;
- exposed crime;
- assassination;
- highly visible rescue;
- confirmed scandal.

Shock magnitude depends on:
- visibility;
- credibility;
- importance;
- audience relevance.

## 7.20.20 Positive and Negative Reputation Are Not Symmetric

Some negative events may spread faster because they are:
- shocking;
- threatening;
- scandalous.

Some positive events may spread strongly because they are:
- inspirational;
- politically useful;
- highly visible.

No universal rule should assume negativity always spreads faster.

## 7.20.21 Reputation and Evidence

Strong evidence makes reputational claims more durable.

Weak or anonymous rumor should generally produce:
- uncertainty;
- suspicion;
- unstable reputation.

Official confirmation may dramatically change belief.

## 7.20.22 Reputation and Rumor

Ruleset 7.21 will govern rumor propagation.

Ruleset 7.20 handles the resulting persistent social image when enough people adopt a belief.

Rumor may therefore be one input into reputation.

## 7.20.23 Institutional Reputation

Organizations may maintain official assessments.

Examples:
- bingo book threat rating;
- internal reliability assessment;
- disciplinary history;
- mission-performance reputation.

These may differ from public opinion.

## 7.20.24 Public Reputation vs Classified Reputation

A shinobi may publicly be known as:
- ordinary jonin;

while classified intelligence knows:
- elite assassination specialist.

The system should preserve separate audience layers.

## 7.20.25 Reputation and First Impressions

When an NPC first meets someone, known reputation may influence:
- Trust;
- Fear;
- Respect;
- suspicion;
- expectations.

These are starting biases, not permanent relationship values.

Direct experience can override reputation.

## 7.20.26 Reputation and Opportunity

Reputation can affect access to:
- jobs;
- missions;
- mentorship;
- leadership;
- merchants;
- political influence;
- criminal contacts;
- romantic opportunities.

Other systems own the final institutional or economic outcome.

Ruleset 7 supplies the social perception.

## 7.20.27 Reputation and Social Resistance

A trusted reputation may reduce initial resistance.

A reputation for manipulation may increase skepticism.

A reputation for danger may increase Fear.

Reputation should modify context, not substitute for social skill.

## 7.20.28 Reputation and Intimidation

Fearsome reputation may make threats more credible.

But it can also cause:
- preemptive avoidance;
- authorities to react;
- stronger security;
- people refusing contact.

Reputation creates both benefits and costs.

## 7.20.29 Reputation and Leadership

A respected leader may receive faster initial cooperation.

But reputation cannot sustain leadership if direct experience repeatedly contradicts it.

## 7.20.30 Reputation and Romance

Romantic reputation may influence expectations.

Examples:
- known flirt;
- perceived commitment-phobic;
- publicly devoted partner;
- rumored scandal.

These should affect expectation and caution rather than mechanically determining attraction.

## 7.20.31 Reputation and Crime

A criminal reputation may include:
- suspected criminal;
- confirmed offender;
- wanted shinobi;
- feared underworld figure.

Being suspected is different from being publicly proven guilty.

## 7.20.32 Wanted Status

Formal wanted status is an institutional/legal state rather than purely reputation.

Ruleset 7 may track:
- who knows;
- how recognized;
- associated fear or notoriety.

A future law/institution ruleset should own legal consequences.

## 7.20.33 Propaganda

Organizations may deliberately shape reputation through:
- selective stories;
- heroic framing;
- demonization;
- suppression;
- false claims;
- staged events.

Reputation changes only as those messages reach and persuade audiences.

## 7.20.34 Counter-Reputation

A character may attempt to repair or challenge reputation through:
- public evidence;
- testimony;
- consistent behavior;
- formal exoneration;
- visible restitution;
- trusted advocates.

Reputation repair is usually slower when the negative belief is deeply entrenched.

## 7.20.35 Reputation Repair

Repair depends on:
- evidence;
- source trust;
- severity;
- repetition;
- passage of time;
- visible changed behavior.

One speech should rarely erase a major established reputation.

## 7.20.36 Reputation Can Remain Unfair

A reputation does not have to become accurate just because the truth exists.

False reputations may persist when:
- evidence is inaccessible;
- institutions benefit from the narrative;
- prejudice reinforces it;
- rumors spread faster than corrections;
- witnesses are disbelieved.

This should emerge from information systems rather than narrative fiat.

## 7.20.37 Contradictory Reputations

The same person may simultaneously be known as:
- hero;
- murderer;
- loyal shinobi;
- traitor;
- compassionate medic;
- ruthless interrogator;

to different audiences.

Contradiction is expected.

## 7.20.38 Reputation Decay

Reputation may fade when:
- time passes;
- the person becomes inactive;
- new figures replace them;
- no reinforcing stories appear.

Decay depends on:
- fame;
- severity;
- cultural memory;
- institutional records.

Legendary events may persist for generations.

## 7.20.39 Reputation After Death

A dead character may continue accumulating reputation through:
- memorialization;
- historical reinterpretation;
- propaganda;
- exposed secrets;
- descendants.

Reputation is therefore not limited to living characters.

## 7.20.40 Historical Reputation

Long-term world simulation may track:
- official history;
- folk memory;
- clan interpretation;
- scholarly interpretation.

These may disagree.

## 7.20.41 Reputation by Simulation Tier

### Major NPC
Track multiple relevant audience-specific reputations.

### Supporting NPC
Track important local or professional reputation.

### Persistent Minor NPC
Use broad local reputation where relevant.

### Background NPC
Generate reputation only when context requires it.

## 7.20.42 Reputation Ledger

For important figures, the engine may maintain:

**Audience:** Kusagakure civilians
- Fame: High
- Combat reputation: Strong
- Reliability reputation: High
- Danger reputation: Moderate

**Audience:** Village Intelligence
- Fame: Moderate
- Reliability reputation: Uncertain
- Security concern: High

**Audience:** Criminal network
- Recognition: High
- Danger reputation: Very High
- Trustworthiness: Mixed

The exact implementation may remain hidden.

## 7.20.43 Reputation Event Record

Important changes may store:
- event;
- audience reached;
- claim;
- source;
- credibility;
- direction;
- magnitude;
- resulting reputation shift.

This supports later rumor tracing.

## 7.20.44 Player-Facing Reputation

Players should normally learn reputation through:
- NPC reactions;
- titles;
- overheard discussion;
- formal notices;
- treatment by institutions;
- explicit statements.

Exact hidden reputation values should not normally be displayed.

## 7.20.45 Design Standard

A reputation mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- uses one universal reputation score;
- assumes everyone has heard the same information;
- treats fame as approval;
- treats notoriety as universal hatred;
- makes secret actions automatically public;
- merges aliases without discovery;
- makes reputation always accurate;
- makes one rumor instantly become established fact;
- prevents direct experience from overriding reputation;
- requires detailed reputation tracking for irrelevant background NPCs.

## Governing Rule

**Reputation should represent what particular audiences have come to believe about someone through actual information channels, with fame determining how widely the person is known and direct experience remaining capable of reinforcing or overturning public perception.**
