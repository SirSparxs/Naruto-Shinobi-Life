# 7.22 — Social Networks

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.22 defines how individual relationships combine into larger social structures.

The governing principle is:

**NPCs exist inside networks of relationships, and those networks shape access, information, opportunity, influence, protection, conflict, and social consequence.**

## 7.22.1 Social Network Defined

A social network is the set of people and groups connected through persistent relationships.

Connections may come from:
- family;
- clan;
- friendship;
- romance;
- mentorship;
- team membership;
- workplace;
- neighborhood;
- criminal association;
- political alliance;
- shared institution.

The network is not necessarily formal.

## 7.22.2 Nodes and Ties

Conceptually:

- **Node:** a person or relevant social group.
- **Tie:** a meaningful social connection.

A tie may include:
- familiarity;
- trust;
- affection;
- loyalty;
- obligation;
- hostility;
- professional relationship.

The network should use existing relationship state rather than duplicate it.

## 7.22.3 Strong Ties

Strong ties usually involve:
- frequent interaction;
- high familiarity;
- emotional investment;
- trust;
- obligation;
- family or close friendship.

Strong ties often provide:
- support;
- reliable communication;
- high social influence;
- rapid information transfer.

## 7.22.4 Weak Ties

Weak ties may include:
- acquaintances;
- former classmates;
- occasional coworkers;
- merchants;
- distant relatives;
- professional contacts.

Weak ties are socially important because they connect otherwise separate networks.

They often provide:
- new information;
- introductions;
- access to unfamiliar groups;
- unexpected opportunities.

## 7.22.5 Bridge Relationships

A bridge tie connects two social clusters that otherwise have little contact.

Examples:
- shinobi with merchant contacts;
- clan member dating outside clan;
- medic connected to criminal informant;
- traveler linking two villages.

Bridge characters can become unusually important for:
- rumor spread;
- diplomacy;
- trade;
- recruitment;
- intelligence.

## 7.22.6 Social Hubs

Some NPCs have many connections.

Examples:
- clan elders;
- popular merchants;
- instructors;
- team leaders;
- innkeepers;
- hospital staff;
- political figures.

Hubs may:
- spread information faster;
- provide introductions;
- amplify reputation.

Being a hub does not automatically mean being trusted.

## 7.22.7 Network Density

Dense networks have many overlapping relationships.

Examples:
- small clan;
- close squad;
- tight neighborhood.

Dense networks often produce:
- fast information spread;
- strong social pressure;
- reduced privacy;
- easier verification.

## 7.22.8 Sparse Networks

Sparse networks contain fewer connections.

They may produce:
- slower information spread;
- greater privacy;
- weaker social support;
- harder verification;
- greater dependence on bridge contacts.

## 7.22.9 Social Clusters

People naturally form clusters around:
- family;
- clan;
- team;
- workplace;
- school;
- neighborhood;
- shared interests;
- ideology.

Clusters may overlap.

An NPC may belong to several.

## 7.22.10 Overlapping Networks

A character may simultaneously belong to:
- clan network;
- shinobi team;
- hospital staff;
- romantic partner's family;
- merchant circle.

Overlapping networks create:
- information crossover;
- competing loyalties;
- conflict of interest;
- social opportunity.

## 7.22.11 Network Membership Is Not Uniform

Belonging to a group does not mean equal connection to every member.

A clan may contain:
- close family;
- distant relatives;
- rivals;
- respected elders;
- near strangers.

Group membership creates possible connection, not automatic intimacy.

## 7.22.12 Network Access

A character may gain access through:
- membership;
- introduction;
- reputation;
- role;
- invitation;
- relationship.

Access may allow:
- information;
- jobs;
- meetings;
- resources;
- protection;
- social opportunities.

Other systems own final economic or institutional consequences.

## 7.22.13 Introductions

Trusted introductions can reduce social barriers.

Example:
A respected medic introduces Rex to a hospital administrator.

This may improve:
- credibility;
- willingness to meet;
- initial Trust expectations.

The introducer's reputation and relationship with both parties matter.

## 7.22.14 Mutual Contacts

Shared contacts may influence:
- trust;
- caution;
- conversation topics;
- willingness to cooperate.

A mutual friend is evidence of connection, not proof of character.

## 7.22.15 Social Proof Through Networks

NPCs may infer:
- "If people I trust associate with them, they may be safe."

This effect depends on:
- trust in mutual contacts;
- network norms;
- context.

It should never become automatic validation.

## 7.22.16 Gatekeepers

Some network members control access.

Examples:
- clan elder;
- receptionist;
- team leader;
- gang lieutenant;
- political aide.

Gatekeepers may:
- introduce;
- block;
- delay;
- filter.

Social skill may help, but authority and policy still matter.

## 7.22.17 Brokers

A broker deliberately connects people or groups.

Possible roles:
- fixer;
- recruiter;
- diplomat;
- merchant;
- intelligence handler.

Brokers may gain:
- influence;
- information;
- obligations.

## 7.22.18 Network Centrality

A character's importance to a network may depend on:
- number of ties;
- quality of ties;
- bridge position;
- authority;
- unique access.

Centrality is contextual.

Someone may be central in one network and irrelevant in another.

## 7.22.19 Isolation

An isolated NPC has few meaningful connections.

Isolation may reduce:
- information access;
- protection;
- reputation reach;
- social support.

It may increase:
- secrecy;
- dependence on rare relationships;
- vulnerability to manipulation.

Isolation should not automatically imply loneliness.

## 7.22.20 Social Support

Networks may provide:
- emotional support;
- practical help;
- shelter;
- protection;
- information;
- advocacy.

Support depends on actual relationship strength.

## 7.22.21 Network Pressure

Networks can pressure members through:
- norms;
- reputation;
- loyalty expectations;
- family expectations;
- fear of exclusion.

This can influence choices without direct command.

## 7.22.22 Ostracism

A network may socially exclude someone.

Possible consequences:
- reduced access;
- fewer information channels;
- reputation damage;
- loss of support.

Formal expulsion belongs to the relevant institution system.

## 7.22.23 Cliques and Factions

Informal subgroups may form within larger organizations.

Examples:
- academy friend group;
- hospital clique;
- jonin political bloc;
- clan youth faction.

These may produce:
- internal alliances;
- rivalry;
- information silos.

## 7.22.24 Rival Networks

Two networks may:
- compete;
- distrust;
- feud;
- cooperate temporarily.

Individual bridge relationships may soften or intensify these dynamics.

## 7.22.25 Family Networks

Family ties often create:
- obligations;
- support;
- information flow;
- inheritance expectations;
- conflict.

Family membership does not guarantee affection or loyalty.

## 7.22.26 Clan Networks

Clan networks may add:
- lineage;
- hierarchy;
- shared reputation;
- secrets;
- political expectations.

Detailed clan governance should belong to future institutional systems.

## 7.22.27 Team Networks

Mission teams can create:
- strong trust;
- practical familiarity;
- shared secrets;
- duty-based loyalty.

Team relationships may persist after reassignment.

## 7.22.28 Professional Networks

Professions create contacts through:
- shared work;
- referrals;
- reputation;
- expertise.

Examples:
- medics;
- merchants;
- teachers;
- shinobi;
- craftsmen.

## 7.22.29 Criminal Networks

Criminal networks may rely on:
- secrecy;
- reputation;
- favors;
- fear;
- intermediaries.

Access may require:
- introduction;
- proof;
- obligation;
- reputation.

## 7.22.30 Romantic Network Effects

Romantic relationships may connect:
- friend groups;
- families;
- clans;
- workplaces.

A breakup may therefore affect more than two people.

## 7.22.31 Ex-Partner Networks

Former partners may retain:
- shared friends;
- family ties;
- professional overlap.

Separation does not automatically sever the surrounding network.

## 7.22.32 Network Spillover

Actions toward one person may affect connected others if:
- they hear about it;
- they care;
- the relationship is relevant.

Examples:
- harming a sibling affects family network;
- betraying teammate affects squad;
- insulting clan elder affects loyal members.

## 7.22.33 Network Reputation

A character may be known through a network before direct contact.

Example:
- "Karn's cousin."
- "Rena's teammate."
- "Student of Kaito."

Association may create initial expectations.

## 7.22.34 Guilt by Association

NPCs may unfairly judge someone because of connections.

This is a belief process, not objective guilt.

## 7.22.35 Prestige by Association

Association with respected people may improve credibility.

Again, it affects first impression, not truth.

## 7.22.36 Network Conflict of Interest

Overlapping ties may create conflict.

Example:
An NPC is:
- clan member;
- village officer;
- sibling of suspect.

Different networks may demand different behavior.

## 7.22.37 Social Capital

Social capital represents practical benefit available through relationships.

Examples:
- introductions;
- favors;
- information;
- advocacy;
- access.

It should not become one universal spendable currency.

## 7.22.38 Calling on Contacts

A character may ask a contact for:
- information;
- help;
- introduction;
- protection.

Success depends on:
- relationship;
- cost;
- obligation;
- risk;
- contact capability.

## 7.22.39 Contact Reliability

Contacts vary in:
- competence;
- loyalty;
- information quality;
- discretion.

A large network is not automatically a good network.

## 7.22.40 Network Maintenance

Some ties require periodic contact.

Others persist despite long absence.

Maintenance expectations depend on:
- relationship type;
- personality;
- culture;
- history.

## 7.22.41 Network Growth

Networks expand through:
- work;
- travel;
- school;
- missions;
- romance;
- introductions;
- reputation.

Growth should emerge from actual social activity.

## 7.22.42 Network Decay

Connections may weaken through:
- distance;
- conflict;
- inactivity;
- changed role.

Weak ties often fade faster than strong ties.

## 7.22.43 Reconnection

Old ties may be reactivated.

Prior history affects:
- familiarity;
- trust;
- expectations.

Reconnection may be easier than forming a new tie, but not always.

## 7.22.44 Network Visibility

The player should not automatically know another NPC's complete network.

They may know:
- obvious family;
- teammates;
- public allies.

Hidden contacts may require investigation.

## 7.22.45 Hidden Networks

Secret societies, covert teams, criminal cells, and intelligence networks may conceal ties.

Ruleset 7.6 governs who knows the connection exists.

## 7.22.46 Network Inference

Characters may infer hidden relationships from:
- repeated contact;
- shared behavior;
- information leaks;
- mutual protection.

Inference may be wrong.

## 7.22.47 Network Mapping

For important investigations, the engine may maintain a compact graph.

Example:
- Rex -> Rena: sibling
- Rex -> Karn: teammate
- Karn -> Dagar: suspicious contact
- Dagar -> missing-nin: covert tie

This allows emergent investigation.

## 7.22.48 Off-Screen Network Effects

Networks continue functioning off-screen.

NPCs may:
- introduce each other;
- exchange information;
- argue;
- cooperate;
- form new ties.

Only consequential changes require explicit simulation.

## 7.22.49 Compressed Network Simulation

Large networks should use summaries.

Example:
- "Hospital staff broadly know Rena and trust her professionally."

Do not create individual relationship objects for every employee unless needed.

## 7.22.50 Tier-Based Network Detail

### Major NPC
Track important strong ties, weak bridges, hubs, and hidden contacts.

### Supporting NPC
Track key family, friends, work contacts.

### Persistent Minor NPC
Track one or two relevant connections.

### Background NPC
Use group membership and immediate contacts.

## 7.22.51 Network Event Record

Important network changes may store:
- tie created;
- tie broken;
- introduction;
- bridge formed;
- exclusion;
- network access gained or lost.

## 7.22.52 Design Standard

A social-network mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- treats every group member as equally connected;
- assumes friendship with one person grants access to all friends;
- ignores weak ties;
- makes social networks fully visible;
- creates information spread without connections;
- turns social capital into universal currency;
- requires full graph simulation for every background NPC;
- ignores bridge characters;
- assumes family or clan membership guarantees loyalty.

## Governing Rule

**Social networks should emerge from real relationships and group memberships, shaping who can reach whom, who hears what, who can provide access or support, and how consequences spread beyond one-to-one interactions.**
