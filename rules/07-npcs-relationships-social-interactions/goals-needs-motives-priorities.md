# 7.4 — Goals, Needs, Motives & Priorities

Status: Provisional design section pending explicit approval.

Ruleset 7.4 defines what NPCs want, why they want it, how strongly they care, and how competing priorities shape decisions.

This section should answer:

**What is this person trying to accomplish right now, what deeper need is driving it, and what are they willing to sacrifice for it?**

Goals should be dynamic, contextual, and causally connected to identity, relationships, knowledge, obligations, and current circumstances.

## 7.4.1 Goals Are Not Personality

Goals describe desired outcomes.

Examples:
- pass the chunin exams;
- protect a sibling;
- expose corruption;
- become clan head;
- earn enough money to move;
- repair a friendship;
- seduce an adult romantic interest;
- discover who betrayed the village;
- survive a dangerous mission;
- keep a secret hidden.

Personality influences how an NPC pursues a goal, but personality and goals must remain separate.

A cautious and a reckless NPC may both want the same outcome while pursuing it very differently.

## 7.4.2 Goal Time Horizons

NPC goals should generally fall into several time horizons.

### Immediate Goals
Minutes to hours.

Examples:
- escape danger;
- calm an argument;
- hide evidence;
- impress someone during a conversation;
- finish a negotiation;
- avoid being noticed.

### Short-Term Goals
Days to weeks.

Examples:
- complete a mission;
- pay off a debt;
- learn a technique;
- repair damaged trust;
- investigate a rumor;
- arrange a date;
- secure temporary housing.

### Medium-Term Goals
Weeks to months.

Examples:
- earn promotion;
- rebuild a team;
- develop a romantic relationship;
- gain political influence;
- improve clan standing;
- save money for a major purchase.

### Long-Term Goals
Months to years.

Examples:
- become jōnin;
- become clan head;
- retire safely;
- restore family honor;
- raise children;
- reshape village policy;
- build a lasting partnership;
- avenge a major loss.

Different time horizons can compete.

## 7.4.3 Needs vs Goals

A need is an underlying pressure or condition.
A goal is one strategy for satisfying that need.

Examples:

Need:
- safety.

Possible goals:
- flee the village;
- become stronger;
- join a powerful faction;
- reconcile with an enemy.

Need:
- belonging.

Possible goals:
- repair a friendship;
- join a team;
- pursue romance;
- gain clan acceptance.

Need:
- status.

Possible goals:
- earn promotion;
- defeat a rival;
- gain wealth;
- become famous.

This distinction prevents the simulation from treating one failed goal as the end of the underlying motive.

An NPC whose goal is blocked may adopt a new strategy to satisfy the same need.

## 7.4.4 Core Motive Categories

Motives may arise from:
- survival;
- safety;
- belonging;
- affection;
- family;
- loyalty;
- status;
- achievement;
- autonomy;
- wealth;
- comfort;
- revenge;
- justice;
- curiosity;
- knowledge;
- duty;
- ideology;
- faith;
- recognition;
- legacy;
- romance;
- sexual desire between adults;
- fear;
- guilt;
- shame;
- pride;
- obligation.

These categories are not exhaustive.

Motives should remain character-specific.

## 7.4.5 Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Motivation

### Intrinsic
The NPC wants the outcome for its own sake.

Examples:
- enjoys research;
- genuinely loves someone;
- values justice;
- wants to become stronger.

### Extrinsic
The NPC wants the outcome because of an external reward, threat, pressure, or consequence.

Examples:
- money;
- promotion;
- punishment avoidance;
- clan approval;
- blackmail;
- reputation;
- mission orders.

Most important decisions may contain both.

## 7.4.6 Stated Motive vs Actual Motive

NPCs may explain their actions inaccurately.

Possible reasons:
- deception;
- self-deception;
- embarrassment;
- privacy;
- political caution;
- social expectations;
- misunderstanding their own feelings.

The simulation should distinguish:

**Stated motive**
What the NPC says they want.

**Conscious motive**
What the NPC actually believes they want.

**Underlying motive**
A deeper need or emotional driver that may be only partly conscious.

Example:
An NPC says:
- "I only want to beat him because he annoys me."

Conscious motive:
- prove superiority.

Underlying motive:
- fear of being seen as inferior.

## 7.4.7 Hidden Motives

Hidden motives should not exist solely to create twists.

They should have:
- a causal origin;
- behavior consistent with them;
- plausible information boundaries;
- meaningful consequences if discovered.

A hidden motive may involve:
- secret loyalty;
- forbidden romance;
- revenge;
- espionage;
- financial desperation;
- political ambition;
- family protection;
- concealed jealousy;
- fear of exposure.

## 7.4.8 Goal Priority

Each active goal should have an approximate priority.

Priority may be influenced by:
- importance;
- urgency;
- emotional intensity;
- consequences of failure;
- obligation;
- opportunity;
- feasibility;
- social pressure;
- risk;
- proximity to completion.

Priority should not require a universal numeric scale if a simpler ordering is sufficient.

Useful qualitative levels may include:
- dominant;
- high;
- moderate;
- low;
- dormant.

## 7.4.9 Dynamic Priority

Priority changes over time.

Examples:
- a long-term promotion goal becomes irrelevant during a hostage crisis;
- romance becomes less important during a war;
- a previously minor grudge becomes dominant after public humiliation;
- financial concerns become urgent after losing a job;
- a hidden secret becomes critical when someone begins investigating it.

This prevents NPCs from behaving as though long-term goals remain equally important in every scene.

## 7.4.10 Goal Conflict

NPCs often want incompatible things.

Examples:
- protect family vs obey village orders;
- pursue romance vs preserve clan expectations;
- tell the truth vs protect a friend;
- gain promotion vs avoid dangerous missions;
- seek revenge vs remain loyal to the law;
- maintain privacy vs ask for help.

When goals conflict, the decision should consider:
- relative priority;
- values;
- current emotion;
- relationship strength;
- expected consequences;
- risk;
- perceived alternatives;
- time pressure.

## 7.4.11 Sacrifice Threshold

A useful concept for each important goal is:

**What is this person willing to sacrifice for it?**

Possible costs include:
- money;
- time;
- comfort;
- reputation;
- safety;
- friendship;
- career;
- clan standing;
- romantic relationship;
- freedom;
- health;
- life.

The stronger the goal, the greater the cost an NPC may accept.

But willingness to sacrifice should still respect identity and moral boundaries.

## 7.4.12 Goal Feasibility

NPCs should evaluate goals through perceived feasibility.

A goal may be:
- easy;
- achievable;
- difficult;
- improbable;
- currently impossible.

Perceived feasibility can differ from actual feasibility.

An NPC may:
- underestimate themselves;
- overestimate themselves;
- misunderstand the situation;
- believe false information.

Low feasibility may cause:
- abandonment;
- delay;
- alternative strategy;
- desperation;
- seeking help.

## 7.4.13 Goal Plans

Important NPCs may maintain plans.

A plan includes:
- target outcome;
- current strategy;
- next action;
- resources needed;
- obstacles;
- dependencies;
- fallback options.

Plans should not require exhaustive simulation for every NPC.

Major NPCs may maintain detailed plans.
Supporting NPCs may use concise next-step logic.

## 7.4.14 Goal Revision

NPCs should revise goals after:
- new information;
- failure;
- success;
- betrayal;
- injury;
- promotion;
- relationship change;
- loss;
- political change;
- financial change;
- new opportunity.

Revision may involve:
- increasing priority;
- decreasing priority;
- abandoning the goal;
- replacing the strategy;
- changing the target;
- creating a new subgoal.

## 7.4.15 Success Does Not End Motivation Automatically

Achieving a goal may:
- satisfy the motive;
- reveal a larger goal;
- create a new problem;
- increase ambition;
- reduce pressure.

Example:
An NPC becomes chunin.

Possible next outcomes:
- satisfaction and reduced ambition;
- desire to become jōnin;
- disappointment that promotion did not provide respect;
- increased responsibility creating stress.

## 7.4.16 Failure Does Not Always End a Goal

Failure may create:
- renewed effort;
- alternative strategy;
- lowered expectations;
- obsession;
- resignation;
- resentment;
- request for help;
- deception;
- revenge.

Response should depend on personality, values, temperament, and stakes.

## 7.4.17 Desperation

Desperation occurs when:
- need intensity is high;
- stakes are severe;
- alternatives are limited;
- time is short.

Desperation can change:
- risk tolerance;
- moral flexibility;
- willingness to seek help;
- willingness to lie;
- willingness to bargain;
- willingness to betray;
- willingness to accept humiliating terms.

Desperation should not erase identity entirely.

It applies pressure to existing boundaries.

## 7.4.18 Obligations as Motives

NPCs may pursue goals because they believe they owe something.

Sources include:
- family;
- clan;
- village;
- rank;
- promises;
- debts;
- mentorship;
- friendship;
- romance;
- honor.

Obligation should be distinct from desire.

An NPC may strongly dislike an action while still believing they must do it.

## 7.4.19 Relationship-Driven Goals

Relationships often create goals.

Examples:
- protect someone;
- impress someone;
- reconcile;
- avoid someone;
- gain approval;
- avenge someone;
- confess feelings;
- end a relationship;
- maintain secrecy;
- support a partner;
- compete with a rival.

The goal should reference the actual relationship state rather than replacing it.

## 7.4.20 Romantic & Adult Sexual Motives

For adults, romantic and sexual motives may be independent.

An NPC may want:
- emotional closeness without sex;
- sex without romance;
- romance with slow physical progression;
- long-term commitment;
- casual intimacy;
- exclusivity;
- experimentation;
- reconciliation with an ex;
- distance after unwanted attention.

These motives should interact with:
- attraction;
- arousal;
- affection;
- trust;
- compatibility;
- boundaries;
- consent;
- relationship expectations.

Sexual desire should not override refusal, incompatibility, or other priorities.

## 7.4.21 Fear-Driven Motives

Fear can create goals such as:
- escape;
- conceal;
- appease;
- surrender;
- seek protection;
- remove a threat;
- avoid exposure.

Fear-driven goals may temporarily outrank other goals.

But fear should not automatically imply obedience.

## 7.4.22 Revenge & Grievance Motives

Revenge may have:
- target;
- trigger;
- perceived justification;
- intensity;
- patience;
- acceptable cost;
- desired outcome.

Not every grievance produces revenge.

Possible responses include:
- avoidance;
- reporting;
- social retaliation;
- competition;
- sabotage;
- forgiveness;
- direct violence.

## 7.4.23 Duty & Ideology

Some NPCs may act primarily from:
- professional duty;
- clan doctrine;
- nationalism;
- religion;
- political ideology;
- personal code.

These motives can remain strong even when emotionally unpleasant.

An NPC may risk their life without wanting to die because duty outranks safety.

## 7.4.24 Opportunity

A dormant motive may become active when opportunity appears.

Examples:
- attraction becomes pursuit after learning the other person is single;
- ambition activates after a leadership vacancy;
- revenge becomes actionable after discovering the target's location;
- theft becomes tempting when security fails.

Opportunity does not create the motive.
It makes pursuit feasible.

## 7.4.25 Dormant Goals

Not every motive needs active processing.

Dormant goals remain stored but do not consume frequent simulation.

Examples:
- eventually retire;
- someday have children;
- someday leave the village;
- eventually confront estranged parent.

They become active when triggered by context.

## 7.4.26 Goal Dependencies

Some goals require others first.

Example:
Long-term goal:
- become clan head.

Subgoals:
- earn rank;
- improve reputation;
- secure allies;
- discredit rival;
- gain elder support.

Dependencies create coherent multi-step behavior.

## 7.4.27 NPCs Can Abandon Goals

NPCs should not pursue every goal forever.

Abandonment may occur because:
- cost becomes too high;
- feasibility collapses;
- values change;
- motive disappears;
- relationship changes;
- new goal outranks it;
- success elsewhere satisfies the need.

Abandonment should itself be meaningful when the goal mattered deeply.

## 7.4.28 Self-Deception

Major NPCs may misunderstand their own motives.

Examples:
- claims rivalry is about competition when it is partly jealousy;
- claims romance is casual when emotionally attached;
- claims loyalty to clan when actually afraid of rejection;
- claims revenge is justice when driven by humiliation.

Self-deception should emerge from character psychology rather than arbitrary hidden-state twists.

## 7.4.29 Motive Discovery

NPCs may gradually understand themselves better through:
- reflection;
- conversation;
- failure;
- therapy/counseling where culturally appropriate;
- mentorship;
- relationship conflict;
- major life events.

This may change how goals are framed.

## 7.4.30 Goal Tracking by Simulation Tier

### Major NPC
Track:
- several active goals across time horizons;
- major needs;
- goal priorities;
- conflicts;
- sacrifices;
- plans;
- hidden motives;
- dormant long-term goals.

### Supporting NPC
Track:
- 1–3 active goals;
- important motive;
- next likely action;
- one major conflict if relevant.

### Persistent Minor NPC
Track:
- one current concern or goal when useful.

### Background NPC
Generate only immediate intent required by the scene.

## 7.4.31 Off-Screen Goal Progression

Major and relevant Supporting NPCs may pursue goals off-screen.

Progress should consider:
- time;
- skill;
- resources;
- opposition;
- opportunity;
- information;
- relationships;
- world events.

Off-screen success should not be guaranteed.

If uncertainty matters, Ruleset 2 may resolve it.

## 7.4.32 Player Interference

The player may:
- help;
- obstruct;
- redirect;
- expose;
- accelerate;
- delay;
- unknowingly affect an NPC goal.

NPCs should react to interference according to:
- awareness;
- motive importance;
- relationship;
- perceived intent;
- actual consequences.

## 7.4.33 Conflicting NPC Goals

NPCs may oppose one another because their goals conflict even when they do not personally dislike each other.

Examples:
- two candidates want the same promotion;
- two clans want the same resource;
- friends support different policies;
- romantic partners want incompatible futures.

Conflict does not require hostility.

## 7.4.34 Goal-Driven Social Behavior

Social interactions should often emerge from goals.

An NPC may:
- befriend someone for genuine affection;
- befriend someone strategically;
- flirt because attracted;
- flirt to gain influence;
- deceive to protect family;
- negotiate for resources;
- intimidate to deter danger;
- apologize to repair trust.

The same behavior can arise from different motives.

Ruleset 7 should track the motive separately from the visible action.

## 7.4.35 Design Standard

A goal/motive mechanic should be rejected or revised if it:
- treats every NPC as pursuing only one goal;
- confuses personality with goals;
- makes goals static;
- assumes stated motives are always true;
- generates secret motives only for surprise twists;
- ignores feasibility;
- ignores urgency;
- prevents NPCs from changing strategy;
- makes failure automatically erase motivation;
- makes romantic or sexual desire override consent or boundaries;
- requires detailed long-term planning for every background NPC;
- makes NPCs pursue goals they have no information or opportunity to pursue;
- makes all goal conflicts produce hostility.

## Governing Rule

**NPC behavior should emerge from the interaction between what they want, why they want it, how urgently they want it, what they believe is possible, and what costs they are willing to accept.**
